## Team and leadership (T), continued

- T6 [managing employees who get leads, sell or deliver: standards, feedback, check-ins, culture, team structure and how work is reported] [stage: S3+] Hiring, recruiting and pay are T1. Managing beats doing: replace the owner in each role as soon as possible, then hand the managing to a manager. Weak staff are the fault of whoever hired and trained them: train with document, demonstrate, duplicate (D5), give new starters a reduced workload until quality measures are met, tell them how corrections will come (D13), and if a trained person is still below standard after about three weeks, act through the fair process (see X18). Before the first hire, write how the business behaves with clients (culture is the internal brand), capped at three values, each with concrete behaviours and what gets praised and corrected, including "invite disagreement, keep it about the work, then commit once it's decided"; the owner models it first and every manager reinforces the same things. Choose leaders on written criteria (consistent results, every C5 step certified, mentoring shown, never tenure alone), train them, let them manage one starter first, and have no permanent player-manager roles; managers still take some client calls each month. Run a daily, weekly and one-to-one rhythm with a short daily role-play, give lead-quality feedback one to one as data, and mystery-shop anyone who sells against a checklist of each C5 step. Standards: the weakest performer kept in a function is the standard signed off for it, so judge on output after training and use the fair capability process rather than leave a weak performer; a recurring problem under the same person is a who problem; at each review, judge each person against what the owner would do in their role (the initiative they aren't taking, the questions they aren't asking; the strongest ask questions the owner hadn't thought of), and treat an owner who rates everyone as excellent as not looking closely enough; in each new starter's first week, ask them what their manager said the job involves and which standards are optional, since a manager who quietly waives the owner's standards undoes all training; enforce a stated standard (on time, leads followed up, the site left clean) from the first breach through the written, lawful disciplinary process, never by instant dismissal (X18); whoever holds the highest standard for a function decides it; each quarter, ask whether team quality has risen. Before each headcount jump, list which checklists, reports and decision rights change (D5) and expect a dip; when politics or bottlenecks appear, first write down who decides each function and check whether pay rewards a count over the outcome (M38). Each role has a one-page decision list (decide alone, decide and tell, bring to the owner). Staff get least-access views and no client data (X5); monthly numbers are shared with staff, never with variable-fee suppliers (T2). Keep politics and needless meetings away from top performers; deep-work roles get a short daily huddle only. Never script forced cheerfulness; after a hard push, tell people to rest. Managers own their slips (L75). Every exit names one specific thing the person did well; if a role ends through the owner's mistake, say so and leave the door open. New hires get a short check-in at the start and end of each day for their first two weeks and shadow other functions. Write each role's path as behaviours: people move up by already doing the next role's work, and having no next step is a common reason good people leave. Know where the problems are: the owner keeps a standing, one-level-deeper view of each area's current biggest problem and its largest recurring costs (by reading the numbers and asking about the work, not only "how is it going?"), and company-wide pay changes, and any recurring outside cost above the F2 spend limit, need the owner's written sign-off; delegating a function (compliance, HR, finance) still means checking what it costs and what it produces each month. Getting clients is one job: put advertising and sales under one owner (the founder until a head of revenue is hired), since sales only fills the gaps the advertising left, so the two are judged on clients won, never against each other. A growing business needs three leaders: one for getting clients, one for delivering the result and one for running the operation (finance, legal, HR, systems), the last serving the other two rather than setting direction. Once the business has several product lines, run each line as its own unit with its own product and acquisition leads and its own profit, all served by one shared core (finance, legal, HR, IT); launch a new line only once a leader as capable as the owner will run it, not by the owner managing it alongside the core business. For these unit leaders, prefer people who like driving growth and new initiatives but want a salaried role over running their own business; when a candidate has run their own business, ask why they now want a salaried role and whether they plan to start again, and keep client and data protections in the written terms lawful in the project's country (X). Any team or role created to fix a metric gets that metric and a review date in writing at the start; if the metric hasn't moved by the date, stop the work and handle any role changes lawfully (X18). When someone isn't doing what was asked, check five reasons in order and fix the reason, not the person: they didn't know it was wanted (say it in writing), didn't know how (train, D5), didn't know by when (set a date), didn't want to (reward, praise, autonomy, training in their skills), or something blocks them (remove it). Run the check as five questions, the same with a team member, a supplier or the owner's own stalled work: (1) did they know it was wanted: if it isn't written, treat it as never asked, so put each request in a message or task with the date it was given; (2) did they know what was wanted, defined in behaviours or outcomes (the format, where it goes, what done looks like; a label such as "be more professional" names no behaviour, so say what to do instead next time), since two or three extra minutes of clarity save hours; (3) did they know how: break the task into its steps and find the one step they can't do, then document, demonstrate and have them duplicate it (D5); everything can be trained but not everything is worth training, so set real prerequisites for a role rather than training basic skills it assumes; (4) did they know by when: ask how long the work itself takes and what else is on their plate, then agree the order, which also shows the team's real workload; make end of day the default deadline for small asks rather than end of week; (5) did something block them: ask what they did to remove it and whether it was reasonable for it to block them that long, since this shows whether they solve problems on their own; if the block was real, reorder the work. Ask the first four before the fifth, since people blame circumstances first and the earlier questions keep the conversation off the person. Only then look at motivation, which no one admits to lacking when asked directly: raise the value of the outcome to them by linking the task to what they said they want, and reframe a struggle such as boredom with repetition as a skill they are building on the way to that goal. Open the conversation by naming the gap against what the owner believes they are capable of and asking which of the five it is, so both work on the problem from the same side. Someone who still won't do the work after all five, including a relative, is not kept on because no one else would hire them; act through a fair, lawful process (D16, X18). A standard is enforced only once the person knew it, could meet it and chose not to; then respond every time, straight away and in proportion, stepping up for a repeat through the written disciplinary process (a conversation, then a written warning), since a consequence that happens only sometimes teaches people to gamble on it; a repeated pattern ends in a lawful exit even for a high performer, out of fairness to everyone who keeps the standard (X18). Treat any theft found as likely not the first time: investigate, follow the gross misconduct process and end employment if it is upheld, seek to recover the loss and report it where appropriate (X18), then tighten the controls that let it happen (reconciliations, two-person checks). The owner applies the same five questions to their own stalled work, and turns statements such as "no one can sell like me" into questions such as "how do I find or train someone to sell like me?", which can be answered. Define what good looks like for every role and task in observable, measurable terms ("reply to client messages within two hours", never "stop being lazy") before giving feedback, coaching or judging; what can't be seen or measured can't be coached. Authority must match responsibility: whoever answers for a number decides the people and actions that drive it, and a manager blocked from those decisions raises it with the person above, in writing. When two team members can't work together, meet them together once: each says calmly how it felt, each names something good about the other and what could be true in the other's view, each says back what they heard and commits to one change; repeated conflict is then a performance issue. When a good performer's results drop for a personal reason, give a stated period of support and say so plainly, then judge whether it is a short setback or a new pattern; if it continues and the team suffers, have the direct conversation, with UK employment rules and any health or disability duties checked first (X18). To be trusted as a leader, never use what someone disclosed against them, now or later, thank them for telling you, and keep every promise, since one broken word can end trust built over years; sharing something real with the team invites them to earn more trust. Bringing in a senior leader: the manager owns the first two to four weeks and writes an onboarding plan before day one; tell the team the plain truth about why (what the person has done that the business hasn't), say it will be different rather than promising it will be better, and tell each affected person what changes for them and what doesn't. The new leader meets everyone in the first week or two to hear the patterns (what several people praise, what several name as the same problem), learns from the bottom up (every line of the profit and loss, every number in their function), offers help rather than telling people what to do, and ships visible pieces of work early (a memo, a process, a decision) so the team sees results; a strong leader starts adding value within the first week, and one who needs a quarter to get their bearings is a warning sign. People who say "I got it" and deliver every time are the ones to give the most room and support; track each person's say-do ratio. Give feedback as start, stop and keep: what to start doing, what to stop doing, and what to keep doing that will keep being rewarded; who is hired, who is let go, and which behaviour is rewarded or left unchallenged are the culture. Once there are several product lines, run each as a unit with its own profit and loss, charged its share of overheads such as rent and shared staff, and pay its leader a share of that unit's profit (profit share, not equity, T3); put specialists such as developers or marketers who work for a unit under that unit's accountability, not on a separate island. Anyone may propose a new line but must make the case and the resource request in writing; orphan ideas and failing units go to a small group of senior leaders, and a line that proves itself in a test is handed to a leader who championed it, usually from inside, since a champion fights for it and is easier to rein in than a reluctant leader is to motivate. As headcount grows, watch the ratio of managers to people doing the work: a layer of managers each managing one or two people slows the business and adds process; keep managers' spans wide and move people into doing roles. Ask each team member three questions and expect concrete answers: what do you actually do (actions, not labels such as "analyse"), what does the business do to make money, and how does what you do help that (more clients, or clients worth more)? Anyone who can't link their work to that is either in the wrong role or needs the link explained. Running a priority push: (1) name the one priority and say plainly which smaller problems will be left to burn for now, giving each person a line to use with anyone who complains ("the owner said it's fine"); (2) open with a no-blame statement covering everything before today, so questions about causes are for understanding, not fault; (3) clear the priority's owners of other work rather than adding it on top; (4) break it into named steps with an owner and a time in minutes or hours, then set the next check-in by those times (the same day, or twice a day, not weekly); (5) have each person say back their next action and correct it until it matches; (6) show progress where the team sees it, with an agreed shared reward when it is done; (7) regularly ask each person to list everything they are working on, and cancel or table what no longer matters. Share the business's real numbers (revenue, margins, conversion rates) widely enough that people can help solve problems, within any confidentiality the law or contracts require (X8). When a checklist is followed in full and the results are still worse than another person's, the checklist is incomplete: find what the better person does that it misses and add it, rather than blaming the person. Roles with slow feedback (long projects, complex work) need feedback built in: short check-ins, visible milestones and prompt recognition, since behaviour is shaped by what happens after it. Learn what each person responds to (praise, money, autonomy, recognition, time) by asking and watching, and use that rather than one reward for all. The less skilled the person, the more specific the instructions need to be; complaints about communication often mean the wrong person in the seat. Culture is what the owner tolerates: let go, lawfully (X18), those who don't hold the stated values, and judge each later hire against them. How vague an instruction can be depends on the person's skill in that area; for low skill, write it step by step, including how to greet and speak to clients, and weigh the training time against hiring someone with more base skill. When automation removes work, raise the standard for everyone; a role no longer needed ends only through a fair, lawful redundancy process (X18). Give titles that match the real scope (a manager of one area, not a chief officer of a five-person business). When a strong performer promoted into managing struggles, say so early, own the owner's part in promoting them without support, and offer a role that fits their strengths with an experienced leader brought in above or alongside, explained truthfully as an investment in them; any change to role or pay follows their contract and a fair, lawful process (X18). Praise finished work the same day, in the team's work tool, for the exact behaviour; keep a short profile of each person's likes from a voluntary starter form to make thanks personal (any gift within X18 and X19). To get the truth, ask "what would you tell me if you knew I wouldn't be upset?", thank them and act on it. Extend trust inside the team quickly and take it back quickly when it is broken. When someone the business invested in is a better fit elsewhere, help them leave well (a fair, lawful process, X18, and a reference where honest); good exits send back referrals, candidates and later work.Offer two ways to grow: a management track and an expert track with its own pay steps for people who want to get better at the craft rather than manage (ask each person which they want). Give the team one decision rule from the mission (such as "which option is better for the client?") so decisions made without the owner still line up. Share the business's results with the whole team each quarter (what made money, what was spent and didn't work), keeping personal pay, client details and anything confidential out (X8). The owner stays in the detail on the few things that matter most rather than managing only from a distance. Judge an operator or manager by whether the business as a whole does less unnecessary work and the owner has more time: each new rule or form must remove more work elsewhere than it adds locally, and a manager who only adds tasks is making things worse. Each employee writes their own quarterly goals in the F2 form, starting from the company's goals and working down: which of the three results their work serves, the hypothesis, the input, the expected output; review at quarter end on two questions, did they do it and did it happen. It shows each person how their daily work ties to growth. Never let a newly promoted manager without experience set headcount alone: size each team from the workload numbers, since overhiring forces layoffs that damage the business's reputation as an employer for years. When someone still doesn't do the work after the expectation was set in writing, they were trained and the reasons and pay were aligned, the problem is motivation: don't default to moving them into another open role, unless they are the person the business would hire for that role anyway (the struggling, previously strong performer promoted beyond their strengths is the exception noted above). Don't promote the best individual performer to manager by default: test whether they can recruit, train and hold others to a standard first, or keep them as a highly paid individual contributor. A value is non-negotiable and must exclude someone: if no good candidate would fail it, it is a platitude; state each in the words the team already uses, hire, correct and part ways by all three (two out of three is a no), and apply the same values to clients and partners: if the owner wouldn't work with someone for ten years, don't start for a day. Plan communication on four fronts, each with its own calendar: prospects (to become clients), clients (to buy again), staff (the vision, the mission and why the work matters, repeated often) and candidates (the hiring funnel above). Ask anyone remote or less experienced for a short weekly written report (their main win, their main problem, the help they need, progress on their quarterly priorities, and for front-line roles their numbers against target); give experienced leaders fewer, more open questions, and reply to every item in every report, since reports nobody answers quickly turn into box-ticking. A new finance lead spends the first 30 days meeting each leader to learn their goals and how those show in the numbers, and building one dashboard of what leadership needs to see; the next 60 days go on making it accurate and current, and on checking payment controls (D17) are in place. When someone's work misses a standard that was never shown to them, the leader owns the gap: say so plainly, give two or three finished examples of the work done well, and ask for the next ones to match them, so the person can see the difference themselves; the same applies to briefing a contractor or an AI tool. State the vision as one concrete, specific line (a place, a kind of client, a result) that the team can use to test any new opportunity, and name the anti-vision it implies, what the business will not do, so the team can call the owner out when an idea falls outside it; avoid large arbitrary numbers that mean nothing to the owner or the team. When someone starts in a role, ask what tools (systems, software, templates) and support (an assistant, training, a coach, a regular check-in) they need to do it, and provide them before judging results: the right person in the right seat still fails without them. When coaching someone towards a goal (as against correcting a slip, D16): ask first whether they want help on it; tie the advice to the goal they have stated, not the owner's; say plainly it will get harder before it gets easier; give a simple framework and the first next step; then let them say what they will do in the next day or two, what might get in the way and when they will report back, and leave the work with them rather than taking it back. Once there is a team, run decisions and meetings from short written memos (no memo, no meeting), and plan in three layers: a three-year picture, a one-year target and 90-day actions, with each person holding two or three priorities per 90 days.
