## Money models (M)
### The money model sequence
- M24 [designing or changing the money model, or reviewing results] A money model is a deliberate sequence of offers: what the business offers, when and how, to make the most money as fast as possible. Four offer types: attraction offers turn strangers into clients, upsells get them to spend more, downsells turn a no into a yes, continuity keeps them paying. Use one type, several, or all four in any order. Find every point where a client has a problem and offer the fix at that moment, framing each offer as the solution to a named problem: offered when they realise they need it, there is no limit on how many offers to make. Swap larger costs and hassle later for a smaller, certain fee now. Most of the profit sits in the second, third and fourth offers: a business that sells one thing only has a front end. Where a client will only ever buy once, make that one purchase as large as the value supports. Bad money models fail in a set order: acquisition costs more than first profit, outreach is cut, fewer clients come in, the owner props it up with personal cash or loans, then sells equity or waits years and loses it. Minimum standard: 30-day gross profit from a client exceeds the cost to acquire and serve them (M20). Higher standard: one client's 30-day profit pays to acquire and serve at least two more, which removes cash as the limit on growth. Growth multiplies across three levers: how many clients, how much each pays, and how fast they pay (doubling all three is 8x). Copying another business's model disappoints: design this one, and ask "how do I make this work here?", never "this won't work here". Build lifetime gross profit back to front: design the most valuable offer first, then the steps that lead to it. With several possible front-end offers, advertise only the one with the best lifetime gross profit to acquisition cost ratio and sell the others behind it as back-end offers, adding the take-up rate times each back-end offer's gross profit to the front end's lifetime value; the exception is a market too small to keep feeding it. Compare product lines on that ratio, the number of buyers and the time from yes to delivered result, not on ticket size: a simple, widely wanted line that is fast to deliver and easy to productise often beats a larger custom one. The current sequence and stage are in the instructions box. After the first target, before building a new product line, list what the business already pays for and doesn't fully use: team knowledge, spare capacity and idle hours of space or tools, content and recordings, and enquiries from people who aren't a fit for the core offer. A product made from these leftovers, which adds no new team, building or delivery process, can lift margin cheaply; if it needs its own operation, it is a new line and goes through F22 and F3 instead.
- M25 [deciding what to build next in the money model] Build in stages and perfect one offer at a time: (1) get clients reliably, (2) make them pay for themselves reliably, (3) make them pay for other clients reliably, (4) maximise each client's long-term value, (5) spend as much on outreach and advertising as the model allows. Each stage funds the next, and reliable means both financially and operationally. Run one offer until it is reliable, then until it is automatic, then add the next. Measure in quarters, not weeks. A bootstrapped business that starts with a complete model collapses under it. If clients aren't coming in, fix the attraction offer first. When the model starts working, delivery starts breaking: decide who will build and lead the team before that point. Over years, build a connected set of offers that solve most of the client's related problems. Don't start new businesses to get more offers: make many offers from what the business already sells (sessions per week, tiers, terms, start dates) and fill gaps with affiliate products (M18). Simple scales, fancy fails. Once acquisition works (after the first target), if the ratio falls, fix back to front: build the back end before trying to cut acquisition cost.
- M26 [checking cash, or before any prepaid or free-time offer] Months of cash left is the first number to know: track the runway. Prepaid cash (several periods paid upfront) is ring-fenced for delivering what was paid for and never spent on anything else. Never sell what the business can't deliver: it breaks the law and the reputation (see X9). Don't borrow to fund outreach before the first target (M19). At stages S0 and S1, check the business account and months of runway first thing each business day, with the funnel numbers (L30); move to weekly once runway is steady. Before the first payment arrives, open a separate business account and card, and run every business cost and receipt through it; costs paid personally each month should be zero.
- M27 [any offer, sales call or refund request] Make the offer when they have the problem, never push anyone against their will, and if they don't want it, find someone who does: hard selling is for weak products. If a client asks for a refund, first offer a rollover (a redo, or credit towards something that fits better, M35); if they still want it, refund them without a fight, entitled or not, and put the effort into attracting better clients. If the owner made a mistake, own it the same day and fix it (L75). State the facts: if they aren't compelling, change reality or frame it better, never lie, because a bad reputation can't be cleared like a debt. Check every offer against current advertising law (see X3); "free" is the most tightly regulated word. Ethics test: if the client knew everything the owner knows, it should still want to buy; a yes it wouldn't give with the full picture is deception (see X9). The aim is to help them decide well, which may be no; the only bad outcome is an undecided prospect. Watch for caring more about the close than the person. Receiving-end test: if the owner were the buyer on the other end of this script, page or call, would they feel respected or played? Cut anything that would feel played, even if true. If a prospect mentions a bereavement, illness, a lost key customer or other bad news, say sorry, offer to rebook and make no ask on that call; log only "rebooked at their request" (C4). Busy buyers decide on shortcuts (price, proof, a reason, scarcity, a local name, credentials): use a cue only when the fact behind it is true and relevant, never one that would mislead if they checked (O11, X3). If a prospect rushes to pay "before I talk myself out of it" or straight after a hard question, slow down and answer the doubt they are avoiding; if they say they feel boxed in or pushed, agree, drop the ask and offer a later step (C10). Never push borrowing, and let a buyer that truly can't afford it go. Cap repeated asks (C10). In any dispute, write down the steps, gather the documents, and take advice before acting. Set a written threshold below which a disputed small charge is simply written off; above it, follow those steps. When a client disputes a fee (a missed-action fee, an exit fee, an invoice line), first ask what the business did or failed to do and hear it all, apologise for anything on the business's side, then ask what they think is fair, and write the outcome down. Gifts both ways: cards and handwritten notes are fine; nothing beyond token value given to or accepted from prospects, clients or their staff, and log any (see X16; T2).
- M28 [choosing or testing an attraction offer] Attraction offers turn strangers into clients and bring in cash upfront, ideally covering acquisition and delivery several times over so the cash funds the next client. They work because strangers can't judge the business's value but understand price exactly, so a discount makes anything look like a deal; free, a discount and a token price sit on one continuum (free is a 100% discount). People often come for one thing and buy another: design for that on purpose, and more free things beat one. The five: decoy (M29), win your money back, giveaways, buy X get Y free, and pay less now or pay more later (M30). A sixth, free plus another revenue stream: use only the independent version (the free item stands alone and the paid offer is encouraged), never the paired one, because "free" that needs a purchase breaks X3. Finding one that works can take up to a year. Once clients come in, raise 30-day profit with upsells (M31 onwards). Never a 10 to 20% attraction discount: it doesn't change behaviour. If a splinter is discounted, make it 80 to 100% off a cheap-to-deliver piece; the core offer itself stays at full price (M5, M36), and any "90% off" or "worth X" needs a genuinely charged reference price (see X3).
- M29 [lead magnet, first sales call, or the DIY tier] Decoy offer: advertise a free, stripped-down version (the lead magnet and the DIY tier) and on the call present the core offer beside it as the premium, with more features, the bonuses and the guarantee. Build the decoy with fewer parts, less personalisation and no guarantee; make the contrast as large as is honest. Advertise the dream outcome and benefits, not features; detail belongs on the call. On calls, cover the DIY tier in a sentence or two, then the core offer in full (within C5's pitch length); in written material, a short block for the DIY tier and the detail for the core offer. Present the core offer first where possible; if they ask for the decoy, get permission to show them both. Script: ask whether they are after the free version or the full outcome; if the outcome, go straight to the core offer; if the free version, show it next to the core offer and ask "Which gets you there faster?", then proceed once they pick. Have one plain analogy ready for why the DIY route alone can't get them there safely in time. A low-ticket decoy adds almost nothing to revenue, while free gets far more buyers using it; gate it with one qualifying question (L44). Free brings more leads; a small price brings better show-up: if call show-up is low, test a small paid step credited against the core offer within 30 days. Be visibly keen on the core offer because it is better for them, and close calmly as a formality. Decoy takers are still leads: add one or two zero-cost extras to build goodwill, learn how to deliver the decoy well, and upsell later. Test the four ways of stating any discount (percent off, money off, time free, total price). If fast money isn't coming, widen the contrast. Give buyers what they want now so the business can give them what they need later. Track the share of lead magnet and DIY takers who book a call within 14 days, and how many of those buy: that first step is the number to fix. When the paid-step test runs, also log the core-offer close rate of those who paid for the small step against free takers.
- M30 [after the first target (F3), choosing the next attraction offer to test] [stage: S3+] Test one at a time, in this order of fit. (a) Pay less now or pay more later (fits one-off services): nothing today with a payment mandate taken at sign-up, so the full price collects automatically after delivery unless they cancel in time; or pay now at 20% to 50% off plus bonuses, in place of the guarantee. Tie the right to cancel to having met the conditions (such as attendance) and to a promised clear yes-or-no result measured before and after, easy to track and hard to dispute. Have continuity ready for the end, because pay-later only pays off fully with a next offer. Offer pay-now only after they accept pay-later. Track the conditions, which are the actions that get the most value. Too many pick pay-later: raise the pay-now discount or bonuses; too many pick pay-now: the reverse. Over 10% cancelling means overpromising, conditions too low or a price too high. Watch anyone who says before the deadline that they haven't got the result. Recurring version: a higher continuity rate starting in 30 days, or pay less today and keep the lower rate for good, plus bonuses. (b) Win your money back: they pay, then earn it back as credit by hitting results, doing set actions, or both. Criteria must be easy to track (prefer things already logged automatically), produce results (copy what the best clients do; expect a few attempts) and advertise the business (a disclosed review or case study, X3). Make check-ins mandatory, because each is an offer point. It works for new, current and past clients. Train them exactly on the criteria. Sell it as earned, then halfway through, privately, ask about their long-term goal and offer to credit the fee towards the next offer whether or not they hit the short-term goal. At the end, let the losers win: anyone who refused and missed gets a second offer crediting the fee towards staying. Present the credit as a discount spread over a longer, dearer offer, so they keep paying and keep skin in the game. Run it only if refunds are under 5% and the owner is comfortable paying back those who ask. It needs a back end bigger than the core offer, because the credit must be no more than a quarter of the next offer (M35). (c) Giveaway: one buyer wins a full place at its genuine price; every other entrant gets an entrant offer worth 10% to 30% of gross margin off, stated in the rules from the start. Entry asks fit and need questions about their problem, never rewarding praise of the business; more effort to enter means fewer, better entrants. Run it 3 to 7 days, capped at the number of entrants the owner can call within 7 days, with a daily countdown on every permitted channel carrying the prize's benefits and social proof. Collect explicit marketing consent on the entry form (see X2). Announce the winner publicly, contact everyone else privately to book a call the same or next day, and give the entrant offer a 7-day claim deadline. On the claim call, open with the discounted core offer and compare its genuine value with what they pay; if no, offer the same percentage on another product, spreading any recurring discount over the longest term they'll accept, after which billing moves to the normal rate. If it flops, the prize wasn't big enough: bundle a known supplier's product into the grand prize. A second prize for when someone they referred wins drives referrals. Prize rules follow X3. (d) Buy X get Y free (continuity): more free than paid, free items can differ from the paid ones, and it lengthens tenure. Test several cheaper free items against one dear one, and a free framing against the same percentage discount. Keep the margin by raising prices first, only within X3. Offer prepaid deals to no more than about 10% of existing members to protect regular cash, and keep selling to prepaid clients. Ring-fence the cash (M26).

### Upsells
- M31 [a client finishes a step, a new problem appears, or the core offer is ending] Classic upsell: offer the fix to the next problem at the moment they notice it ("you can't have X without Y"); solving one problem always reveals the next. Existing clients are the likeliest buyers; understand their problem better than they do. Upsells fail when they're unwanted, too different, mistimed (before they feel the problem) or not believed. Three types: more of the same, a better version, something new. Offer the higher-profit upsell first. Close with "You don't want anything else, do you?" Hold back a few small bonuses for buyers on the fence and add them one at a time; give all of them to everyone who buys. While the avatar's problem is at its peak, keep making relevant offers. Predict the common mistake buyers make and offer the fix at the point of sale. Use free bonuses that reveal a problem the upsell solves. Give access as soon as possible, ideally a preview before they say yes: what they hold is harder to hand back. Name bundles ("Fastest results", "Minimum package"); items can later be pulled out to make downsells. Build the next offer into delivery and hint at it early. End every session by booking the next, so they leave knowing when and why you meet again. Offer as many fixes as there are problems the business can solve within scope. Speed is a price variable too: alongside the standard turnaround, offer paid priority versions of the same work (sooner, or jumping the queue) at a premium. It is not free margin: price it on the value of the time saved to the buyer and on the capacity it takes from other work, offer only turnaround times that can be met every time with the current capacity (see X9), cap how many priority slots are sold at once, and track the share bought and how often the time is met. Charge for extra guarantees or cover (5% to 50% on top) instead of giving them away. Paid cover is never pure profit: price it from its expected claim cost plus margin, and track claims against what it brings in; keep the core offer's guarantee (O8) inside the price, and sell only cover that goes beyond it and beyond the buyer's legal rights (see X11 [Fixed]). Earn on things the business doesn't deliver through disclosed referral fees (M18). Set an ascension target from warm existing clients and replace it with own data. Before the first paid batch ends, write down the next three problems a client meets, with the offer, bonus or referral for each. Upsell at the point of greatest need, not greatest satisfaction: straight after a big win they feel full, so tell them what's coming rather than pitching then. Engineer an activation point: when a member hits a set, provable result, recognise it and offer a call about the next level, rather than pitching everyone. Add a higher tier only once successful clients keep asking for more; separate tiers by implementation help (costs the owner's time, priced higher) versus zero-marginal-cost material. Activation call line: "you'll get there on your own; the question is how long it takes" (no time figures until results prove them, O15). Recognising a member's result, labelled as the owner's own recognition and never an industry award (O25), is also when to ask for a video and evidence, with written consent to use them as proof (M45, X5). Position continuity and every later offer as building on the core offer's method, never a new method that implies the first was wrong.
- M32 [sales call, renewal, or proposing add-ons] Menu upsell: unsell what they don't need, crossing out options that genuinely don't fit this client and saying why; unsell lower-margin items (or give a free fix for a minor problem) so the money goes to the higher-margin offer; when the business can't help, point them to a cheaper option elsewhere: the goodwill sells the rest. Prescribe in detail what they do need, how it fits what they already have and how to get the most from it, talking as if they own it, and write that personal plan (what, who does it, when) into the proposal titled after the client, with the client's name only on what really is theirs (M13). Then ask A or B instead of yes or no (this batch or the next, upfront or two payments, email or call support, start date); both options must be real and either must suit them, never a choice of staff by sex or any other protected characteristic (see X18). Then make paying easy by asking how, never whether: for existing clients, offer to add it to their existing payment; for a first purchase, ask which address the invoice and payment form should go to. Nudge where they lack experience ("most clients your size start here"). If a batch is full, take payment for the next with clear dates. To sell both of two offers, price "both" at the dearer single offer, if margins hold. When hiring, let staff unsell and show clients how to get the most value. Every paid extra appears on one menu with the full total before they sign; never introduce a new charge after the price is agreed (see X3, X3). Unsell only when it is literally true for this client, never as theatre (O11). For non-price asks (early-client commitments, testimonial format, staff time), offer three real options with the preferred one in the middle, each one the owner would genuinely accept (M27, M33).
- M33 [presenting price on a call] Anchor upsell: present the premium first at 5 to 10x the main offer, sell it properly, and wait for the gasp; then rescue (ask whether they need the premium level of contact or whether the core offer would do it), present the core offer (same primary features, cheaper secondary ones, about a fifth of the price or less), and ask how they'll pay. No gasp means they find it reasonable: ask how they'd like to pay, and don't gasp yourself. Have the rescue offer ready before they react. The anchor must be real and something the owner would happily deliver: a fake anchor fails and loses trust. Worse than offering ten times the price to someone who can't pay is offering a tenth to someone who would have paid ten times: solve the problems of buyers who can pay most. Judge premium take-up only on calls with buyers at the top of the avatar. Log the reaction to each anchor (gasp, interest, or laughter and "is that serious?"): an opening that reads as absurd backfires, so if the premium reads as not serious at the top of the avatar, say what justifies it or lower its price. Test (L63): tag each core-offer buyer by whether the premium was shown first, and compare attendance, task completion and continuity take-up.
- M34 [setting tier prices] Read the tier mix: if most buy the cheapest, narrow the price gap between it and the middle so the middle looks the obvious deal; if most buy the middle, nudge them to the top; if most buy the top, raise all prices.
- M35 [win-back, an upset client, a competitor's unhappy client, or a current client buying again] Rollover upsell: credit some or all of what they paid towards the next, dearer offer. Who: past clients 6 or more months since buying, upset clients (before any refund), competitors' unhappy clients (credit what they'd lose by leaving), current clients. What: more, better or new. How: all or part, upfront or spread as a periodic discount. The next offer must be at least 4x the credit (a discount of 25% at most); credit enough to motivate and test the level. Make it one time only, available in the moment it's offered. Win-back steps: list past clients, check what they paid, decide the credit, offer it, ideally with a personalised video each. Find competitors' unhappy clients through public complaints or places where buyers share gripes, within data and marketing law (see X2, X8). Gift card play, to test after the first target: sell vouchers at about 10% of face value, at most two per client, usable only by another buyer, with a face value of about 20% of the next offer's price; ask who it's for and get an introduction (face value and tax within X3).

### Downsells
- M36 [any no] A no is to this offer, not to every offer and not to the owner: find out what they want and make another offer. Downsells are trades: if the business gives something, it gets something. Personalise, never pressure. Only offer what the business already sells. Never drop the price of the same thing in the moment: that is discounting, breeds resentment and kills trust. Wanting to pay less now means a payment plan (M37); wanting to pay less overall means a feature downsell (M39); nobody pays less "just because". Planned price tests on a set number of prospects are fine. Stay a helpful guide finding the best deal for them, ask "Fair enough?" after each change, and alternate payment-plan and feature downsells in one call. Everybody buys something. Discounting lowers how buyers judge the work and trains them to wait for the next deal: the price is the price.
- M37 [a buyer says it costs too much] Payment plan downsell: "too much" often means too much today. Present options from most cash upfront to least and stop as soon as they buy: (1) reward paying in full: state the instalment total as the price and the upfront figure as the saving, with at least 10% off for paying upfront; (2) "Would you rather I set the payment terms or you set them?", then suggest their business credit card, so they set their own terms and the business is paid today; (2b) layaway: they pay in instalments, split as they like, before delivery starts, and delivery begins once it's paid in full (no credit risk, and it fits pre-selling, M14); (3) half now, half when their cash lands ("When does your income come in?"; "What's the most you can put down today?"); (4) a 1 to 10 check: 8 or more, "we'll find a way" and keep going; 7 or less, ask what would make it a 10 and move to a feature downsell; (5) three payments; (6) payments spread evenly over delivery. Seesaw version for short calls: "Would you rather have big regular payments or small ones? Pay upfront and there are none; the more you put down now, the smaller they get." During any plan, offer the upfront saving again. People pay slower if allowed and faster if rewarded. Schedule collections for just after their income lands, use automatic payment collection, and let the provider retry failures within card and scheme limits; never retry a hard decline. Check plans add buyers: overall close rate should rise while the share paying upfront stays the same. [Fixed] Instalments, credit and third-party finance only within X3. Never call it "paid in full": it's the upfront saving. Alternative to test: a smaller saving for half upfront, so each step down costs a little more. Time any instalments to delivery milestones (O42 delayed second payment). Instalment plans (not M30's pay-later offer) never run past the end of delivery (layaway before, or payments within it): payments after the value is delivered feel like paying for nothing. Upfront payers can get first pick of places and session slots, if that priority is real and limited (O36).
- M38 [a buyer declines continuity, or for any recurring offer that needs their work] Trial with penalty, only as a downsell after a no: they start without paying if they do the success actions, taken from the M30 (b) criteria (attend the check-ins, do the tasks, log results, and one that advertises the business, such as a disclosed case study, X3), and pay a stated fee for each missed action (per action is fairer than one lump fee, unless a single miss wrecks their result). What they get is the stripped-down version, or more if affordable. Steps: (1) offer it last; (2) take a payment mandate, because people who won't commit and do the work complain more and convert less; (3) sell staying: "If this gets you the result, will you stay?"; if no, don't offer the trial; if they want more explanation, say they won't hit the long-term goal during the trial but will build the habits, and there is no honest quick fix; (4) explain every criterion, its cost if missed and its benefit if met, and the check-ins, which are offer points: "we charge if you miss them because it's the only way we get you results"; (5) they initial the fee terms. [Fixed] All terms are stated before they give payment details (see X3). Never say "it's how we've always done it" while it isn't true. Afterwards: liked it, offer longer or higher; hated it, ask what should have been different, take the blame, ask to make it up, offer the better-fitting tier; didn't use it, contact them several times before the end and waive the fee if they meet the owner. Let people make up a slip before billing. Troubleshooting: nobody takes it, lower the requirements or fees; nobody follows through, explain the fees better and make meetings mandatory; nobody stays, sell staying, deliver better and fit the back end to the front end; it's printing money, change nothing. For one-off services, use pay less now or pay more later (M30 (a)) as this downsell instead. Before any reward or fee tied to a count (here, M30 (b), and later T1 and L69 pay plans), write down how it could be met without the real result and measure the outcome as well as the count, then check the drop-off in the month after any clawback, payout or reward window ends, stretching tiers past the known churn months (M9) rather than one cliff.
- M39 [price objection after payment-plan options] Feature downsell: lower the price by changing what they get. First remove something valuable with a small price drop, which makes them reconsider the original; then keep removing features, highest value first, until they buy: something beats nothing. Remove what they dislike for a big drop; remove what they love for a small one. Levers: quantity (fewer sessions or deliverables), quality (response time, call length, one-to-one or group, live or recorded, done-for-you, done-with-you or DIY, personalisation, guarantee length, conditional or unconditional terms, priority or waiting, fixed or flexible slots, who delivers, how long access lasts, rescheduling terms), dropping a whole feature ("keep chat and email, drop the calls"), removing the guarantee ("pay less without the results guarantee, or keep it: which would you prefer?"), and finally the DIY route: "Even though we won't work together on this, I still want to help: come to a free walkthrough", then give them the free DIY tier at the end. Name the top package aspirationally and the bottom "the Minimum package" ("so nothing more than the minimum?"). After two refused changes, ask the 1 to 10 question: 8 or more, move to payment plans; 7 or less, "What would a 10 look like?" and recombine. Standardise the combinations once the owner knows what buyers value. Continuity declined: offer a minimum continuity tier (core materials plus email support at a lower rate) alongside the trial with penalty (M38). Downsell current members before they cancel: a lower price for only what they use. Barter for advertising on a price objection: a stated amount off for an honest review, a case study, progress posts and two introductions, disclosed and never conditional on being positive [Fixed] (see X12, X3).

### Continuity
- M40 [designing or pricing continuity] Continuity gives ongoing value for ongoing payments until they cancel: sell once, get paid repeatedly, and end with more clients to upsell. Use it only for ongoing value. It brings less cash today, so it goes last in the sequence, after attraction, upsell and downsell cash; continuity on its own is hard to advertise, so lead with the valuable thing they get. Its tools are bonuses (M41), discounts (M42) and waived fees (M43); prefer rewards to punishment. Bill less often: default to the longest billing period that fits the client's natural cycle (quarterly or annual over monthly), and offer annual prepay; where billing is by weeks, every 4 weeks gives 13 payments a year. Present it as a plan (support through each cycle), not a subscription; continuation terms are still agreed upfront (X3). Price continuity below the core offer per period, because value per period falls once the pain is solved; the core offer, sold at peak pain, carries the high price and is collected upfront where possible (M37). The reason they buy isn't the reason they stay: at each renewal and exit interview ask why they stay, and sell and price continuity on that reason. Ask for one or two reasons ("what's the main thing that's changed?"), never "list all the reasons", here and in D12, M45 and C5. Alternative to test (after the first target): a lower base plus a fee per unit used, so smaller clients don't cancel in a quiet period; check how buyers react to variable bills first. Continuity must deliver something used up each period (a fresh pack, updated templates, a short "what changed" brief); a period with nothing new invites churn. If the core offer makes the client independent, design continuity as the next level, judged on ascension (M31), not more of the same teaching, and don't make peer community its core value. Later only, after the first target: a private members' space can be tested as a support add-on, kept in a system the business owns (L36), with the same limits as M12; judge any platform on own data. Frame it as keeping the core investment working only where that's true, never leaning on sunk cost (M27). After the first target (M18), negotiate member-only partner discounts worth at least the periodic fee and show the saving at each renewal, disclosing any commission (O41, X3). After 12 months of members, test a higher price or a higher-touch tier. Features buyers tend to pay most for: exclusive access to the owner, exclusive content, exclusive events and a regular briefing. Frequency of access sells; a fixed question format lets one call answer many. Busy buyers may value quick written access over calls. Price recurring support so a client can afford it in its worst month, not just its average one (test after early clients). List every deliverable as one-time or ongoing: one-time items justify the core price, only ongoing items justify the continuity fee, and a thin ongoing column gets fuller before any price rise. Launch with the minimum and build more only from what members ask for in the first two periods (L59 question log); beyond the core pack, send material only when there's a real problem to fix. Price changes, stated in the terms before they sign (X3): on a rise, existing members keep their rate for a stated period with written notice; on a cut, they move to the lower rate from their next period, so nobody has to cancel and rejoin. Before removing or replacing anything members rely on, tell them first and keep the old version for a stated period (M22). No lifetime deals: every paid service has a stated term (materials the client keeps are fine, M9); if the business stops serving a segment, finish fixed-term commitments as sold and refund the unused part of anything open-ended. Hold member Q&A when clients are free, never in their busiest weeks. Recurring revenue lifts the business's value (F30 step 9).
- M41 [selling continuity] Continuity bonus: give a valuable bonus for joining today, worth more than the first payment, related to the core offer and built from what the business already has. Sell the bonus and its genuine value first, ask "Do you want to know how to get this free?", then "Join today and it's a free gift, or buy it on its own for its stated price: which would you prefer?", then stack the other member bonuses with their values. A bigger gap between the bonus's one-off price and the first payment pushes more buyers into continuity. Bonus types: more of the same, something complementary, an upgrade, limited availability. Making bonuses members-only pushes everyone into continuity. Bonuses only for longer commitments trade fewer sign-ups for more committed members. Present the prepaid block first, sold sincerely as a real option, then the regular plan as the step down (alternative to test: join first, then "Want to save even more?"). Work out the real lift in 30-day profit from the billing period, not from monthly examples. A prepaid block rolls automatically into regular continuity when it ends, with a renewal reminder beforehand (X3); line the last core-offer instalment up with the first continuity payment so payments never stop. Give titles by tenure. Test a physical bonus on a digital offer. Use realistic values only: anchors from prices actually charged. Alternative to test: continuity starts by default when the core offer ends, with every term stated and initialled before signing and an easy way to opt out (X3), against offering it at the end. Set take-up expectations from own data.
- M42 [offering free time or a discount for commitment] Continuity discount: before offering free time, decide how the discount applies and the cancellation policy. Methods: upfront (delays cash; only where contracts are reliably honoured and churn is low), at the end (earned if every payment is on time), spread evenly (the default: it keeps cash flowing; option to keep the lower rate for life after the term), or after the first one or two payments (take the first and last period upfront or a small activation fee, so cash covers costs and the payment method is proven; a nominal first payment sits better than "free" when asking for payment details). Extend the term rather than eating into it, and use the shorter version as a feature downsell. Front-loaded discounts convert more and churn more; back-loaded, the reverse. Soft cancellation terms bring more sign-ups and more leavers; strict terms, fewer of both. Option to test: give the discounted time as a voucher, usable after the first period or passed to another buyer with an introduction. Start a lifetime discount just past the month most members leave, and remind them as it approaches. Cancellation fee: the discount they received, so leaving early returns them to the normal rate. Make cancelling easy and visible, and complete without any call; afterwards, invite everyone to an exit interview, waiving the cancellation fee if they come: open with the goal they set at kickoff (D13), let them vent, be angrier about the problem than they are, fix what's fixable, and if they wanted more, roll them into a higher tier (M35). Ask for a backup payment method, with no fee for it [Fixed] (see X3). End every cancellation, and every early client that doesn't continue, with one specific thing they did well, a warm line and an open door, and treat leavers as a "not now" under L12 unless they asked not to be contacted (X2).
- M43 [long-term continuity terms] Waived fee: rolling terms with a set-up fee of 3 to 5x the monthly rate, or the fee waived for a 12-month commitment, payable only if they leave early. Justify it by real onboarding cost: "If you only want to test us, you cover the set-up; commit for a year and we cover it." They initial the early-exit line. The fee ends when the commitment is met. A bigger fee pushes commitment; a smaller one (1.5 to 3x) brings more rolling cash. If more than 5% want to leave early, investigate the product: nudge, don't handcuff. Fees follow X3.

### Proof
- M44: moved to X3.
- M45 [asking for a review or testimonial] Specific testimonials with a before, an after and a number build the most credibility. Many genuine reviews beat a perfect average. Invite every client to review, not only the happy ones, and never publish only the good ones; label any chosen quotes as testimonials (see X12). Make the ask small and urgent, with exact steps ("it takes less than 60 seconds: click here, pick 1 to 5 stars, write one line"). Follow L37 and X3. Success interviews (asked as the favour they are, any perk disclosed, never tied to a positive answer): ask about life before (internal feelings and external numbers), what made them act, their biggest concern before buying and why they went ahead anyway, then life after, inside and out. The concern-and-decision part speaks straight to prospects. One interview becomes a testimonial, content and later an ad, with written consent and none of their customers' data (L33). [Fixed] A clip cut from a testimonial must keep the speaker's meaning; any claim in it needs documentary evidence; consent must cover use in ads; personal details beyond the business result stay out (see X13, X8). Never draft testimonial wording for a client to sign: quote their own words from the success interview, edited only for length with their approval. Ask for the testimonial and success interview (and repeat the L66 referral ask) within a few days of a clear, measured result; after a narrow one, wait for the next. Link the ask to what they and their team have already done, true only, never a guilt line (L66). [Fixed] No actors, composites, AI-generated faces or voices, or stock photos presented as clients; any illustrative person is labelled as such (see X13).
