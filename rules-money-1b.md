## Money models (M), continued

- M19 [spending business money or deciding what to do with profit] Early money only on tools, implementation help and paid tests. Reinvest profit, keep a personal cash buffer, keep living costs low. Business revenue isn't personal wealth, and the first real milestone is personal savings that cover years of living costs. Build a business that runs without the owner, so it becomes an asset worth a multiple of profit. Money is wasted, stored or multiplied, and reinvested profit compounds. After the first target, reinvest first in people (T3, T1), then brand: moments that get talked about without paying directly, which wait under L4. Brand shows in three numbers, checked at each quarterly L1 re-score: cold reply rate over time, continuity take-up and renewal, and the price the business can hold. Plan personal wealth from profit taken and saved, not from a future sale. Growing revenue usually beats cutting costs. Before any spend above a threshold the owner sets in the instructions box, convert it into hours at the M1 hourly rate, estimate the extra monthly gross profit it should bring, and spend only if it pays back within 12 months on cautious inputs (cost doubled and sales halved, still paying back within 12 months and within M26's runway); log the estimate and check it on the date set (F2). If a countdown, "last places" or rival buyers create an urge to buy a tool, course or list now, wait until the next day, then run the payback check; for anything bought or bid on, write the top figure and the reason before talking, and treat bidding against others as a cue to walk away. When a client payment lands, split it straight away: tax reserve first, then a fixed personal pay, then buffer and reinvestment; keep personal pay fixed so every rise in income goes to the buffer or reinvestment, and check reserve rates with a tax adviser (see X19). As an alternative to a fixed split, keep a watermark: leave in the account the monthly cost of the next growth step plus reserves, and pay out everything above it. Take some cash out every month even while reinvesting, and treat a rising personal balance with lower personal spending as a standing goal: the cash forces real cash flow and less stressed decisions. Hold an annual-bills reserve (insurance, regulatory fees, renewals) divided by 12 and set aside monthly. After the first target, set a written split of profit invested against profit consumed. Write these money decisions once, as standing rules, so they never take attention from selling. While the business can use extra cash at this payback, it goes back in; only true surplus beyond the buffer goes to the passive side of a barbell (all active risk and attention in the business; low-risk, passive holdings run by regulated managers on the other side), aiming for a steady return. Never take an investment pitch on the spot: score it on four questions (does it keep the capital safe, is it tax efficient, does it pay out while held, does it grow), size it so a total loss wouldn't touch the buffer, and take regulated advice before acting (see X4); this is a question list, not investment advice. The further a bet sits from what the owner is skilled at (for most owners: getting clients, selling, pricing and delivering), the likelier the loss, so put active money and attention only inside that skill and treat anything outside it as a gamble, sized so a total loss is small against the owner's savings and income. When a pitch comes from a friend, or everyone seems to be making money from it, treat the urge to act fast as the cue to wait a day (as above). Before any opportunity brought by someone else, ask: if the returns are as claimed, why are they selling it rather than keeping it all; how many years they have done this exact thing themselves (under two or three years, decline); and will they put their own money into the same deal alongside the owner (if not, decline). A friend's gains are not evidence. Test with the smallest unit first, buy only what has been checked by someone independent, and prefer a surer, smaller return to a riskier, larger one. Taking no pay while "reinvesting everything" counts as reinvesting only when the owner can state the return on that capital; if they can't, the business isn't making a profit yet, so fix margin and price (M1, M2) before calling it growth. Every expense line must answer how it makes money; an upgrade (a bigger or nicer site, tool or fit-out) passes only if it beats the plain version on the payback test above. If the owner still wants an upgrade that fails the test, and only once the personal buffer is in place, fund the difference with new money: a defined extra project using something already owned but underused (spare hours, spare space, a one-off client project the business would not normally take), with the target sum and end date written down, rather than core income, savings or open-ended debt; any borrowing for it needs a written plan to repay within about a year and no early-repayment penalty. Once the business pays the owner (from S1), check a wealth ladder each quarter, one rung at a time: active income covers costs; a fixed share of personal pay goes automatically to long-term holdings once the buffer is in place; the owner holds something that pays to own it (something that pays only when sold is speculation); passive income starts to cover costs. Start small, never with borrowed money. Tax: know the law, follow the rules, document the truth; once profit is large enough, get planning from a tax adviser separately from year-end preparation, during the year, never by prepaying costs in the last weeks just to cut the bill. A business's value grows with its retained, recurring profit or revenue times the multiple buyers pay, so keeping clients and raising lifetime value often builds more wealth than taking cash out; that growth is not taxed while it is unsold, but it is taxed when the business or shares are sold, so treat it as tax deferred, never tax-free, and take advice on how a sale would be taxed (X1). Before putting money into anything outside the core business, answer five questions in writing: can it compound over years; who controls it; what happens if it fails (how much comes back); is the upside clearly larger than the downside; can the owner explain how it makes money and what could go wrong. Concentrate only where the owner both understands the risk and has control (usually the business itself), and spread money out where neither holds. Size every outside bet so that losing it all leaves the business and the owner's buffer intact, and prefer lowering the risk at the same return over chasing a higher return. Speed makes money and time makes wealth: act fast on opportunities in the business, and hold good decisions (owned equity, held assets) for years rather than turning them over. Borrowing magnifies gains and losses alike: borrow only against collateral to buy an income-producing asset whose return is known, after the first target, never to fund unproven acquisition or spending, and with a qualified adviser (X4, X19). Keep passive investments passive: spending owner time to squeeze a few points more from them costs more than that time earns in the business. Four places money for a purchase can come from: debt (costs the future), savings or reserves (costs the past), this month's cash flow (costs the present) and a new opportunity taken on for it; fund an unplanned purchase or learning spend from new, extra paid work or sales taken on for that purpose where possible, so the payment split, reserves and growth plan stay untouched, and never from consumer debt. If a project reveals a downside with no cap (liability for others' losses, fraud exposure, regulatory penalties the business can't size), stop it whatever has been spent, since the sunk cost is small beside the open exposure; before starting anything in a regulated field, list the exposures with an adviser who works in it (X4). Outside investors' incentives differ from the owner's: many funds earn fees on money deployed and want it spent fast, and money raised on a story ties the business to that story even when the numbers say otherwise, so prefer funding growth from customers (pre-sales, prepayment, M20) and treat any raise as a decision under T3 with advice. Before buying a business, list why buy it at all (what it gives that can't be got more cheaply, such as a supplier relationship), and treat two findings as red flags: a large share of revenue from one customer, and an owner who is leaving but holds the know-how and relationships undocumented. Every business takes on a debt of some kind: funding it yourself trades money owed for a shortage of talent and systems early on, while outside money trades ownership and control for speed; outside capital fits mainly models that need large sums before any profit (heavy research, physical build-out) or winner-take-all markets where the first to scale keeps the market, and most other businesses do better growing from their own profits. Beyond acquisition cost against lifetime value, track the return on all the capital a new unit needs (fit-out, licences, recruiting, set-up): while that return is high, reinvesting in the business usually beats putting profit elsewhere. What a buyer pays for a business follows its economics, not its label: revenue retention, client retention, gross and incremental margin, how much value one staff member can deliver, and how likely future profit is. Adding software to a service, or billing a service through software, changes nothing unless those numbers change; a tech-enabled service is worth more only because one person can serve many more clients at a lower skill level. Compare models on total return for the effort, not on the multiple alone (a lower-multiple, higher-price model can return as much), and build for profit and retention rather than for a category name. A bet with good odds can still ruin a business that can only afford one or two tries: size each bet so the business survives several misses in a row, and take long-shot bets only from money it can lose entirely. There are three ways to make a business worth more: more clients, more value per client, and less risk (how likely the profit is to continue without the owner). Buyers apply a multiple to profit (usually earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation) that rises with: profit that is reliable and growing, recurring rather than one-off revenue, more than one way of winning clients, no key person (F30), a category with better economics (a tech-enabled service only counts if its numbers change, see above), more size, and a longer record of not shrinking. List the business's risks and turn them one by one into strengths (a single channel into several, an owner-run function into a documented team), since each removed risk both stops a discount and adds to the multiple. Every spend of time or money should name which of the three it serves. An offer to buy the business is a risk decision: how sure is further growth, how much revenue recurs, how exposed is it to one channel (L62); read the terms (cash at completion, deferred or seller-financed parts, how long the owner must stay, share sold) with an adviser (X4) and ask how the owner's day changes; if it doesn't, the sale buys little. Write what the owner will do the day after before signing. A growing or steady business keeps gaining value, but one that starts falling becomes hard to sell at all. When to hire with profit is the same risk choice: what the owner draws is what can't be reinvested, and full reinvestment matters most only in a race for network effects. A loss-making business is only "reinvesting in growth" if it could take profit now and has a written return thesis for each reinvested sum; otherwise it simply isn't making money. Buyers and investors value an untested plan by discounting it for the risk it won't be executed, so the trailing numbers set the price.Before planning a sale, check that businesses of this type actually sell: look for recent deals in the category and compare what those businesses have that this one lacks. If the engine is the owner (an audience, a coaching programme, a personal brand), keep it and sell what it produces instead: product lines, client businesses grouped into a roll-up, or a minority stake raised against the whole, so the owner keeps the engine.Before selling, write what the proceeds will be put into and the return expected after tax, and compare it with what the business already pays out each year: a business that reliably pays a high yield is often worth more kept, or partly sold, than turned into cash that must find a new home (X20, X21). For each new unit (a location, a machine, a team), count every cost to open it (launch marketing, fit-out, recruiting, staff until it pays) and track three figures: the payback month, the first-year return and the yearly profit at capacity against that cost. For a unit the owner works on actively, require a yearly return at capacity far above a passive market return, because the owner's time and attention are part of the cost (the speaker asks for at least three, ideally five, times the capital each year, a method threshold, not a measured norm). Payback depends on two things: the size of the fit-out and the skill of launching; opening in a new area where no one knows the business is a different skill from running nearby units, so test a launch plan that spends enough before opening to start near capacity. If whether a deal or opportunity makes money isn't obvious without a detailed model, put it in the too-hard pile and pass; T3's spreadsheet is for checking the downside, not for finding an upside that isn't plain. In deals the owner wants to repeat (partners, suppliers, joint ventures, sellers of businesses), leave the other side a fair share rather than negotiating away every last point, since being the first call for the next deal is worth more than the margin squeezed from this one. Before rolling out more units, simplify the format for capital and running cost (the least space, the fewest staff and the core range most buyers choose), since a smaller, simpler unit at a similar profit needs far fewer openings to compound, and do not count on one acquisition channel staying cheap. Once surplus money attracts offers, write investment rules in advance (the asset types the owner knows, a share of money for each in proportion to how well the owner knows it, the largest single cheque) and decline anything outside them at once, which saves the diligence time spent on deals that would be refused anyway. Deal rules for any investment, purchase or partnership deal, written as if-then rules: size each so the owner could afford to make it at least twice; if it looks like a grand slam, get a second opinion; if the owner can't list how money could be lost, don't do it yet; if it feels rushed, pause; if it is the only deal on the table, find others to compare first; if it is complicated beyond the owner's understanding, pass; no diligence, model or track record, no deal; a counterparty who misses the response times they set, no deal; if it costs peace of mind or doesn't build the owner's reputation, don't; borrow less than the maximum available; and no written contract, no deal (anyone who insists on a handshake only is declined). With a new partner, do a small starter deal first, even when everything looks right on paper. Before putting money into anything, know step by step how it comes back, and where in the order of repayment the owner sits if it fails. Judge an opportunity by how long it takes to double, not only its yearly rate. Keep the owner's personal money rules the same as the business's (for example, no debt in either). Before a service or education business builds its own software to make clients stay or to raise the sale price, the software must pass on its own: people buy it as a product, use it regularly without being told to, and recommend it by name; if clients only use it because the service tells them to and leave it when they leave the service, it is part of the service, not software. Without that test, put the money into making the service itself keep clients longer, and get any tool clients need through a referral deal with an existing product. Never buy something the business would not otherwise buy because it is tax-deductible: the deduction returns only the tax rate on the cost, so most of the money is still spent; buy what would be bought anyway, or an asset that builds value.
- M20 [designing the money model, or reviewing a new client] Get paid to get clients: cash collected in a client's first 30 days should exceed what it cost to win and deliver to them (the owner's hours at the M1 hourly rate plus any spend). Track it for every client; if it doesn't, fix pricing or payment terms before adding volume. Treat covering the cost once as the floor and twice as the target: when 30-day cash is at least twice the cost to win and deliver to the client, each client pays for themselves and for the next one, and any multiple above that funds other work. Never fund acquisition on borrowed money such as a credit card's interest-free period on the assumption the 30-day cash will arrive in time (M19). The 30-day window lets cash be recycled into the next client without outside funding; the usual fix is an immediate upsell or upfront payment. Choose the way of pulling cash forward that adds the least operational drag. To design for it: add up every cost of a new client until their next payment (acquisition including commission, any equipment, delivery until the next bill), then build the first transaction so the blended average cash collected per sale beats that total: a core option (for example a fee for equipment the client keeps plus the first period) and a higher prepaid option that includes the equipment free, presented as an either-or (M32); count the blend at the share each option actually takes. Equipment the client hasn't paid off returns if they cancel early, under written terms (X3). Whether a smaller first offer is needed depends on how much information the buyer needs and how cold they are: buyers from cold ads rarely buy a large offer from strangers, so sell a fast, low-effort first step that solves one specific problem with a large, quick return (step one of the larger process, sold on one call), then offer the main offer at the first result the client notices, again a week later and once more before the first period ends, crediting part or all of the first step's price (M35); buyers who already know the business through content or referral can go straight to the main offer. Gift cards and prepaid credit bring cash in before anything is delivered; account for them as owed until redeemed, and check the rules in the project's country on expiry, refunds and unredeemed balances before relying on any credit going unused (X3, X19). Four ways to shorten payback, in order of least drag: the first and last period paid up front; a set-up, onboarding or enrolment fee on top of the recurring price, which must be shown as part of the total price from the start (X3); an optional upsell at the first transaction (M31); or a third-party finance provider who pays the business up front for a share, only where X3 allows it (in the UK, no introductions to finance providers without the permission X3 names). Draw the cash timeline per client month by month from minus the acquisition cost, and prefer the version that reaches zero sooner at the same lifetime gross profit: it survives a downturn better.
- M21 [free early-client or delivery places are full] Offer free clients the choice to pay the current price or step aside for a paying client, and collect their review either way.
- M22 [deciding how much to deliver, or making delivery more efficient] Sales to fulfilment continuum: the more the business delivers, the easier it sells and the harder it is to fulfil. Create flow, monetise flow, then add friction: overdeliver on the first offers to get cash and demand moving, then use that cash to systemise, keeping the same promise while changing the how. Only once people are saying yes, add friction or deliver less for the same price. Overdelivering stays within the owner's set hours (F1). When delivery outgrows those hours, first move more of the core offer into one-to-many assets and group sessions; hiring deliverers is the second route, not the default (D5). Before adding a delivery batch, audit session length and the gaps around sessions to fit more clients into the hours cap (F1); keep a change only if results hold. Where it fits the offer, paid delivery defaults to a small group, with one-to-one kept for the premium tier (M7); check it against the M1 margin bar on the first clients. Label every early-client extra (one-to-one time, fast replies, extra sessions) in writing from the start as temporary with an end date; cut service levels only for new batches, and existing clients keep what they were sold until their term ends (see X3): taking back what people were given provokes more than never giving it. When the business does something extra, say briefly and truthfully what it took, never inflated (O11) and never as a debt to repay (L46, M27). Free early clients follow the paid offer's structure (sessions, tasks, timeline), with any extra support logged separately in hours, so the gap to the paid version is known. Size a small group so each buyer pays less than one-to-one (often about two-thirds or less of the one-to-one rate) while the hour earns several times more, and keep it small enough for personal attention (members take turns, the deliverer corrects live). Where the result depends on a measurable input (problems solved, sessions done, calls made), sell the outcome and build the plan and price around that input.
- M23 [judging whether advertising or outreach is profitable] Measure lifetime gross profit (everything a client ever pays minus everything it costs to deliver, including the owner's hours at the M1 hourly rate) against the full cost to acquire them. 3:1 is the minimum, not the target. Each human step in attraction, conversion or delivery raises the ratio needed: 3:1 with no human steps, 6:1 with one, 9:1 with two, 12:1 with all three. Count the business's human steps: 3:1 stays the floor until the first target, and the matching higher ratio is needed before scaling with hires. These ratios are floors for running profitably, not the padding scaling needs: growth with human steps is lumpy, since new outreach staff, new sellers and new delivery staff each cost money before they are proficient and some never become so, colder audiences convert worse and management layers add cost, so a business with all three steps manual should aim far higher before scaling (the speaker's own aim is 20:1 or more, a method threshold, not a measured norm); below that, keep improving the offer, price and delivery cost in the one unit rather than scaling. Lifetime value is roughly annual gross profit divided by annual churn, capped where demand is expected to fade (M10). If cost per customer is within 3x the industry average, work on lifetime value; above that, fix advertising or sales. Of the two levers, raising lifetime gross profit beats cutting cost per customer, which hits diminishing returns first. Whoever makes the most per client can spend the most to win them. Work out lifetime gross profit simply: last year's total gross profit divided by the number of clients (it underestimates repeat buyers, which is the safe side). A very high early ratio (10:1 or more) signals room to scale: push volume while it lasts, because ratios fall as volume rises and absolute return is what counts. An unusually low cost per customer from a new method is usually temporary: plan the money model on a normal cost per customer, not the best month. Once members have left, cross-check lifetime value with the average total paid by churned members, and the implied churn as the periodic fee divided by that average. On the first clients, compute lifetime gross profit against full acquisition cost and record the ratio: human outbound must clear 3:1 on it. Acquisition cost is everything spent to win clients in the period (media, marketing and sales pay, commissions, tools) divided by new clients won; use the last 12 months where there is the history, to smooth out good and bad weeks. With little history, estimate churn from one cohort: of the clients paying at the start of last month, how many still pay 30 days later. To cut acquisition cost, work the funnel front to back (cost of attention, the hook and the offer, then later steps), because early steps touch the most people; jump to a later step only when it is far below the norm for the field. Two speed checks sit beside the ratio. First, cash in the first 30 days: aim for gross profit collected from a new client within 30 days of at least twice what it cost to win and deliver to them, so each client pays for themselves and for the next one and cash stops limiting growth (M20 sets the 1x floor; prepayment with a real bonus, M37, M40, and a fitting next offer, M31, raise it). Second, the cost of a permanent client: divide the cost to win a client by the share who stay two years or more (if 1 in 10 stays, the real cost is 10 times the cost per client); compare that with what a permanent client is worth, and aim outreach at the traits the permanent clients share, even if each lead costs more. Each year compare how much the cost to win a client rose with how much advertising costs rose; if it rose much faster, suspect clients telling others not to buy, and check satisfaction, refunds and retention before spending more on ads.
- M46 [every working day, any "what next" question, any plan, or any result reported] Daily numbers: the business runs on one ratio, lifetime gross profit to acquisition cost (M1, M23), and every day starts by updating it and choosing the next action from it. Keep one numbers sheet, one row a day: new clients won; spend to win them (media, sales and marketing pay, commissions, tools); acquisition cost, rolling 30 days and 12 months (M23); price, delivery cost per client and monthly churn, giving lifetime gross profit per client (M1); the ratio against its bar (3:1, higher with each human step, M23); and the funnel counts (reached, replied or clicked, booked, showed, closed, L30). Missing figures are estimated, marked "Proposed, not decided", and replaced by real ones as they come. Then pick the next action by this order and name one action and the number to check tomorrow: (1) too little data to read (below L43's sample) means keep the daily inputs (L41) and log; (2) ratio below its bar with acquisition cost far above the field's norm, or one funnel step far below its norm, means fix acquisition front to back (offer, hook, targeting, then the weak step, M23); (3) ratio below its bar otherwise means raise lifetime gross profit (price, delivery cost, churn, then upsells and the back end, M1, M24, M31); (4) ratio at or above its bar with delivery capacity free means more volume on what works (L62, F3); (5) ratio high but delivery full means fix capacity before more volume (D11, O34). Any activity that does not raise lifetime gross profit or cut acquisition cost waits (F2). Track a number only if a change in it, up or down, would change what someone does; drop the rest, and cancel any recurring meeting that only shares numbers without deciding an action from them. Tracking a step usually improves it, so start measuring before changing anything. Track two pipelines: click to close (click rate, opt-in, booking, show, offer, close, cash collected) and close to resell (activation, time to value, churn, gross profit per product line, ascension rate by cohort); if only one is possible, track the one where the business is weakest. Optimise the dominant number, the one whose gains carry the lesser numbers with them: clients actively using or retained over sign-ups, retained revenue over bookings. Monthly, work out where the business levels off if nothing changes: new clients a month times lifetime gross profit (and times lifetime revenue) gives the monthly gross profit (and revenue) it settles at, and new clients a month divided by monthly churn gives the number of clients it settles at. If that ceiling is above today's figure the business grows towards it, so plan delivery capacity and hires before it arrives (D11); if below, it is shrinking whatever this month shows, so find whether sales fell or churn rose (usually delivery) and plan headcount down or fix the cause. Work it on trailing 90-day, 6-month and 12-month averages, since a small business's monthly figures swing.
