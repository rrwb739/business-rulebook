## Strategy and thinking (F)

### Growth, constraints and the owner

- F17: moved to T3.
- F18 [planning strategy or where to put resources] Ask what would have to be true for the business to be number one in its niche, price it, and put resources behind that rather than small improvements. Also ask: what one thing, if true, would make most other problems go away? Before any paying client it is often pilot proof (L32, C7). If the business is not growing, it is falling behind. Better step rates never prove the strategy is right: check the L3 need-to-believes before reading the funnel numbers. Run the F30 strategy check first.
- F19 [solving any business problem with no single right answer] Think divergently: list many possible answers before choosing, then pick the one that is most right. Use convergent maths only for questions with one answer. Before solving, restate the worry as a question about one number.
- F20 [the owner is stuck, or deciding what to learn next] Entrepreneurship means building skills and beliefs. Soft skills are bundles of small, trainable skills that are hard to measure: train them like any other skill. Beliefs set the ceiling: list the reasons given for why the owner cannot do something and test each one; the objection answers used on buyers (C14) work on the owner's own stalls too (time, money, permission, fear of choosing wrong). Name the missing skill or belief, then learn it from experience and high-quality sources. Advice from people ahead can still be bad: judge it on evidence. Gut feel is only a tie-break where no number exists, never over one. If output swings week to week, the gap is sustaining performance: track the daily outreach streak (L41) and fix the first day it breaks. Enthusiasm is trainable too. The owner is worth what has been proven, not what is felt deserved: entitlement before results shows up as a shaky price (C7). When a result is poor, start with what the owner did or did not do: regulators, the market and hours are conditions to plan around, not reasons. Use what the owner has now rather than waiting for case studies, a network or funds. State a stall as plain behaviour with no "because", name the action that fixes it and reward doing it that day; drop labels like "I'm not a salesperson", because reps are the test (C13). Test each belief three ways: define it as a behaviour in a condition, count how often it actually happened, and ask what it changes for the first target; if nothing, drop it. After a setback, track the days until the outreach streak is back, and aim to shorten them. When the next move is unclear, build capacity so the next opportunity can be seen and taken: savings (F26), the skill most limiting growth (F25), an audience that sees the work (L33), people ahead (F15). Capacity building runs alongside the daily outreach minimum (L41), never instead of it. Keep slack in the day: in the 1973 Good Samaritan study, students told they were late helped far less often than those with time to spare, so a schedule packed to the minute misses what is in front of it. When an action the owner knows they need feels impossible, ask what trade they are unwilling to make, then play the feared consequence out step by step in specifics; most fears shrink once stated concretely. Build confidence by naming a past area where repetition worked and applying the same volume to the new skill.
- F21 [writing the owner's bio, pitch or founder story] Ideas come from one of three Ps: pain, past profession or passion; one is enough, and profession beats passion as a source. Tell the true story of why the problem matters to the owner, never overstating it (O11), and never name an employer unless the owner's contract allows it. Write the owner's true skill stack in one line and use it as the "only I can say" line (O2), never overstated (O11); to buyers, say it as what it means for them, never as a list of credentials. The bio names that one thing, never a list of ventures: other side work stays out of buyer-facing material. Keep a 3-line host bio for anyone who introduces the owner: the skill stack line plus one true result, never implying the business has traded as long as the owner's experience (O25). Before citing any credential, ask whether the avatar would see it as relevant, and drop the rest. Keep a dated early-stage log: one true line and a photo for each hard moment (a run of noes, the first no-show, the first pilot signed), as material for the founder story and the C14 story bank, with no prospect or client names, emails or screenshots (see X8). Write one origin story in these parts: backstory, desires, the wall, the epiphany, the plan, the conflict, the achievement, the change in the owner; split desires into the external goal and the internal struggle (found by asking why until it lands on status or being trusted, only as far as true, O11), told as the true moment the owner saw the method work, never implying the owner ran the avatar's kind of business (O11). The owner needs to be ahead of the buyer on what the offer covers, never on regulated matters outside it (X4), and says so when asked. In a bio or intro, give one true external fact and one line on what it changed for the owner, never a posture of expertise (F15). Open the origin story with one or two true identities the avatar relates to plus one true flaw from O11, and give one true line on the moment the want went from "should" to "must", never used to pressure a prospect (M27).
- F22 [tempted by a new idea, or doubting the business] Focus is measured by what is said no to; commitment means removing every alternative of attention and time (no side ventures, no second channel), never irreversible money or job steps, so a disproving number can still be read honestly (F26, M19). The cycle is uninformed optimism, informed pessimism, the valley of despair, then restarting or becoming an informed optimist: the new idea looks better because its valley has not been hit yet, so compare year 3 of this business with year 0 of the new one. Every restart resets the clock, and money left on the table is the price of focus. Pause other ventures rather than run them alongside. The exception is a better vehicle for the same skills: leverage (people, capital, code, content) is output for input, and a switch is worth it only when the new vehicle gives far more leverage for the same skills, ideally in the same industry, and someone further ahead agrees; usually deepen the current vehicle, getting more from the leverage already in use (templates and one-to-many assets, O34) before adding kinds. People often quit an industry when they really quit a function: change role before changing industry. Be married to the game, not the vehicle, and keep the owner's identity separate from the business. Before adding any venture (after the first target), the core offer must pass an absence test: it runs without the owner for at least a month, six months preferred, on checklists alone (D5), passing only if the business holds steady or grows, with every hole logged; role-play likely problems with whoever covers first, and if the owner quietly does the work, the test has not happened. Write every new idea on one idea list, tell no one, and do not act on it; within 24 hours write it up on one page against seven questions (is it a good idea; does it feel right, a tie-break only, F20; what if wrong, M19; can a test be afforded; the main tasks; who does each, as action, owner and hours within F1; Plan B with stop-loss points), then park it. Set stop-loss points (the money, months or number that ends it) in writing before starting any project. Review the list only when the current constraint is solved or after the first target, and pick the single biggest; an idea that still looks good after a month earns a look. Before any switch, ask: if the owner did only this for 40 years, would it succeed, and if it made 10 times the money, would the owner stay? If yes, stay. On hitting the first target, spend at least the next quarter on more volume for the core offer (L62) before reviewing the idea list: the head start in reputation and proof is the moat (O2). If a shortcut looks easy, write what would need to be known to be sure before acting. After the first target, allow one genuinely new thing a year; before it, trying new things stays within L3(b). Write down what the business is a vehicle for, beyond money, and check hours against it each quarter. A later second offer line needs its own hours and owner. Before adding effort to any problem, first ask what to stop, cut or hand off. Each quarter, list every commitment outside the core offer that takes hours and pause, hand off or end each by a written date, or write why it stays and its end date (F3). Better beats new: output is volume times leverage. On the first business day of each month, list what the owner knows should be done for the core offer but is not, order it by output for the effort, and work down it before any new idea, tool or source, asking "if I were coaching me, what would I tell me to do?". A repeated mistake means the lesson was not learned: log it in F24 with the changed behaviour. Patience is a behaviour: not acting on the shiny thing while still wanting to. New ideas usually come from one of three places: a pain the owner overcame, a professional skill the owner can sell to several businesses at once, or a passion; prefer problems the owner has lived with for years, since the avatar's words and the failed alternatives are already known (L1, O5). What can be sold: products, services (the buyer saves the time to learn, the time to do, or both), software, access (physical, digital or media), risk and money; selling risk or money is regulated (X4). When the owner runs two businesses and the second splits attention, set a replacement test: could the better business replace the other's net income within a stated period (months, not years) with the owner's full attention? If yes, and the better one already converts and keeps clients, exit the other on a written plan, accepting a lower sale price for the focus it buys (M19, X20 on how a sale is taxed and structured), rather than waiting to polish it for the best valuation; if no, keep it and stop treating the second as a side venture.
- F23 [planning the week, or feeling short of time] Foundation: productivity is the money got out for the time put in, and there are two ways to invest time. Maker time is a few long, uninterrupted blocks (about four to six hours, usually two a day at most) for work that builds something new (the offer, scripts, outreach batches, content, systems, the product); one short meeting inside a maker block wastes the whole block, so for maker work an empty calendar is the most productive one. Manager time is many short slots for calls, coordination, decisions and training, where a full calendar is the productive one. Nearly all the work that moves the business forward is done in maker time, so plan it first and protect it: (1) at the start, with other commitments, take one maker block at the very start or end of the day and use weekend blocks too; (2) once there is some control, maker in the first half of the day and manager in the second, booking meetings from the end of the day backwards so the morning stays whole; (3) later, whole maker days and one or two stacked manager days. Offer meetings only in set meeting windows, with a few spare slots for genuine one-offs; tell people when replies will be slow and when they will be fast, then keep to both; if a meeting must break a maker block, turn that block into manager time and do every other pending call in it. A request for "five minutes" is answered with the next meeting window unless it is truly urgent. Work flat out in the maker blocks the owner has protected, or the protection isn't earned. Most gains come from stopping things, not adding routines: while one priority is set (F2, F30), run a season of no, where every other request, call, event and side project gets no by default until the priority is done. Count each no as a point scored for focus. Two things can't be equally important: time spent equally means neither is the priority. Having time to reply is not an obligation to reply; answer messages in set slots and only those that serve the priority or a client. Once the owner knows what needs doing, stop seeking more information (events, groups, courses, sources) and execute; return only when a specific gap blocks the work (F25). Keep the same sleep and wake times every day, weekends included, and give health and other areas their minimum effective dose during the season rather than progress on every front. Remove consumption apps from the phone and post, if at all, from a computer at a set time. Run a one-week time audit in 15-minute slots, then hand off or systemise the biggest block. Protect the outreach block first (L70). Set each task's deadline to the hours it really needs, not the next convenient slot. Do work early before known crunches (the owner's other commitments, the avatar's busy seasons): lead research, scripts, follow-up batches and tool fixes done ahead. In heavy seasons, cut non-core commitments to a small fixed minimum on set days, fix the environment for the outreach block, and before a hard push tell the people closest to the owner the plan, the hours and the end date. Send the 12-week input target in writing to one or two people ahead in the channel (F15) and report the real number to them each week. When a slump hits, keep going 15 more minutes before any break. Any 5-minute task is done now. Rest that protects output is part of the plan, within F1 hours. Run a visible timer in the outreach block with an estimate set first, pause it at every interruption, and log focused against planned minutes each week; under about 75% focused means fix the environment before adding hours. All notifications off except lead replies and booked calls (L72); everything else is pulled at set times. A pre-work routine stays only if output is higher on days it is done (touches before noon, L41); sleep stays protected. Buy back recurring personal chores only once the business earns more per hour than they cost (M19 buffer first), and put the freed hours into the outreach block, never past F1's cap. Start with the one-off, low-cost sleep basics (a dark, quiet room, no screens before bed), since better rest raises output per hour for every hour worked; then buy back chores in order of lowest cost per hour saved, one at a time, keeping each only if the logged hours actually move into business work. Use unavoidable travel or waiting time for small business tasks (replies, reviews, notes). At the start of each week delete every meeting that isn't needed; after every recurring meeting ask whether it needs to happen again and whether a person or a system could replace it; each quarter review all recurring meetings, counting a meeting's cost as its length times every attendee's hourly rate. With a team (S3+, T1): mark who does maker work, give them a weekly quiet day with no meetings or messages, ask each maker what their ideal week looks like and follow it as far as possible, treat a maker's no to a meeting as protecting the work, extend trust measured on output and deadlines rather than visible activity, say which hat (maker or manager) the owner is wearing that day, and keep makers out of meetings they don't need. Book call blocks and big decisions for well-rested days. After an upsetting call, a run of noes or bad news, make no price, discount, term or supplier decision until the next business day (M5, M36). Assign every business slot to a named job (making or managing), and keep an open-loops list. Put sales calls and client sessions into fixed call windows on several set days a week, around the owner's other commitments, so a same or next working day slot always exists (C3), while research, writing and build work keep whole half-day blocks with no calls; for group sessions, ask each client's named lead to send questions the day before. Copy work gets one or two fixed copy blocks a week, at the same desk with the same short start-up routine, outside and after the outreach block (L70): the only goal is the page (no checking replies, trackers or the bank balance), run with a ringing timer in cycles of about 30 minutes' work and 5 minutes' break, writing or doing nothing, with a pad nearby; rotating between 2 or 3 assets of the core offer is fine, never across ventures (F22). Cap claim and source research for copy (O47) at about an hour a session in its own block, and spread a big asset's research over several days (lead research keeps L8). Capture hooks and headlines away from the desk on a pad (L58). In outreach, copy and build blocks, the phone stays in another room with distracting apps blocked; check the inbox and calendar at the end of each block, at most every 50 minutes, so lead replies still go out within the hour (L13, L72).
- F24 [starting any new skill: sales calls, emails, delivery] Write down the number of reps after which it would be unreasonable to still be bad, and commit to it. After every 10 to 20, review the best 10 to 20% and change one thing. When something fails, find the step that failed, never "it doesn't work". Doing beats reading. Drill a corrected line several times in a row, work on one skill for a week or two, and study runs of wins as closely as losses (C13). After every win, write one line on why it happened. After a big win, check the next 2 weeks of the L41 scoreboard: if inputs fell, the win is costing the owner. Only real outreach, real calls and role-plays in call conditions (C13) count as sales reps; employer work, reading and rulebook work do not. Log focused drilling hours alongside reps: aim for about 20 focused hours of script drilling and role-play before the first live pilot sales call, and give any new tool or skill about 20 focused hours over 2 to 3 weeks, starting within 48 hours of deciding, before calling it too hard. Keep the first recorded call and first email batch; at call 20 and 100, and email 300, compare them side by side and write what changed, and use that rather than comparing with others when discouraged (L71). When an input falls short, raise the bid on the same method (more volume, more follow-ups, a better asset) and write the next bid down before calling it failed, within F1 hours and L43's stop rules. Keep a running lessons log, written the day each lesson happens, losses as well as wins. In each review, a trait counts as a factor only if all the top reps share it and the rest mostly lack it; drop the rest as superstition.
- F25 [learning the market or a skill fast] Consultant method: ask one expert for the 5 best people they know, ask each of those the same, interview them all, sort notes by category, and distil a few core truths. Interviews with the avatar must be genuine: say up front that the owner is building an offer for them and that a sales conversation may follow, and never disguise a pitch as an advice or research request (see X9; X2, M27, O11); those who then show interest are engaged leads. In those interviews, ask how they built what they run and let them tell it; ask what they do all day, what is easy, what is hard, what causes stress, what their bosses or partners want and how they measure success; ask what they have struggled with, for how long, what it is like when it goes wrong and what the ideal would look like, and, about past solutions, what they had to start doing that they disliked and what they had to give up that they enjoyed (O28); log the answers in the O5 phrase bank, and record only with consent and no client personal data (X5). When an avatar member or peer has been cool, ask one small, genuine favour of their expertise, saying plainly if a sales conversation may follow, never an invented favour. Paying for access to people who have done it compresses years of mistakes: not knowing a skill is ignorance debt, a yearly bill equal to the gap between what the owner earns now and would earn with it (M19). Buying a skill means paying someone who already gets the result to watch the owner do the activity and give feedback until the owner gets it without them; buying only gives permission to start. Compare any spend on a skill with what an index fund would return. Skills compound: choose the next skill partly by how much it multiplies the ones the owner has. Investing in the owner's own skills is the lowest-risk spend: put as much as affordable, within M19's cash buffer, into the one skill most limiting growth now. Writing teaches a market: write the whole method up as the DIY version during the pilots. Run a course exactly as taught once, then keep what works; ask its success stories what they did beyond the teaching. Before paying for any course, coach or tool, write the result and the date, and commit to being its best success story; if it fails, first list what was not applied. Before paying a coach or a room, ask what they got wrong at the owner's stage and how they fixed it, and prefer one who can name specific failures (F28). Label each learning spend "what exists" (rooms, people far ahead) when the options are unknown, or "how to" (an expert watching the owner, T2) when known. Learn just enough of a skill to get it working, and deepen it only when the next stage needs it (L6). Pay down ignorance with the cheapest currency first (interviews, communities where the owner gives first, employer-funded training, favours), then money from a fixed monthly education pot within M19's buffer, spent only on the one skill most limiting the first target. In any chance conversation with a member of the avatar, ask about the problem the offer solves, thank them and log their phrases (L59); no pitch unless they ask, and say a sale may follow if it might. With no skill and no money, the fastest route is to work for or alongside the best operator the owner can reach, for long enough to copy how they work: who the owner learns from matters more than the industry. List up to 20 people the owner admires in the community, rank them, and approach the top ones offering one free, proven piece of value first (F15), never a bare request. Read by current constraint: on the topic that limits the owner now, read the best-reviewed books in turn rather than whatever is recommended, then move to the next topic. Skills build on each other: before buying a programme, check it starts where the owner is and ask what it assumes they already know; when one fails, look first for the missing earlier skill before judging the teacher.
- F26 [thinking about leaving a job to run the business full time] Only once the owner has 3 to 6 months of savings and the business has matched the job's income for 6 months in a row (not one good month: small businesses swing). Leaving early means more work for less money for a long time. Until then, the job pays the owner to learn: use it to build skills the business needs, say which skills are wanted and ask about training, and when it stops teaching what the business needs, ask for a stretch project or a funded course. Keep living costs low so personal risk stays small and business risk can be bigger. Treat the business as the future main business, not side income: if 40 hours on it cannot beat 40 hours of the job, it is not working yet. Do not judge it, or listen to others judge it, while it is small. Inside the job, a move to a role with more responsibility usually pays more than asking for a raise.
- F27 [someone criticises the owner in public, or a competitor talks the business down] [Fixed] Never argue point by point. Two options: grow so big no one hears them, or answer with kindness ("You're right, I'm far from perfect. Congrats on what you've built."). Claim the owner's flaws first (new, small, in business to make money; O11). Most criticism is a different preference, not a fact; build the tallest building instead of knocking others down. One client's criticism is a preference; the same point from 3 or more independent clients is a fact: log it (C14 objection log, M9 cancellation log) and fix it (L75). Never complain to prospects, clients or in public about regulators, suppliers, competitors, clients or workload: say the fact and the next step. When a message or slip annoys the owner, write the reply but do not send it: a neutral acknowledgement the same day if needed, then the full reply only after a night's sleep, reread from their side and sent calm and factual, or not at all. Before sending, answer: could they be right, even partly; will this fix the problem or only vent; will it bring them closer or push them away; what would winning cost? Count complaints settled without escalation (L75). Never pay or pressure anyone to remove, bury or suppress genuine reviews or posts, and never post or commission counter-reviews; ask a platform to remove a post only if it is fake or defamatory (see X12). If a rival uses fake reviews, actor testimonials or false scarcity, report it privately with evidence to the regulator or the platform (see X12), never call it out in public or to prospects (O2). For a public hit piece, do not acknowledge it to the owner's audience: put the time into client results and proof.
- F28 [the owner feels stuck, results plateau, or is choosing what to work on] Name the business the owner is really in, which is often not the one assumed. Look at whoever is a hundred steps ahead, and ask people already in the market what the hardest part is. Ask who the largest competitors sell to: if bigger buyers, note it as an up-market direction for L2 after the first target, not a reason to switch now (F3). A business is limited by its product (build), its selling, or its people (lead). Before changing the product, act as the owner's own consultant and try changing only how the same delivery is packaged (sell the goal, not the sessions). Rate each function out of 100 against what is out there (outreach, offer, sales calls, delivery results, proof, and finance: cash collected, M1 margin, M26 runway) and work on the lowest, watching for time spent on the enjoyable part instead (F4). Before naming the constraint, check the avatar (L3, F12). Separate bugs (fixable) from features (built into the market, such as urgency fading after an external deadline passes, M10) and build around the features. Know the business shape: an education-shaped offer grows fast with low retention by design, and service-shaped delivery caps growth at the owner's hours (D11); where retention is the built-in hard part, price it and work on it, and build the next offer before any known stop (M9, M10). The four shapes and their built-in hard parts: education or info starts fast and is hard to scale because buyers leave once they have learned; software starts slowly and burns cash and time for a long period before it scales fast; e-commerce scales fast but strains cash (stock bought ahead) and suppliers as volume grows; service is slowest but steady, limited by hiring, onboarding and training people. A field many people want to work in is easy to staff and hard to win and keep buyers in; a field few people can do keeps buyers and is hard to staff. Hitting the hard part of the business's own shape is the nature of the business, not a reason to switch (F22). Put a price on solving the problem to stay motivated. When choosing between two paths, name each path's big hairy problem and ask which the owner would rather have and is better equipped to solve today. A local maximum is a plateau only a step back can get past: check it is the same vehicle with a better customer, not a new vehicle (F22). Borrow proven sales and delivery processes from unrelated industries, not just hooks. If a change is inevitable (a better-fit avatar, a price rise, the next offer, M10), make it now on purpose. At each new stage (F10), list what most hours go on and ask whether it was the last stage's constraint; if it no longer moves the constraint, hand it off or cut it. Name which side limits the business: until the first target usually demand, fixed with outreach volume and proof; after it supply (the owner's hours, D11), fixed with templates and one-to-many assets (O34) before hiring. Then decide whether the weak function needs scaling (it works: L62) or fixing (it is broken: the D3 order of question, delete, simplify, speed up, automate). Name one priority for each 12-week block, write down what stops or pauses, and put every spare business hour on it; routine work carries on. Compare the constraint with the industry norm: short of demand before any paying client is expected; the opposite of the industry's shape means something is very right or very wrong. Copy what people ahead did at the owner's stage, not what they do now, and before copying a tactic ask whether others did the same and failed. The problem-type and one-constraint steps are in F30. Decide on purpose whether the owner wants to be the expert or the business owner, since both pay and each is a different game: the expert path keeps doing the work and raises the price as demand passes capacity (M5); the business-owner path hires and trains others, buys in bulk, systemises and automates (F34, D5), which means learning skills outside the craft (leads, sales, hiring, operations) and takes years, about as long as learning the craft did. Switching paths halfway because the second is hard is the common mistake: write the choice in the instructions box and judge it after a fixed period, not after a bad month (F22). Seasonal demand is one of the most common built-in hard parts: treat the swing as the business, not a fault; pair a service for each season that the same people can deliver, never describe either season to the team as the off season, plan cash and staffing around the peak (M26), and fix the selling and price of any season that loses money before cutting it (M5, M23). To diagnose any stated problem ("our messaging is off", "sales is the issue"), ask three questions in order before acting: what exactly does it mean (define the term in plain words), how do we know (a number or observed fact, such as the share of calls that are unqualified or don't understand the offer), and why does it matter (what result moves if it is fixed, M46). A problem that can't pass the second question is a feeling to measure first, not a constraint to fix.
- F29 [deciding what to work on or learn, or tempted to polish branding, a logo, a website or a CRM] Money comes from three skills: build (make the thing that gets the result), sell (everything from a stranger noticing the business to paying) and lead (getting other people to build and sell). They are a tripod: selling without a good product is lying at scale, a good product nobody hears about earns nothing, and leading without the other two organises people around nothing. Start with what to sell, then learn to sell it; leading comes once there is someone to lead (T3). Logos, branding, websites and CRMs are neither building nor selling. Use technology on all three to get more done per hour, raise quality and lower the skill needed. Owners carry the risk and keep the upside: keep ownership rather than give away equity early, without leaving a job early (F26). Skills cannot be taken away (F25). Selling one to one is the quickest of these to learn; marketing and copywriting take longer, because they translate the same persuasion into other media: learn to sell first, then turn what works on calls into emails and pages. Selling first is right until the first target, but a rising reply rate does not show the product is good: only measured results and referrals from paid clients do (L32, L65). Once paid clients exist, put fixed weekly hours into the product (D15) and track the share of new clients that came from referrals.
- F30 [planning strategy, a quarterly review, results stall, or choosing what to work on next] Strategy check. (1) Name the problem type first: not enough clients, not keeping the clients, or not making enough per client (M1's levers). Pick the one that applies, put all resources on it, and ignore everything else until it moves; before any paying client it is the first. (2) One constraint: find it with numbers (L30, L63, F28), fix it, and only then look for the next. (3) If the business is not making money, either it cannot solve a valuable problem or nobody knows it can: check the product (pilot results, D8) and distribution (engaged leads, L39) first; more money comes from solving bigger problems (M2, O1). (4) Two things matter, a great product and wide distribution: make the core offer the best option for the avatar, get it in front of more of them, repeat until there are no more to reach (L2 widening rule), and treat everything else as a distraction (F29). (5) Basics done with consistency and precision beat complexity: get bigger by getting better (L65); alongside L62, better here means the product and its results, while for lead flow more volume still comes first. (6) Growth usually fails because things break when volume rises: before raising outreach or adding capacity, check what would break (D11, M25) and fix it first. (7) A system is doing the same thing 1,000 times without the owner: every repeated task gets a checklist and template (D5, D6), and the F22 absence test is the proof. (8) Easy to sell and hard to scale beats hard to sell and easy to scale: high-touch delivery is right first, then move the same results into templates and one-to-many assets (M22, O34, O43). (9) Model value for an exit: recurring products beat recurring services, which beat one-time products, which beat one-time services; if the core offer is one-time, the recurring follow-on (M8, M40) is what makes the business valuable, so track core-to-recurring conversion as a strategy number; a recurring product is a later option, never before the first target (F3, F16). An expert-dependent business makes a good income but little enterprise value: while it needs the owner for every sale and session it is an enhanced job. That is fine for the first target; after it, move every repeated task into checklists and recorded assets (D5, O34) and grow the recurring offer, so the business passes the F22 absence test and could be sold. Owned systems and software add enterprise value and rented tools add none, so after the first target, where it would raise the sale value, consider owning the software for the core workflow, built under F16's conditions. Choose by the goal written in F22 (income, cash flow or enterprise value), and change vehicle only under F22 and L3, never because another vehicle has a higher ceiling. When choosing or comparing vehicles, prefer low capital spend: a business whose output can rise many times without its cost base rising in step (services, digital products) usually keeps more cash and pricing power than one that must buy stock, equipment or capacity before it can grow (F28 shapes); a model that has kept making money for generations, through downturns, is evidence the model itself works. Choose the vehicle with the highest likelihood of success over the one tied to a passion: at scale every business is run the same way (a team, sales, marketing, finance, operations reporting to the owner), so the game matters more than the product (F22). (10) After the first target, choose one competitive advantage that gets stronger as the business grows, rather than weaker, and build it into how the business works: clients who make the service more valuable to each other (a buying group, a community, shared data); data or methods no rival can legally copy (owned data, a trademarked name, contracts that protect know-how); costs that fall per client with volume; owning more of the chain from supply to the client; a brand that keeps its promises (O2); lawful long-term agreements with suppliers or distributors; buying small, fragmented local competitors and combining them; or giving away what rivals charge for and earning on something else, priced so the cost of the free part divided by the share who pay stays inside M23. One done fully beats several started; each is checked against competition law first (X20) and waits for its roadmap stage (F32). Expert-dependent practitioner businesses (a clinic, a studio, a professional practice) usually succeed in one of two end states: a recurring model clients rarely leave, or specialised, very high-priced packages (often a year of service) for problems buyers will pay most to solve; pick one and nail it in the first location before anything else. The usual path after that: learn to get leads and sell without depending on outside agencies (L40), then bring in a second practitioner motivated by a minority share of that location's profit or equity, set in writing (T3), then grow by buying existing practices from owners ready to leave, often at low prices, and moving them onto the proven system, rather than opening new ones (M15, X20, K3); expect this to take years. Once one business works, owners of the same kind of business are a market for its proven system (training, licensing, software), but only with measured, provable results the buyer can run without the owner (X1, X9, K2). After the first target, buying and building (acquiring businesses or assets and improving them) usually creates more wealth than selling, since the buyer sets the terms; it is a later-stage path checked against the stage gate (F32), M19's five questions and the four goods (T3). Once the one constraint is named, attack it many ways at once rather than one: list ten or more ways to move it (for a lack of staff: recruiters, trade forums, relocation bonuses, hiring from competitors, ads, trade shows, school placements) and run as many as the owner's hours allow, judging each on the constraint's own number; when it moves, stop the ones that didn't help and put that effort into the ones that did. The constraint is usually the part of the business the owner enjoys least, since the parts they enjoy already get their attention, so check the calendar: most of the owner's best hours go to the constraint. Every change to something that works has a near-certain short-term cost (people retrain, other parts are affected) and an uncertain gain, while leaving a working process alone usually lets the team improve at it: so a small team makes only one or two material changes a year, each expected to beat the dip it causes, chosen by impact, confidence and ease (D9), and starts from the result wanted rather than from what is lying around.
