## Strategy and thinking (F)

### Growth, constraints and the owner

- F17: moved to T3.
- F18 [planning strategy or where to put resources] Ask what would have to be true for the business to be number one in its niche, price it, and put resources behind that rather than small improvements. Also ask: what one thing, if true, would make most other problems go away? Before any paying client it is often pilot proof (L32, C7). If the business is not growing, it is falling behind. Better step rates never prove the strategy is right: check the L3 need-to-believes before reading the funnel numbers. Run the F30 strategy check first. Test any claim, from a source, a team member, a vendor or the owner, with three questions before acting on it: what does it mean in observable terms (replace labels such as "motivated" or "lonely" with what actually happened), how do you know (the evidence, and whether the claimed cause also appears without the effect), and so what (which decision or action it would change, and which number it would move: more clients or more value per client). Invert a hard goal: list the quickest ways to ruin the business or miss the goal (nobody hears of it, a product people regret and warn friends about, debt it can't carry, a key person who can leave with everything), then write the opposite of each as an action; people spot threats far faster than opportunities, so the inverted list often finds what a positive plan misses.
- F19 [solving any business problem with no single right answer] Think divergently: list many possible answers before choosing, then pick the one that is most right. Use convergent maths only for questions with one answer. Before solving, restate the worry as a question about one number.
- F20 [the owner is stuck, or deciding what to learn next] Entrepreneurship means building skills and beliefs. Soft skills are bundles of small, trainable skills that are hard to measure: train them like any other skill. Beliefs set the ceiling: list the reasons given for why the owner cannot do something and test each one; the objection answers used on buyers (C14) work on the owner's own stalls too (time, money, permission, fear of choosing wrong). Name the missing skill or belief, then learn it from experience and high-quality sources. Advice from people ahead can still be bad: judge it on evidence. Gut feel is only a tie-break where no number exists, never over one. If output swings week to week, the gap is sustaining performance: track the daily outreach streak (L41) and fix the first day it breaks. Enthusiasm is trainable too. The owner is worth what has been proven, not what is felt deserved: entitlement before results shows up as a shaky price (C7). When a result is poor, start with what the owner did or did not do: regulators, the market and hours are conditions to plan around, not reasons. Use what the owner has now rather than waiting for case studies, a network or funds. When cash is short, trade the product's spare capacity (a place on a session, an unused slot) for recurring costs or services the business needs, asking for less of their service until the swap matches the product's normal price; agree the value of each swap in writing and record it as a sale, never as untaxed (X19). Ask openly for help and favours early: people help someone who says what they need, not someone posturing as already successful (F15). State a stall as plain behaviour with no "because", name the action that fixes it and reward doing it that day; drop labels like "I'm not a salesperson", because reps are the test (C13). Test each belief three ways: define it as a behaviour in a condition, count how often it actually happened, and ask what it changes for the first target; if nothing, drop it. After a setback, track the days until the outreach streak is back, and aim to shorten them. Judge each setback on four measures: how much it took before the owner's work changed, how far it dropped (missed outreach days, sharp replies, skipped follow-ups), how many days until normal output returned, and whether the new level ends above or below the old one. Feelings can stay; actions keep the normal standard: once the owner notices a dip, ask what they would do on a normal day and do that, starting with the next outreach block, and do not let the person or event that caused it set the day's behaviour. After recovery, ask what the setback can be used for (a script fix, a new rule, a check added) so the new level ends higher. Short sleep and missed meals lower all four measures, so protect both in hard weeks. When the next move is unclear, build capacity so the next opportunity can be seen and taken: savings (F26), the skill most limiting growth (F25), an audience that sees the work (L33), people ahead (F15). Capacity building runs alongside the daily outreach minimum (L41), never instead of it. Keep slack in the day: in the 1973 Good Samaritan study, students told they were late helped far less often than those with time to spare, so a schedule packed to the minute misses what is in front of it. When an action the owner knows they need feels impossible, ask what trade they are unwilling to make, then play the feared consequence out step by step in specifics; most fears shrink once stated concretely. Build confidence by naming a past area where repetition worked and applying the same volume to the new skill.When a stall is stated as a fact about the world ("this business isn't scalable", "no one can sell like me", "marketing doesn't work for us"), restate it as a gap ("I don't know how to scale this yet") and ask what would physically stop it; if nothing would, it is a skill to learn and a problem to price (F28), not a limit, and the owner usually stops at the first problem of a kind never solved before.Nerves before a call, meeting or talk usually signal under-preparation, not a trait: prepare more, and rehearse in conditions as close as possible to the real event (live, in front of people, on the real device), so attention is free for the surprises that can't be controlled (C4, C13).When buyers, staff or peers seem to doubt the owner for how they look, sound or how old they are, treat it as information, not an excuse: first check honestly whether part of it is a real gap the owner can fix (sounding unsure on calls, missed times, thin proof), then ask the doubter plainly what it would take, and aim to beat that standard rather than argue the bias, since proving a bias exists changes nothing about the next sale; another buyer may say yes. Never answer doubt with a false claim (F21, X9); and inside the business, hold everyone to the same job-relevant standard (T1, X18).
- F21 [writing the owner's bio, pitch or founder story] Ideas come from one of three Ps: pain, past profession or passion; one is enough, and profession beats passion as a source. Tell the true story of why the problem matters to the owner, never overstating it (O11), and never name an employer unless the owner's contract allows it. Write the owner's true skill stack in one line and use it as the "only I can say" line (O2), never overstated (O11); to buyers, say it as what it means for them, never as a list of credentials. The bio names that one thing, never a list of ventures: other side work stays out of buyer-facing material. Keep a 3-line host bio for anyone who introduces the owner: the skill stack line plus one true result, never implying the business has traded as long as the owner's experience (O25). Before citing any credential, ask whether the avatar would see it as relevant, and drop the rest. Keep a dated early-stage log: one true line and a photo for each hard moment (a run of noes, the first no-show, the first pilot signed), as material for the founder story and the C14 story bank, with no prospect or client names, emails or screenshots (see X8). Write one origin story in these parts: backstory, desires, the wall, the epiphany, the plan, the conflict, the achievement, the change in the owner; split desires into the external goal and the internal struggle (found by asking why until it lands on status or being trusted, only as far as true, O11), told as the true moment the owner saw the method work, never implying the owner ran the avatar's kind of business (O11). The owner needs to be ahead of the buyer on what the offer covers, never on regulated matters outside it (X4), and says so when asked. In a bio or intro, give one true external fact and one line on what it changed for the owner, never a posture of expertise (F15). Open the origin story with one or two true identities the avatar relates to plus one true flaw from O11, and give one true line on the moment the want went from "should" to "must", never used to pressure a prospect (M27). Tell the whole story, including what the owner was bad at: buyers believe the good parts once they hear the bad ones, so it sells better than an edited version. A feeling of being an impostor usually means a claim is overstated; restate it truthfully (what the owner did well, what failed, and why this offer exists) rather than pushing through it. Speak and teach only on topics where the owner has proof.
- F22 [tempted by a new idea, or doubting the business] Focus is measured by what is said no to; commitment means removing every alternative of attention and time (no side ventures, no second channel), never irreversible money or job steps, so a disproving number can still be read honestly (F26, M19). The cycle is uninformed optimism, informed pessimism, the valley of despair, then restarting or becoming an informed optimist: the new idea looks better because its valley has not been hit yet, so compare year 3 of this business with year 0 of the new one. Every restart resets the clock, and money left on the table is the price of focus. Pause other ventures rather than run them alongside. The exception is a better vehicle for the same skills: leverage (people, capital, code, content) is output for input, and a switch is worth it only when the new vehicle gives far more leverage for the same skills, ideally in the same industry, and someone further ahead agrees; usually deepen the current vehicle, getting more from the leverage already in use (templates and one-to-many assets, O34) before adding kinds. People often quit an industry when they really quit a function: change role before changing industry. Be married to the game, not the vehicle, and keep the owner's identity separate from the business. Before adding any venture (after the first target), the core offer must pass an absence test: it runs without the owner for at least a month, six months preferred, on checklists alone (D5), passing only if the business holds steady or grows, with every hole logged; role-play likely problems with whoever covers first, and if the owner quietly does the work, the test has not happened. Write every new idea on one idea list, tell no one, and do not act on it; within 24 hours write it up on one page against seven questions (is it a good idea; does it feel right, a tie-break only, F20; what if wrong, M19; can a test be afforded; the main tasks; who does each, as action, owner and hours within F1; Plan B with stop-loss points), then park it. Set stop-loss points (the money, months or number that ends it) in writing before starting any project. Review the list only when the current constraint is solved or after the first target, and pick the single biggest; an idea that still looks good after a month earns a look. Before any switch, ask: if the owner did only this for 40 years, would it succeed, and if it made 10 times the money, would the owner stay? If yes, stay. On hitting the first target, spend at least the next quarter on more volume for the core offer (L62) before reviewing the idea list: the head start in reputation and proof is the moat (O2). If a shortcut looks easy, write what would need to be known to be sure before acting. After the first target, allow one genuinely new thing a year; before it, trying new things stays within L3(b). Write down what the business is a vehicle for, beyond money, and check hours against it each quarter. A later second offer line needs its own hours and owner. Before adding effort to any problem, first ask what to stop, cut or hand off. Each quarter, list every commitment outside the core offer that takes hours and pause, hand off or end each by a written date, or write why it stays and its end date (F3). Better beats new: output is volume times leverage. On the first business day of each month, list what the owner knows should be done for the core offer but is not, order it by output for the effort, and work down it before any new idea, tool or source, asking "if I were coaching me, what would I tell me to do?". A repeated mistake means the lesson was not learned: log it in F24 with the changed behaviour. Patience is a behaviour: not acting on the shiny thing while still wanting to. New ideas usually come from one of three places: a pain the owner overcame, a professional skill the owner can sell to several businesses at once, or a passion; prefer problems the owner has lived with for years, since the avatar's words and the failed alternatives are already known (L1, O5). What can be sold: products, services (the buyer saves the time to learn, the time to do, or both), software, access (physical, digital or media), risk and money; selling risk or money is regulated (X4). When the owner runs two businesses and the second splits attention, set a replacement test: could the better business replace the other's net income within a stated period (months, not years) with the owner's full attention? If yes, and the better one already converts and keeps clients, exit the other on a written plan, accepting a lower sale price for the focus it buys (M19, X20 on how a sale is taxed and structured), rather than waiting to polish it for the best valuation; if no, keep it and stop treating the second as a side venture. For each idea being weighed, write its kill shot: the one unknown that ends the idea if it can't be solved (a legal barrier, a delivery or shipping problem, a supplier), and work on that unknown first, before anything else about the idea. Don't pitch a new idea to the team on the day it arrives: put it on the idea list and revisit the list only once today's problems are solved; most ideas look weaker after a few weeks, and the ones that survive are the ones to bet on. An owner who naturally lives months ahead should set the share of time given to future projects against today's business, weighted towards today, and let the people running today's business say when the balance has slipped. A real no is to something the owner wants and could make work; an obvious no doesn't count. At each weekly review ask "what did we say no to this week that we wanted?". Before accepting any meeting, call, partnership or opportunity, ask how it changes the activities that reach the current goal; if it doesn't, decline, and offer only the owner's set slots to those who still want to meet. Returns concentrate at the top of a market, so the late, small improvements that only focus allows are often worth more than everything before them. Before committing years to any game (an award, a ranking, a platform metric, a market), ask who sets the scoring, whether the owner wants the prize taken to its extreme, and whether its price is worth it across the rest of life; staying in a game only because the owner knows how to win it is comfort, not strategy. An owner with an audience tends to add more products for the same buyers each time one sells; usually the real constraint is growing the audience, and money already spent on a new venture is no reason to keep spending (stop at the stop-loss).Before committing to a lease, build-out or other large fixed cost for a new site or area, test demand first: run the same small ad spend for the same offer in each candidate area and choose the one with the lowest cost per qualified lead; never take payment for a site, product or date that may not happen (X3). After choosing, pre-sell before opening (founding places, prepaid terms that start on opening day) so cash arrives before the costs start, with a refund date stated if opening slips. Only open the next site once the current one has grown for about six months without the owner (the absence test above); a one-month owner holiday shows what breaks.Never start a new venture or product line over the objection of the person who would run it: if they say there isn't capacity, the existing work will suffer; settle capacity first.Where money comes easily, look hard: when a side line or by-product (a financing arrangement, licensing the method, an older trade the owner still runs) earns far more per owner hour with far less effort than the main offer, test whether it is the real business before pushing the main one harder; the problem that makes it feel unscalable is often one the owner hasn't faced (hiring, managing), and the switch still follows the better-vehicle test above. Two more things can be sold: attention (physical or digital ad space in front of an audience) and an endorsement or brand (licensing a name, mark or status for a fee); each sellable thing has a physical and a digital form, and offers can combine several. A fourth source of ideas is what the owner absorbed from the work of family, people around them and past odd jobs. With very little cash, usually start by selling time as a service, to the person the owner can give the most value for the least cost. Before adding a product or menu item, check whether it will mostly take sales from the core offer rather than add new ones; if it does, the business does two things for the same money. The owner tires of an offer, message or campaign long before buyers have noticed it: don't change what works because the owner is bored of it, since buyers value getting the same thing they liked last time. Before building a new product line or upsell the owner is excited about, ask existing clients to choose between more or a bigger version of what they already buy and the new idea, and let the answer decide; owners tend to favour the new thing, clients more often want more of the current one. Before starting or buying into a second business, pass the owner test on the first: it has kept growing for six straight months with the owner spending under about five hours a week on it and never stepping back in to run it; until then, the first business is the one thing, and a new opportunity is pursued only by assembling a separate team for it. Grow by subtraction: at least each quarter, list and cut what drains resources without growing the core (people below the current bar, roles whose work automation has shrunk, unused supplier seats and subscriptions, meetings and threads with people who do not need them, side projects and extra revenue lines), since bloat after a growth spurt is normal and cutting early beats being forced to by the market (lawful process for people, X18); an extra revenue line is fine only if it adds no new workflow, team or way of finding customers. Owning shares in several businesses is not running them: the owner can be chief executive of one. Bigger goals make saying no easier, because fewer opportunities clear the bar. When a new business looks better mainly because the current one lacks a way to win clients directly (for example it relies on referrals from another firm), first try building a new acquisition channel in the current business. Deliver the first offer's promise before building the next products: feedback from the first delivery usually changes the others, and work on unlaunched products starves the one that is paid for. Script the no once and reuse it, so declining costs no thought: name the current priority and when the door reopens ("I've committed to [the main goal] until [date]; happy to reconnect then"), and give anyone who handles the owner's requests the same wording to decline on the owner's behalf.
- F23 [planning the week, or feeling short of time] Foundation: productivity is the money got out for the time put in, and there are two ways to invest time. Maker time is a few long, uninterrupted blocks (about four to six hours, usually two a day at most) for work that builds something new (the offer, scripts, outreach batches, content, systems, the product); one short meeting inside a maker block wastes the whole block, so for maker work an empty calendar is the most productive one. Manager time is many short slots for calls, coordination, decisions and training, where a full calendar is the productive one. Nearly all the work that moves the business forward is done in maker time, so plan it first and protect it: (1) at the start, with other commitments, take one maker block at the very start or end of the day and use weekend blocks too; (2) once there is some control, maker in the first half of the day and manager in the second, booking meetings from the end of the day backwards so the morning stays whole; (3) later, whole maker days and one or two stacked manager days. Offer meetings only in set meeting windows, with a few spare slots for genuine one-offs; tell people when replies will be slow and when they will be fast, then keep to both; if a meeting must break a maker block, turn that block into manager time and do every other pending call in it. A request for "five minutes" is answered with the next meeting window unless it is truly urgent. Work flat out in the maker blocks the owner has protected, or the protection isn't earned. Most gains come from stopping things, not adding routines: while one priority is set (F2, F30), run a season of no, where every other request, call, event and side project gets no by default until the priority is done. Count each no as a point scored for focus. Two things can't be equally important: time spent equally means neither is the priority. Having time to reply is not an obligation to reply; answer messages in set slots and only those that serve the priority or a client. Once the owner knows what needs doing, stop seeking more information (events, groups, courses, sources) and execute; return only when a specific gap blocks the work (F25). Keep the same sleep and wake times every day, weekends included, and give health and other areas their minimum effective dose during the season rather than progress on every front. Remove consumption apps from the phone and post, if at all, from a computer at a set time. Run a one-week time audit in 15-minute slots, then hand off or systemise the biggest block. Protect the outreach block first (L70). Set each task's deadline to the hours it really needs, not the next convenient slot. Do work early before known crunches (the owner's other commitments, the avatar's busy seasons): lead research, scripts, follow-up batches and tool fixes done ahead. In heavy seasons, cut non-core commitments to a small fixed minimum on set days, fix the environment for the outreach block, and before a hard push tell the people closest to the owner the plan, the hours and the end date. Send the 12-week input target in writing to one or two people ahead in the channel (F15) and report the real number to them each week. When a slump hits, keep going 15 more minutes before any break. Any 5-minute task is done now. Rest that protects output is part of the plan, within F1 hours. Run a visible timer in the outreach block with an estimate set first, pause it at every interruption, and log focused against planned minutes each week; under about 75% focused means fix the environment before adding hours. All notifications off except lead replies and booked calls (L72); everything else is pulled at set times. A pre-work routine stays only if output is higher on days it is done (touches before noon, L41); sleep stays protected. Buy back recurring personal chores only once the business earns more per hour than they cost (M19 buffer first), and put the freed hours into the outreach block, never past F1's cap. Start with the one-off, low-cost sleep basics (a dark, quiet room, no screens before bed), since better rest raises output per hour for every hour worked; then buy back chores in order of lowest cost per hour saved, one at a time, keeping each only if the logged hours actually move into business work. Use unavoidable travel or waiting time for small business tasks (replies, reviews, notes). At the start of each week delete every meeting that isn't needed; after every recurring meeting ask whether it needs to happen again and whether a person or a system could replace it; each quarter review all recurring meetings, counting a meeting's cost as its length times every attendee's hourly rate. With a team (S3+, T1): mark who does maker work, give them a weekly quiet day with no meetings or messages, ask each maker what their ideal week looks like and follow it as far as possible, treat a maker's no to a meeting as protecting the work, extend trust measured on output and deadlines rather than visible activity, say which hat (maker or manager) the owner is wearing that day, and keep makers out of meetings they don't need. Book call blocks and big decisions for well-rested days. After an upsetting call, a run of noes or bad news, make no price, discount, term or supplier decision until the next business day (M5, M36). Assign every business slot to a named job (making or managing), and keep an open-loops list. Put sales calls and client sessions into fixed call windows on several set days a week, around the owner's other commitments, so a same or next working day slot always exists (C3), while research, writing and build work keep whole half-day blocks with no calls; for group sessions, ask each client's named lead to send questions the day before. Copy work gets one or two fixed copy blocks a week, at the same desk with the same short start-up routine, outside and after the outreach block (L70): the only goal is the page (no checking replies, trackers or the bank balance), run with a ringing timer in cycles of about 30 minutes' work and 5 minutes' break, writing or doing nothing, with a pad nearby; rotating between 2 or 3 assets of the core offer is fine, never across ventures (F22). Cap claim and source research for copy (O47) at about an hour a session in its own block, and spread a big asset's research over several days (lead research keeps L8). Capture hooks and headlines away from the desk on a pad (L58). In outreach, copy and build blocks, the phone stays in another room with distracting apps blocked; check the inbox and calendar at the end of each block, at most every 50 minutes, so lead replies still go out within the hour (L13, L72). Set internal deadlines from the hours a task really takes, not from weekly cadences: ask "how many hours will this take?", set it due at that point today, then ask the same of the next task, and each time ask how it could take less (cut, automate, hand off). As an optional test, switch the phone to greyscale for two weeks and compare its screen-time report with the two weeks before; keep it only if the number falls. Match the work to the day's energy rather than skipping days: on high-energy days clear calls and do the deepest work (writing, building, scripting); on low-energy days move to calls, reviews and admin, so output never drops to zero. A simple split for a solo owner's working day: promote first (let people know, since no one can buy what they don't know about), then deliver what was sold, then build (find the next opportunities and pick the one with the highest return), sized to the owner's set hours (F1). Every meeting opens by naming the problem it solves and who is needed to solve it; anyone not needed for the decision leaves, and the meeting ends once it is decided, however long was booked. For a season of no, rank up to 25 goals, keep the top five, put the effort into the single most important one and write the minimum needed on the other four so they don't slip backwards; write a list of what will be given up for the goal, not only what will be done. Treat every interruption as costing more than its minutes: the preparation, the thinking afterwards and the switch back. Once a week, on the same day, run a review and preview: go through every meeting and call in the past week's calendar and write down any follow-up owed, then look two weeks ahead and write down the preparation each meeting, trip or deadline needs; those items become the next week's task list. At the same review, move any task untouched for several weeks to a named later quarter instead of carrying it forward each week, and delete it if nobody has asked about it by then. Start any task or project on the day it is set, even if only the first small piece, while the brief is fresh; a start the same day usually carries on further than planned, and a deadline weeks away is not a reason to wait. Aim for a rough first version of any new piece of work (a script, page, offer or memo) as fast as possible, then improve it from real reactions; many quick versions beat one long attempt at a perfect one. End each working day with two questions: what is there to show for today in results (sales, offers made, outreach sent, not meetings held or hours spent), and what one thing tomorrow would make it better than today; put that one thing first on tomorrow's list. Plan bigger projects in two-week blocks: when a new request arrives, give it a slot in the next block and a date, rather than squeezing it into the current one.

- F26 [thinking about leaving a job to run the business full time] Only once the owner has 3 to 6 months of savings and the business has matched the job's income for 6 months in a row (not one good month: small businesses swing). Leaving early means more work for less money for a long time. Until then, the job pays the owner to learn: use it to build skills the business needs, say which skills are wanted and ask about training, and when it stops teaching what the business needs, ask for a stretch project or a funded course. Keep living costs low so personal risk stays small and business risk can be bigger. Treat the business as the future main business, not side income: if 40 hours on it cannot beat 40 hours of the job, it is not working yet. Do not judge it, or listen to others judge it, while it is small. Inside the job, a move to a role with more responsibility usually pays more than asking for a raise.
