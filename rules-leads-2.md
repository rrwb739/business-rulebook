## Leads and marketing (L)

### Lead fundamentals
- L39 [any lead or advertising decision] A lead is anyone the business can contact. An engaged lead is someone who shows interest in what it sells (replies, takes the lead magnet, books a call): engaged leads are the real output of advertising. Advertising means making what the business sells known. Getting more customers needs leads that are more, better, cheaper and more reliable, from several sources. Not enough leads almost always means not advertising enough. Plenty of advertising forgives a weak offer and many mistakes, because it gives endless chances to get it right. The order is fixed: offer, then leads, then sales. The biggest competitor is being unknown, not other providers: the fix is more outreach, louder. Write one line for "call-ready" (fits L2 and has shown the need, for example asked about the problem, used the lead magnet with a qualifying result, or asked for a call), offer every call-ready lead a call within L13's window, and revise the line from close rates after L43's sample.
- L40 [choosing how to get leads] The Core Four are the only ways to let anyone know about anything: warm outreach (one to one, people who know the owner), posting content (one to many, people who know the owner), cold outreach (one to one, strangers), paid ads (one to many, strangers). Automation doesn't change the type. If short of leads, one of the Core Four isn't being done with enough skill or volume. Warm means people who have given permission to be contacted (know the owner, opted in, clients); cold means no permission yet, including bought or built lists. Usual order of adoption: warm outreach, content, cold outreach, paid ads; after warm outreach, with more time and money go to content, and with more money than time go to cold outreach or ads; skip a step in that order only for a reason written in the instructions box (L4). Master one before adding another (F3); once mastered they compound (money, systems and skills carry over, and content makes every other method work better).
- L41 [planning daily lead work] Rule of 100: at least 100 outreach actions every working day on the chosen channel (for content, 100 minutes a day; for ads, 100 minutes a day making ads), run for 100 days in a row, with calls counted separately. Where tools send cold email under L7, the owner may set a higher first-touch target in the instructions box and reach it by adding sending inboxes (L6). Write the daily budget per medium in the instructions box, and fill a combined email budget in this order: (a) replies, (b) final follow-ups due, (c) earlier follow-ups due, (d) first messages to new leads, which fill whatever is left; replies on a messaging app don't count towards that app's new-message quota. Log every send in the lead sheet the same day, and state in every daily report the sends by type, the messages for the owner to send by hand, and bounces. L12's quiet weeks still apply: first touches, final emails and follow-up calls move to the days just after, so only replies and early follow-ups go out. Follow-ups grow as leads move through their sequence, so once it settles new leads per day fall to about the budget divided by touches per lead; if follow-ups alone fill a budget, send no new first touches on that medium that day, carry the rest to the next day and tell the owner. For content, reply to at least 50 comments a day with the most useful reply on each thread, and release at least one piece a day. Any ad spend first passes M19's payback check and M26's runway, and content and ads stay parked until the first target unless they are the chosen channel (L4, F3, L61). Count in hundreds: never set a daily target below 100, never stop before 100 days. Alternative: "open to goal", working each day until a set outcome is hit (for example 2 engaged leads), however many actions it takes. The right action in the wrong amount still fails: most people do far less than the volume needed. Advertising is inputs and outputs: the owner only controls inputs, so keep them consistent before judging outputs (L17). Keep a daily scoreboard of inputs (touches, dials, pick-ups, calls past 2 minutes) and count every polite no as a point, so it rewards volume, not just outcomes. In heavy weeks (holidays, exams, peaks at other work), drop to a set minimum (for example 10 touches a day) rather than stopping, then return to the full budget: restarting costs more than the break saves. One slip doesn't lose the day: go back to the list at the next lead. Count new first touches as their own daily minimum, separate from follow-ups and replies, and flag any week they fall while total actions hold (F4); each week, compare cumulative touches with a straight line to the 12-week target (F11) and make up any shortfall over several days, never in one batch, so replies land when they can be answered.
- L42 [diagnosing poor results] Ask: do the engaged leads have the problem the business solves and the money to pay? No: an advertising problem (targeting or callout). Yes, but too few: an advertising problem (volume). Yes, but they don't buy: a sales problem. Never fix a sales problem with advertising changes, or the reverse. "Saturation" is almost always too little volume, too few methods and no tracking: there are far more leads than it feels like. If the messages reach the right buyers and still don't sell, look at the product as well as the copy: changing the campaign can't fix an offer that doesn't meet what buyers want.
- L43 [a test seems to have failed] Only judge a test on a sample big enough to give a readable signal: if the expected number of responses is one or two, the test proves nothing. For cold email, judge after at least 100 contacted (L16), and an offer after 100 to 300. Expect months, not weeks, to crack a new lead source, and longer to scale cold outreach. Expect the first messages and calls to be slow and clumsy; they speed up with repetition. What looks like volatility (a booked call one week, nothing the next) is usually low volume: work out how many days of outreach produce one sale, then aim to do that much outreach in one day, and treat the gap as the fix. Decide when to stop a test from the budget, time and need-to-believes set in advance (L62), not from frustration: persistence is built by sticking through repeated tries, and the only hard limit is not running out of money while trying (M26). Before dropping a channel, check whether the gap is skill with it (message format, timing) rather than the channel itself. Before calling a channel failed, run a checklist of every condition (list fit, deliverability, volume, follow-ups, offer, sample size): every one has to be met. Also check it was run at the owner's best (script as written, full prep, good-fit leads, a fresh block), so a no is a true negative; a test is not a rehearsal call (C13).

### Lead magnets
- L44 [creating or improving the lead magnet, or building any opt-in, booking or landing page] [Fixed] A lead magnet is a complete solution to a narrow problem that reveals the next problem, which the core offer solves. Its job is to capture contact details from people likely to buy: "book a call" or "get a quote" only works on people who already know the business, so for cold traffic offer the lead magnet instead. Lead with the core offer first; if prospects want to know more before buying (usual for expensive offers), lead with the lead magnet. Compare cash collected per 100 contacts for a direct ask for a call against the lead magnet first over 90 days before deciding which to lead with. Qualify on the way in: one qualifying question on the opt-in sends qualified prospects to a review call and the rest to the self-serve version; offer the review as a stated short call and keep to it. A diagnostic lead magnet can ask 3 to 5 questions and route each prospect to one short answer matched to its biggest issue (O5), ending in one recommended next step; build the rough version first (L45). Seven steps: (1) pick the narrow problem and who it's for; (2) decide how to solve it; (3) decide how to deliver it; (4) test the name; (5) make it easy to consume; (6) make it really good; (7) make it easy to say they want more. Three types: reveal a problem they don't know about (best when it gets worse over time; show the gap in their own numbers, very specific about the problem and vaguer about the result, O5); a sample or trial (full but brief access to the core offer, limited by uses, time or quantity, suiting a recurring problem); one step of a multi-step process, picked by delivery cost so giving it to every qualified prospect costs minutes, not hours (O34). The best combines all three. Never satisfy the need the core offer sells: sell at the point of greatest need. Four delivery methods: software (a tool or checker), information (guide, video, checklist), service (part of the work free), physical (an item only the avatar would want). Combine types and methods into several versions and rotate them; a stripped-down delivery checklist (D5) or, after the first measured pilot, a plain consented walkthrough of the real numbers (L33) are good sources. For a trial, state in writing at the start when it ends and what happens then, remind them a week before, and book the end call when they accept; a trial touching real client data needs a data processing agreement first (X5), and calling it free must meet X3. Every version must pass four tests: it engages ideal clients when they see it, gets more engagement than the core offer alone, is valuable enough that they use it, and makes the right buyers more likely to buy. A failed lead magnet means that one failed, not that lead magnets don't work. Show diagnostic results as one card per group with its count and one finding, biggest risk first, and label the categories and scores as the business's own method, never an official or industry standard (see X9). Pages: use one of three layouts (headline, subheadline and a short form; the same with an image; the same with an image and three bullets) and test it. The headline stops them and promises one clear outcome; the subheadline clarifies it; the image shows the result or a true detail, never decoration; the button says what they get; the form asks only what's needed for a qualified lead (over 5 fields, split into steps), with one line beside each field on how it helps them and optional fields marked; any bullets are three, answering the top three objections, most common first; genuine social proof only if needed, below the fold (M45); nothing else. Mobile-first, fast, one plain typeface readable on a phone. Include who the business is and how to contact it, a privacy notice, and separate marketing consent where needed (see X8). The razor for every element: if it doesn't raise the opt-in rate of qualified leads, cut it. Most gains sit above the fold: most weekly tests are a headline or image test, and if a new headline makes the old image wrong, test the pair together. Test every branch of an automated flow before it goes live. Headlines and first lines use two lines: the hook, then how it's doable for a buyer like them plus true proof (F21). Test three true fascination bullets, each answered where it says (O47), against the objection bullets. Offer buyers at the top of the avatar a call at first reply and send smaller ones to the lead magnet first, moving the line by close rate per type after L43's sample.
- L45 [presenting, naming or testing the lead magnet] The headline is most of the result: test headline, then image, then subheadline; small word changes can win big. The aim is a raised hand, not a sale. Test cheaply: polls, "reply 1 or 2", or asking people directly; if people ask when they can get it, it's a winner. Test whether a magnet is wanted with a rough first version before polishing it: simple and fast beats elaborate. Make it easy to consume in every format (works on phone and laptop, short video plus written version, booking at more times). In any poll or name test, count only answers from the avatar, combine two or three cheap tests for a rough answer, show every option as it would appear in real use (no "new" or "coming soon" label on one only), and judge a name on the quality of leads it brings, not just the number. A poll, name test or "would you use this?" answer stays provisional until a real send or opt-in confirms it: behaviour beats stated intent. The more experienced the audience, the more a live webinar reads as a pitch: prefer self-paced proof they can watch in their own time. Before sending a lead magnet widely, watch 3 to 5 avatar users use it cold, say nothing, log every stall, fix, and repeat. Test a curiosity-only headline against the one-clear-outcome headline (L44), and one open rally line that states a stance, not a result, against it; anything a reader could take as a promised result needs evidence first (O15; see X9). Webinars wait until after the first target: then turn the proven CLOSER call and pre-call video into one, in this order: hook, one modest promise (O15), teaching that breaks one belief at a time (C14), stack (O32), offer, calm close, and Q&A on real attendee questions only (M12), with price, terms and data answered straight (C5); any deadline true (O37 [Fixed]); judged on calls booked and sales from the avatar; its traffic counts as content or ads (L40, L60, L61). When opt-ins are low, first check whether the page gives the answer away, before changing the offer.
- L46 [deciding how good the free stuff is] Give away the secrets, sell the implementation. Free stuff costs time, so if it isn't worth their time they won't come back; if it was, the core offer will seem worth their money. Never give away weak fluff: the market judges the business on its free work, and most who never buy will still spread its reputation. Give the strongest hit first. Aim for the free value to exceed the price of the core offer before they buy. A delivery budget for free material lets the business out-give free competitors: work out what a free service costs per customer, (the owner's hours times the M1 hourly rate, plus any spend) times the number given away per sale, and keep it well under lifetime gross profit (M23). Judge free and paid material on value per minute, not volume: distil and cut so they get the result in less of their time. If a free piece feels too good, it's probably the right thing to give away, as long as it stops short of the core offer's outcome. Make its value visible with genuine reference prices only (X3), and never turn reciprocity into pressure (M27). Hold free assets and delivery to a standard above what the market asks (L50, F29): write any free guide as if it were the business's own internal working manual, to a standard a buyer would forward to a peer, and count forwards as a referral source (L14). Each free asset must save the reader time, money or risk the business has already spent, stated truthfully with no client confidential data (O11, X5), with a 3-line summary at the end; generic tip lists don't count. End each guide section and recorded lesson with 2 or 3 questions the reader answers in their own counts, the last leading to a named action (D14). Long bullet lists belong only in long-form assets people choose to read; opt-in pages keep three (L44). Say plainly that the free assets are free and are also how the business finds clients, so the later offer never feels like a trick; each must be worth having if they never buy. Send a free asset with a one-line personal note at the moment they get it (automated delivery says it is automated), and after delivering what was promised, add one unannounced, buyer-specific extra where there genuinely is one, information only (L4 [Fixed] on gifts).
