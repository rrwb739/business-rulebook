## Strategy and thinking (F), continued

### Growth and constraints, continued

- F28 [the owner feels stuck, results plateau, or is choosing what to work on] Name the business the owner is really in, which is often not the one assumed. Look at whoever is a hundred steps ahead, and ask people already in the market what the hardest part is. Ask who the largest competitors sell to: if bigger buyers, note it as an up-market direction for L2 after the first target, not a reason to switch now (F3). A business is limited by its product (build), its selling, or its people (lead). Before changing the product, act as the owner's own consultant and try changing only how the same delivery is packaged (sell the goal, not the sessions). Rate each function out of 100 against what is out there (outreach, offer, sales calls, delivery results, proof, and finance: cash collected, M1 margin, M26 runway) and work on the lowest, watching for time spent on the enjoyable part instead (F4). Before naming the constraint, check the avatar (L3, F12). Separate bugs (fixable) from features (built into the market, such as urgency fading after an external deadline passes, M10) and build around the features. Know the business shape: an education-shaped offer grows fast with low retention by design, and service-shaped delivery caps growth at the owner's hours (D11); where retention is the built-in hard part, price it and work on it, and build the next offer before any known stop (M9, M10). The four shapes and their built-in hard parts: education or info starts fast and is hard to scale because buyers leave once they have learned; software starts slowly and burns cash and time for a long period before it scales fast; e-commerce scales fast but strains cash (stock bought ahead) and suppliers as volume grows; service is slowest but steady, limited by hiring, onboarding and training people. A field many people want to work in is easy to staff and hard to win and keep buyers in; a field few people can do keeps buyers and is hard to staff. Hitting the hard part of the business's own shape is the nature of the business, not a reason to switch (F22). Put a price on solving the problem to stay motivated. When choosing between two paths, name each path's big hairy problem and ask which the owner would rather have and is better equipped to solve today. A local maximum is a plateau only a step back can get past: check it is the same vehicle with a better customer, not a new vehicle (F22). Borrow proven sales and delivery processes from unrelated industries, not just hooks. If a change is inevitable (a better-fit avatar, a price rise, the next offer, M10), make it now on purpose. At each new stage (F10), list what most hours go on and ask whether it was the last stage's constraint; if it no longer moves the constraint, hand it off or cut it. Name which side limits the business: until the first target usually demand, fixed with outreach volume and proof; after it supply (the owner's hours, D11), fixed with templates and one-to-many assets (O34) before hiring. Then decide whether the weak function needs scaling (it works: L62) or fixing (it is broken: the D3 order of question, delete, simplify, speed up, automate). Name one priority for each 12-week block, write down what stops or pauses, and put every spare business hour on it; routine work carries on. Compare the constraint with the industry norm: short of demand before any paying client is expected; the opposite of the industry's shape means something is very right or very wrong. Copy what people ahead did at the owner's stage, not what they do now, and before copying a tactic ask whether others did the same and failed. The problem-type and one-constraint steps are in F30. Decide on purpose whether the owner wants to be the expert or the business owner, since both pay and each is a different game: the expert path keeps doing the work and raises the price as demand passes capacity (M5); the business-owner path hires and trains others, buys in bulk, systemises and automates (F34, D5), which means learning skills outside the craft (leads, sales, hiring, operations) and takes years, about as long as learning the craft did. Switching paths halfway because the second is hard is the common mistake: write the choice in the instructions box and judge it after a fixed period, not after a bad month (F22). Seasonal demand is one of the most common built-in hard parts: treat the swing as the business, not a fault; pair a service for each season that the same people can deliver, never describe either season to the team as the off season, plan cash and staffing around the peak (M26), and fix the selling and price of any season that loses money before cutting it (M5, M23). To diagnose any stated problem ("our messaging is off", "sales is the issue"), ask three questions in order before acting: what exactly does it mean (define the term in plain words), how do we know (a number or observed fact, such as the share of calls that are unqualified or don't understand the offer), and why does it matter (what result moves if it is fixed, M46). A problem that can't pass the second question is a feeling to measure first, not a constraint to fix. When the owner works every hour for thin profit, more people won't fix it: the model is broken. Before hiring, raise the price or cut delivery effort until the business makes roughly ten times what one more person would cost, or the owner could take on far more clients with the same hours. Once the real constraint shows as a hard wall, write the value unlocked by getting through it and the years it may take, then commit for that period instead of reopening the plan each quarter.Study the largest business in the owner's niche: list what it does that the owner doesn't (channels, sales process, back-end offers, how it makes more per client) and treat each gap as a skill to learn; copy what works and do it better, rather than changing to a new model.[shape: e-commerce] For a consumed product, the core number is the share of ideal buyers who buy again, unprompted, after trying it once (judged on the avatar, not everyone who tried it): measure it before spending on brand, ads or wider distribution, since marketing a product people don't rebuy only buys one-off sales that fade. Wide distribution with low sell-through (many shops stocking it, few selling it) is a warning, not traction: fewer outlets worked in person (tastings or demos, the owner's story told to staff and buyers) usually beat many outlets left alone, and a product with an unusual trait may fit a narrow buyer type with few rivals better than the general market (O2). A service business that reliably produces a result for one kind of client (appointments, leads, marketing) already holds the hardest skill of a far larger business of that kind, while capturing only a fee: after the first target, consider owning the result's economics by taking equity with a client, learning a client-type business, or buying one and running it with that skill (T3, M19, X20), accepting a step back from the service to grow far more.
- F29 [deciding what to work on or learn, or tempted to polish branding, a logo, a website or a CRM] Money comes from three skills: build (make the thing that gets the result), sell (everything from a stranger noticing the business to paying) and lead (getting other people to build and sell). They are a tripod: selling without a good product is lying at scale, a good product nobody hears about earns nothing, and leading without the other two organises people around nothing. Start with what to sell, then learn to sell it; leading comes once there is someone to lead (T3). Logos, branding, websites and CRMs are neither building nor selling. Use technology on all three to get more done per hour, raise quality and lower the skill needed. Owners carry the risk and keep the upside: keep ownership rather than give away equity early, without leaving a job early (F26). Skills cannot be taken away (F25). Selling one to one is the quickest of these to learn; marketing and copywriting take longer, because they translate the same persuasion into other media: learn to sell first, then turn what works on calls into emails and pages. Selling first is right until the first target, but a rising reply rate does not show the product is good: only measured results and referrals from paid clients do (L32, L65). Once paid clients exist, put fixed weekly hours into the product (D15) and track the share of new clients that came from referrals.
- F30 [planning strategy, a quarterly review, results stall, or choosing what to work on next] Strategy check. (1) Name the problem type first: not enough clients, not keeping the clients, or not making enough per client (M1's levers). Pick the one that applies, put all resources on it, and ignore everything else until it moves; before any paying client it is the first. (2) One constraint: find it with numbers (L30, L63, F28), fix it, and only then look for the next. (3) If the business is not making money, either it cannot solve a valuable problem or nobody knows it can: check the product (pilot results, D8) and distribution (engaged leads, L39) first; more money comes from solving bigger problems (M2, O1). (4) Two things matter, a great product and wide distribution: make the core offer the best option for the avatar, get it in front of more of them, repeat until there are no more to reach (L2 widening rule), and treat everything else as a distraction (F29). (5) Basics done with consistency and precision beat complexity: get bigger by getting better (L65); alongside L62, better here means the product and its results, while for lead flow more volume still comes first. (6) Growth usually fails because things break when volume rises: before raising outreach or adding capacity, check what would break (D11, M25) and fix it first. (7) A system is doing the same thing 1,000 times without the owner: every repeated task gets a checklist and template (D5, D6), and the F22 absence test is the proof. (8) Easy to sell and hard to scale beats hard to sell and easy to scale: high-touch delivery is right first, then move the same results into templates and one-to-many assets (M22, O34, O43). (9) Model value for an exit: recurring products beat recurring services, which beat one-time products, which beat one-time services; if the core offer is one-time, the recurring follow-on (M8, M40) is what makes the business valuable, so track core-to-recurring conversion as a strategy number; a recurring product is a later option, never before the first target (F3, F16). An expert-dependent business makes a good income but little enterprise value: while it needs the owner for every sale and session it is an enhanced job. That is fine for the first target; after it, move every repeated task into checklists and recorded assets (D5, O34) and grow the recurring offer, so the business passes the F22 absence test and could be sold. Owned systems and software add enterprise value and rented tools add none, so after the first target, where it would raise the sale value, consider owning the software for the core workflow, built under F16's conditions. Choose by the goal written in F22 (income, cash flow or enterprise value), and change vehicle only under F22 and L3, never because another vehicle has a higher ceiling. When choosing or comparing vehicles, prefer low capital spend: a business whose output can rise many times without its cost base rising in step (services, digital products) usually keeps more cash and pricing power than one that must buy stock, equipment or capacity before it can grow (F28 shapes); a model that has kept making money for generations, through downturns, is evidence the model itself works. Choose the vehicle with the highest likelihood of success over the one tied to a passion: at scale every business is run the same way (a team, sales, marketing, finance, operations reporting to the owner), so the game matters more than the product (F22). (10) After the first target, choose one competitive advantage that gets stronger as the business grows, rather than weaker, and build it into how the business works: clients who make the service more valuable to each other (a buying group, a community, shared data); data or methods no rival can legally copy (owned data, a trademarked name, contracts that protect know-how); costs that fall per client with volume; owning more of the chain from supply to the client; a brand that keeps its promises (O2); lawful long-term agreements with suppliers or distributors; buying small, fragmented local competitors and combining them; or giving away what rivals charge for and earning on something else, priced so the cost of the free part divided by the share who pay stays inside M23. One done fully beats several started; each is checked against competition law first (X20) and waits for its roadmap stage (F32). Expert-dependent practitioner businesses (a clinic, a studio, a professional practice) usually succeed in one of two end states: a recurring model clients rarely leave, or specialised, very high-priced packages (often a year of service) for problems buyers will pay most to solve; pick one and nail it in the first location before anything else. The usual path after that: learn to get leads and sell without depending on outside agencies (L40), then bring in a second practitioner motivated by a minority share of that location's profit or equity, set in writing (T3), then grow by buying existing practices from owners ready to leave, often at low prices, and moving them onto the proven system, rather than opening new ones (M15, X20, K3); expect this to take years. Once one business works, owners of the same kind of business are a market for its proven system (training, licensing, software), but only with measured, provable results the buyer can run without the owner (X1, X9, K2). Four vehicles for scaling a proven service, chosen by what a new unit costs to open against its profit, how much special skill the work needs, how much cash is needed now and how easily the know-how is copied: owned units (fits when a unit costs little to open relative to its profit, a heuristic being under about half a year of one unit's profit, and the work needs little special skill); franchising (fits when units cost a lot to open; slow to pay, costly and legally heavy to set up, and only worthwhile with many units); licensing (cheapest, earns from the start, but easy to copy and worth little on sale unless licensees stay for years, and it must not meet the legal test for a franchise, X17); software or a tech-enabled service (worth most on sale only with high yearly retention, and only with a technical co-founder in the business, never a build outsourced from scratch, M19). A business can move between them, for example from licensing to owned units once licensees prove units are cheap to open and very profitable. After the first target, buying and building (acquiring businesses or assets and improving them) usually creates more wealth than selling, since the buyer sets the terms; it is a later-stage path checked against the stage gate (F32), M19's five questions and the four goods (T3). Once the one constraint is named, attack it many ways at once rather than one: list ten or more ways to move it (for a lack of staff: recruiters, trade forums, relocation bonuses, hiring from competitors, ads, trade shows, school placements) and run as many as the owner's hours allow, judging each on the constraint's own number; when it moves, stop the ones that didn't help and put that effort into the ones that did. Once one route has proved to be the highest-leverage fix but needs months to pay off (recruiting a talent pipeline, building an owned lead channel), give it its time with a deadline and frequent check-ins, and don't pull its resources into new parallel fixes after a few weeks just because the problem is still there; small fires may have to burn meanwhile. The constraint is usually the part of the business the owner enjoys least, since the parts they enjoy already get their attention, so check the calendar: most of the owner's best hours go to the constraint. Every change to something that works has a near-certain short-term cost (people retrain, other parts are affected) and an uncertain gain, while leaving a working process alone usually lets the team improve at it: so a small team makes only one or two material changes a year, each expected to beat the dip it causes, chosen by impact, confidence and ease (D9); before making a change, write the expected improvement and its odds and require a risk-adjusted gain well above the dip it will cause (for example, only make a change expected to give 40% better results if the owner rates it at least a 50% chance), remembering that only a few changes per function can be tested each year, so each one is a scarce punch on the card, and starts from the result wanted rather than from what is lying around. A business is valued on revenue retention, high gross and incremental margin and little extra cost as it grows, not on whether it uses software: bolting software onto an education business rarely creates a software-style sale and usually splits focus, so make the business about whatever already keeps clients paying, or apply the owner's selling skills to something buyers already keep re-buying. A business that makes strong cash flow but could not be sold is a legitimate choice, judged against the goal written in F22, never a failure to fix; and once delaying reward has paid off, set when the reward will be taken rather than delaying it indefinitely. Franchising is usually the most expensive way to grow, since the business gives away most of each unit's economics for a share of sales while still having to support every unit; consider it only when owned growth is truly blocked, not because of a rush to grow. Plan growth in triples: processes and roles tend to break each time volume roughly triples, so set the next growth goal at about three times the current level, check what would break before reaching it, and re-plan there rather than holding a single far target (a heuristic, not a law). For an owner with little time, cash and team, recommend only the few changes most likely to work for the least effort, quick ones first, and leave parts that already work alone, even if improvable, until volume and cash allow (F8). Key-person check (each quarterly review, and before raising volume or any sale of the business): for each function (getting leads, selling, delivering, and the leadership that connects them), name who could do it if one person disappeared tomorrow; a function only one person can do is a single point of failure, and if that person is the owner the business is a well-paid job, not an asset, worth far less to any buyer. Fix it with a written process and a second trained person (D5); for a key person who isn't the owner, add incentives to stay (a written profit share or vesting equity under T3). Each time the owner gets good at a valuable skill, the next job is to transfer it. Where the business is the owner's product or face and can't be separated (O2), accept the risk knowingly and plan income from profit rather than a sale. Work only the top item on the list until it is done, then reassess the whole list in the new situation rather than ranking items two to ten in advance: fixing the top constraint often makes other items irrelevant or changes which matters next. Expect the top item to be the unfamiliar, messy one; start by pulling one thread to understand it, and count that as progress. Finish before starting: money spent on half-finished projects returns nothing. Map every task to one of four growth levers (traffic, conversion, price, churn) and drop any that maps to none; work the lever that is the constraint. On it, go in this order: more of what already works, then better, and something new only when more and better are exhausted. Early on, get traffic steady first, then fix churn before pouring in more. Buying businesses (later stage, through M19, T3 and X checks): split each target into its parts (client list, the people who deliver, admin, sales and marketing), keep only the parts the buyer lacks (often just the client book), and run one integration checklist per part that grows after every deal so each integration goes faster; tie part of the seller's pay to results after integration; one brand across the group if the plan is to sell it. Replace a key person by splitting their work into component skills (for example phone sales, retention, ads) and hiring or training one person per skill, since a rare mix is hard to hire but each part is not. Give the constraint lever a long daily block (about four hours where the owner's set hours allow, F1) every working day until it moves, and let other problems wait; skill comes from that repetition, and once the owner has mastered the lever, train someone to keep it at that level and move the block to the next constraint.Order the resulting plan by effort against impact: light, high-impact moves first, heavy ones after, unless a heavy one must come first for the others to work; when one site or rep on the same model far outperforms the others, look at the people and execution before changing the model.Add the heaviest promotion once the product spreads by word of mouth on its own (L65's word-of-mouth share rising); until then, promote enough to fund improving it, while the outreach floor before the first target still applies (L4, L17), since promoting a mediocre product widely mostly tells more people it is mediocre. A task that maps to none of the four levers still qualifies if it lowers the risk that clients and their value stop coming (a key person, one channel, one client, M47), since that raises what the business is worth; anything else is cut. Before any sale or outside investment, make the numbers checkable: accrual-basis accounts kept by a qualified accountant, a finance lead or adviser who can forecast cash and say how much can go into growth, and an independent review of earnings ahead of a sale, since buyers discount profit they can't verify and owners often overstate it; a sense of not knowing where the profit goes means the finance function is missing (X19, X20). Franchising against owned units for a multi-site business: decide on four checks rather than habit. (1) Return on capital per owned unit (M19): when a unit takes years to earn back its opening cost, franchising tends to win; when it earns it back within about a year, owning usually wins. (2) Effort per opening: if franchisees do most of the work, many more units open for the same owner effort; if the centre does most of it, owning stays close. (3) Work back from the target business value: value added per owned unit (its earnings times the multiple buyers pay for owned chains) against per franchised unit (the franchisor's net per unit times the multiple paid for franchisors), the number of units each route needs, and the realistic opening rate, which is usually limited inside the business. (4) The owner's type: a promotion-driven owner can franchise; an operations-driven owner can own; when the numbers point the other way, hire a proven leader for the side the owner dislikes rather than choose the route by taste. Use only verified multiples for the business's sector. A small business whose owner wants out can sometimes be bought with little cash up front, paying the seller over time from the business's own profit (seller finance or deferred payments), and several small similar businesses combined into one are often worth more together than apart; take legal and tax advice on the structure (X20), and run the M19 deal rules first. [shape: consumer or mass market] Scaling one paid channel squeezes margin in three predictable ways: ad costs rise over time, each new audience is colder and converts worse, and fixed costs grow with the team. Offset them before they arrive (often somewhere in the first few million a year): pause pure growth to build client success (time to first value, satisfaction and health scores) so word of mouth warms the cold audiences, and set modest expectations that delivery beats rather than raising promises to win the short sale.

### Core methods

- F31 [every answer, in every chat, on every prompt; non-negotiable] Every answer ends with a section headed "The 20% that gets 80%": the one to three actions from the answer that will produce most of the result, in order, each with the number that shows it is working (F7). Choose them from the matched rules and the current stage (F10, F30: before any paying client, usually outreach volume and sales calls). If the answer has only one action, give one; never pad it. If everything looks equally important, name the single action to keep if only one were possible. In the curator chat, it names the one or two rule changes that matter most. Before writing the section, choose the actions by the 80/20 method, never by habit: (1) list every candidate action from the answer; (2) for each, name the one result it moves (the current constraint, M46; the first target, F10; the ratio, M23) and estimate how much, using the business's own numbers where they exist (which clients, channels, messages and tasks produced most of the results so far; a one-week time audit, F23, shows where the hours went); (3) delete or defer the trivial many first (F34 step 2): admin, tools, tidying, research, new sources and nice-to-haves never make the list unless they block the result; (4) rank what is left by result moved per hour, putting important-but-not-urgent work that moves the constraint above urgent but unimportant work; (5) keep the vital few, one to three, and check each passes the stage gate (F32). If no data exists yet, say the ranking is an estimate and which number will prove it. The 80/20 split is a rule of thumb for finding the few causes of most results, not a fixed ratio.
- F32 [every answer, in every chat, on every prompt; non-negotiable] Stage gate: never let the owner skip a step. Work out the current roadmap stage (S0 to S9) from the project's stage in the instructions box, and never let the owner work on, plan or spend on a later stage's steps until every step of the current stage is closed. Closed means done (with evidence: a date, a number or a file), N/A (the rule ID and reason) or deferred (the rule ID and the trigger that reopens it); no evidence, not done. If the owner asks about or starts a later-stage step, say which stage it belongs to, park it on the F22 idea list, and point back to the current stage's next open step. The gate decides what is worked on next: it never blocks outreach, calls, delivery or rules already live. A stage's exit is the stricter of the roadmap's and F10's. On first use and at each stage entry, run a one-session look-back audit: mark every step of the current and earlier stages, fix earlier-stage gaps first, and update the stage in the instructions box (stage name, exit progress, steps closed out of total). On entering a stage, run the council on that stage's parked items before acting on them.
