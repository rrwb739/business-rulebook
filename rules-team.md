## Team and leadership (T)

- T1 [hiring, recruiting or paying employees who get leads, sell or deliver] [stage: S3+] Not before the first target (T3). Hiring uses the same Core Four as leads (the owner's network, recruiting, job posts, promoted posts, staff referrals, staffing firms) and is a funnel: fix the worst step, and measure time to fill a role, cost to acquire a hire, and whether both sides rate the fit 10 out of 10 at day 90. Every hire is a certain cost and an uncertain return, so each must build (deliver), sell, or directly support one of those; hire only when D11 shows delivery capacity is the constraint (start recruiting deliverers before outreach outruns their hours), check margins can absorb a new hire's first 30 to 60 days at half output, compare the cost to acquire a hire with the gross profit they bring a year, and track revenue per head before and after each hire. Each month a key role stays empty costs a month of the gross profit it would bring, so paying more to fill it sooner is often cheaper. If cost per customer is within 3x the industry average, work on lifetime value; if more, fix advertising or sales. Measure cost per engaged lead as payroll divided by engaged leads. Pay level attracts recruits, pay tied to output changes effort, and fast feedback and training change how they work; the work itself motivates more than pay. Hiring: adverts state typical earnings, the hard demands and who it isn't for, in job terms only; describe the worst parts of the role plainly; test skill before interviewing; ask every candidate the same structured "tell me about a time" questions (one per value, one on a real failure), scored on an anchored written scale, no brain teasers; add a coachability role-play with a redo, a paid trial task for close-working roles, and referee rankings given with consent; judge only job-relevant evidence and observed behaviour, never demographics or general liking (see X18); screen for self-drive and respectful challenge; screen out the sure fails and train the unsure middle; hire only someone who would raise the current average on the same test and who respects the skill they'd be led with; give each hire a bar raiser outside the hiring line (for the first hire, someone ahead of the owner, F15) whose veto stands; keep a short file on what strong and weak hires looked like and update the questions from it. For a role the owner doesn't know, interview widely first as learning (F25). In any function, a candidate's skill shows in how many and how good the numbers are that they track for their own area: ask what they measure and why, and prefer the one who names measures the owner has not heard of. Don't settle out of urgency or hire friends or family to scale fast; one failed hire proves the person wrong, never the role. Hire broadly at entry level; for senior roles, hire someone the owner learns from in the interview, found by asking many contacts "do you know anyone like you?", pitched like an offer with time to decide and no exploding offers, with stronger incentives such as a stake; make the first senior hire an operator whose own mission fits the business's. For a first marketing lead, look at owners of small agencies who have won clients without a brand behind them: they tend to love the work, can run a small team, and often earn less from their own agency than a salaried role would pay. Look first at engaged opted-in contacts; never approach a client's staff. Grow talent from paid junior roles, promote proven people first, and offer progression reviews at 12 to 18 months, never forced exits. Sales team: hire setters first while the owner keeps closing (F13), each role (research and outreach, reply handling, closing, client success) on its own script, scoreboard and pay (L30, L54), with a written setter-to-closer handoff the closer reads back (C4); hire the sales leader before more closers, one who sells and closes before hiring or training; judge candidates by the output metrics they name and how those make money (a role that can't name its output can't drive it); give every role a pair of numbers reported together, one for volume or speed and one for quality that guards against pushing the first too hard (sales against refunds and cancellations, tickets closed against satisfaction, leads against qualified leads, stock held against stockouts, edits completed against corrections); certify new callers (a written check on X2, X4 and C2, plus a role-play scored on the C13 rubric by someone other than the hiring manager) before calls. Keep total sales pay under about 10% of revenue when leads are supplied (more for self-generating reps); pay only on results the person controls that predict client success, simple, paid soon and explained before any change, never mainly for recruiting (L69); back-test any pay plan on last month's real numbers and at higher output; one contest at a time on one behaviour; run sales contests over a fixed cycle of about six weeks, long enough to reach the goal and short enough to feel near (the owner's experience, not a tested figure), in small mixed teams that pair a stronger seller with a weaker one so the stronger one helps; give sellers a visible path with small pay steps at closed-sale milestones (for example a small rise in pay per sale at each set number of sales), within X18. Build each sales role (first touch, qualifier, closer) as a ladder of about four titled levels, each reached by an objective count (shows set, qualified shows, sales closed) paced at about eight weeks of normal output, each with a title step and a small, mostly symbolic pay step; keep the ladder visible to everyone, drop a person back a level when they fall below that level's threshold, and make reaching the top level earn the chance of promotion, not promotion itself: when a seat opens, promote the best fit from those eligible onto a short trial with a fixed number of chances (for example ten sales opportunities), and return them to their level if they close none. Advertise the role on the earnings of the top of the ladder and how long the climb takes, stated truthfully (X9). For a first-touch calling role that takes a day or two to learn and costs little per lead, screen lightly and let results decide; book cold callers in half-day call blocks, since few people dial well for a full day. All pay, contract, capability, redundancy, working-time, status and reward matters follow the law, with advice first (see X18, X19). Recruiting is selling: strong candidates have options, so sell the role, the people and the path as much as they prove themselves, and move every stage fast, measuring days from first contact to offer, since top candidates leave the market quickly. People are driven mainly by one of lifestyle (what they can buy and do), purpose (the difference the work makes), the next step (being told the next few moves) or competition (a record to beat, an opponent, an injustice to correct): ask each person which, and speak to that one. For these leader roles, hire only someone the owner judges clearly exceptional by the end of the process (a candidate who seems merely good rarely becomes exceptional later, while the reverse error is common), then give them real authority over their decisions and a share of the profit they drive, with a way to buy into their part of the business over time (T3). Change pay to solve a named problem: the current offer no longer attracts good candidates, people are leaving for better offers, or a proven top performer asks for more and losing them would cost more profit than the rise (work the loss out in numbers); a market pay review with no such problem only adds cost that can't be taken back. Pay law still applies: differences between people doing equal work need a lawful, non-discriminatory reason, and some employers must report pay gaps, in the project's country (X18, confirm with a qualified adviser). Before approving a request for a new hire, ask the team lead for the team's current hours by task and whether the work can be cut, simplified or redistributed first. Every hire must raise the average of the team they join, not just meet the requirements: interview 10 to 20 people for a key role, and hire for a role above the owner's own skill only someone the owner learns from in the interview. Tie anyone's sales commission to clients who stay and are happy, not just to the signature: hold back or claw back commission on refunds and early cancellations within a set window, pay it only on cash actually received, never on signed sales whose money has not arrived, and include a client-satisfaction check after onboarding, so no one gains by overselling (X9). For sellers, add a shared team bonus paid when the whole team clears a set level, alongside individual pay, so the team helps its weakest member rather than competing against each other. The first three to five sales hires set the team's culture, so hire them with the most care, and hire sellers in small batches where possible, so a problem shared by all points to the script or the leads and a problem with one points to the person. Pay must still work when the business is full: no revenue or profit share to every person that leaves the delivery unit unprofitable, and at scale no seller should earn more than the business makes from their work. If pay was set wrong, fix it rather than wait: tell the team the truth (the owner set it wrong, and what growth it blocks for them), show the owner is taking a cut too, give the new structure and a dated review, and change any contract terms only lawfully, with advice first (X18); accept that some will leave and recruit to the new structure. To grow past the owner, accept a short-term profit cut to hire leaders better than anyone the business has had, and keep raising the level of each new hire; the vision must be big enough for their own goals to fit inside it. When handing off the role the owner does now, also name the role the owner would grow into next and hire someone who has already done it, instead of learning it first; learn just enough about each role to judge candidates, and treat an interview round that taught the owner nothing about the role as a sign to hire none of them and widen the search. Where good local candidates are scarce and one more worker earns far more than they cost, advertise the role nationally with a written relocation or signing package, part paid on joining and part after a set period (X18). Keep cold outbound sellers and inbound sellers as separate teams: inbound leads cost money, so they go only to sellers who have proved they close, as a promotion, on their own commission plan (more volume, lower pay per sale, steadier); while inbound is new, give it to one proven seller so the offer, lead source and process are the only variables, and keep everyone else on outbound. For a key specialist role, look at the businesses whose work in that area the owner admires, find who does it there (professional profiles, credits, their own posts) and approach them directly with a better offer; keep work core to creating value for clients (attracting, converting, delivering, and the brand) in house, and outsource only supporting functions (T2). Judge candidates more on how fast they learn than on where they start. Group interviews save time for front-line roles; for leadership roles, a candidate the owner learns nothing from in the interview will need teaching instead. For commission-led field or sales roles, write the job advert like a sales ad (the pains of the candidate's current job, the earnings range real sellers reach, the hours, the hard parts stated plainly), screen in short group calls, meet the promising ones in person and make offers there, then have each new seller shadow, then sell while watched; the owner never sells alone, so someone is always learning. Pay must still meet X18 (status checked, minimum wage for workers). Judge drive by work history and behaviour in the trial, never by family status, pregnancy or other protected characteristics (X18, X23). For staff who could leave and take clients, set a written career path: a title and pay step every three to four months over about two years, each step unlocking a new skill the business can charge more for, and a competed-for route to run a new location on a profit-share bonus that rises with tenure (a bonus, not equity, so it can end if the role isn't done). Paying for a top-decile person in a role usually costs a fraction more than the median and returns several times the output, so budget to hire near the top rather than the middle. In interviews, ask a few ways what the candidate does in their own time and what they are obsessed with: someone whose spare time goes into the role's skill will take feedback and keep improving. Hire junior roles for intelligence and work ethic over experience, senior roles for proven results. In the stage where the business needs a senior hire but the hire would cost a large share of profit (often the first few million of revenue), one miss can wipe out a year's progress: raise profit first with more of the owner's own unscalable work or a price rise, so the business can afford two or three attempts at the role, and expect the first hire may not work. To test a hire that frees the owner's time, divide the hire's full yearly cost by the business's margin to get the extra revenue the freed hours must bring in to break even, and hire only if the owner can name how those hours will produce it. Decide in person or remote by role: remote suits defined tasks, while roles where the business wants more than the task done, and people early in their careers, gain from in-person work through unplanned learning across functions, faster trust and feedback; a leader who walks the floor and makes brief personal contact picks up problems early. Whichever is chosen, say it before hiring. For roles that need judgement from experience, consider people near the end of their careers (including the recently retired) alongside early-career people with energy, since both tend to care about the work more than the title. Before any hire, work out the revenue it must add to pay for itself: its full cost divided by the business's net margin (at a 25% margin, a hire costing a given sum must add four times that sum in revenue), and name the activity that will produce it; when that first senior hire would take a third to half of a year's profit, fund the bet by keeping the reserve (M26) and doing more high-margin work the owner can deliver for a period, rather than staying stuck by waiting until the hire feels safe. Write the job post as an offer (headline, pay, benefits, hours, location, expectations) and match the experience asked for to the pay: underpaying attracts weaker people or people with second jobs. Never give staff a share of revenue for work whose costs they don't carry; share profit they control instead (T3). To change pay or structure, test the new terms on new hires first; if they attract people who meet the standard, use that as the precedent for existing staff, with notice (about 3 to 6 months), a plan showing how they could keep their current earnings, and the law followed (X18). When a team is weak, add stronger people at better terms alongside it rather than replacing everyone at once, accepting a short period of paying both. Hire through the same funnel as clients: attract applications, nurture them, treat the interview as a sale, onboard, then retain or promote. The best candidates have options, so sell them the opportunity, with a vision big enough for their own goals to fit inside it, rather than making them sell themselves. Before hiring a role the owner has never filled well, learn what good looks like in it by talking to strong people who do it; for a role the business can't succeed without, it is worth delaying the start rather than hiring the wrong person. Pair every output measure with a quality measure so neither is gamed: pay or bonus per completed job with free redos when the work falls short of the written standard, support judged on both resolution and speed (always at least minimum wage for the hours worked, X18). Sell the business to candidates as a product: a short recruiting video, sent before any interview, that shows how the business makes money, why it exists, and the path a strong performer can take (including leading a new line the business backs later). Before any hire, list the 5 to 10 tasks the role would actually do (with hands, eyes and voice) and turn each that can be a written workflow into an automated or AI-assisted one (F34) before adding a person; plan the work as inputs to outputs, not as an org chart. When the owner wants one hire to take over everything else they do, group the tasks by the skill each needs and hire for each group (often two or three narrower roles) rather than searching for one person who has every skill. Run sales hiring as a funnel parallel to the client funnel (attract, screen, interview, onboard, keep and promote), with its own numbers, and judge a seller hire on lifetime gross profit per seller against total cost to hire and pay them.Many basic behaviour rules (be on time, no drinking at work, no streaming during client calls) signal a hiring bar that is too low: raise the bar rather than add rules. Say truthfully what the work achieves for clients beyond money: a real mission draws stronger candidates.To raise the quality of anyone's work, ask after each draft: "if you had another two hours on this, what would you do?", have them do it, and repeat; then ask what they would do with a week, which often shows the better approach that is worth the time.Effort beyond the minimum comes from reward, not fear: punishment buys only enough work to avoid it, fades when the punisher is absent and has to keep escalating, while specific praise keeps working. Pay client-success staff a variable bonus on the leading measures that keep clients (share reaching the activation point, completion, retention, ascension), scaled by how far into the target range they land, alongside base pay that meets X18; once those measures are tracked per person, the same numbers show who needs coaching. When the business can afford it, hire experienced leaders from the top down so they teach the owner; every growing business carries some debt (financial, management, cultural or technical) and should choose on purpose which one to take on. Speed of growth depends on knowing what excellent looks like in each role and the order to hire them in: before hiring a department head, get the requirements from someone who has built and run that department several times, not someone a step ahead whose last hire may not have lasted. Strong hires usually show it within their first days or first cycle (C13); one who doesn't rarely becomes one, so a mis-hire costs the hiring time, the time to find out and the growth that role would have produced. In interviews, how a candidate answers matters more than what they answer, so ask questions that show character under pressure, honesty with money and taking feedback; a high performer with a large ego who won't take feedback damages the team more than they add. Run hiring as a funnel with the same care as sales: applications per month, the share booked for interview, interviews that become hires, onboarding, training, the career path and staff retention; size weekly interviews to cover front-line turnover plus the growth target, since a hiring plan that only replaces leavers never grows the team. Within that funnel, contact applicants within a day, since strong candidates are often hired elsewhere within days, and give the interviewer a written script; aim for the business to become a name that marks a strong candidate's CV because of the training it gives. When the step that limits hiring is interview time for a high-volume front-line role, run short group interviews and pick from them, rather than adding more one-to-one rounds. For roles not paid on sales: for leaders, split variable pay between company results and objectives within their own control (for example half each), so they still earn part of it when the business has a flat period; for front-line roles, an option to test is a small share of target pay (about 10 to 20%) paid each month against a goal the person sets themselves, measurable, shown to the team and tied to the department's goal, which adds autonomy and peer pressure to set a fair goal. Any variable pay sits on top of a contractual base that never falls below the minimum wage, and changing existing pay into variable pay needs the person's written agreement (X18). Managing people once hired (standards, feedback, check-ins, culture, structure, reporting) is T6.
