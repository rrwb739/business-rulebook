## Team and leadership (T)

- T1 [hiring or managing employees who get leads, sell or deliver] [stage: S3+] Not before the first target (T3). Hiring uses the same Core Four as leads (the owner's network, recruiting, job posts, promoted posts, staff referrals, staffing firms) and is a funnel: fix the worst step, and measure time to fill a role, cost to acquire a hire, and whether both sides rate the fit 10 out of 10 at day 90. Every hire is a certain cost and an uncertain return, so each must build (deliver), sell, or directly support one of those; hire only when D11 shows delivery capacity is the constraint (start recruiting deliverers before outreach outruns their hours), check margins can absorb a new hire's first 30 to 60 days at half output, compare the cost to acquire a hire with the gross profit they bring a year, and track revenue per head before and after each hire. Each month a key role stays empty costs a month of the gross profit it would bring, so paying more to fill it sooner is often cheaper. If cost per customer is within 3x the industry average, work on lifetime value; if more, fix advertising or sales. Measure cost per engaged lead as payroll divided by engaged leads. Pay level attracts recruits, pay tied to output changes effort, and fast feedback and training change how they work; the work itself motivates more than pay. Managing beats doing: replace the owner in each role as soon as possible, then hand the managing to a manager. Weak staff are the fault of whoever hired and trained them: train with document, demonstrate, duplicate (D5), give new starters a reduced workload until quality measures are met, tell them how corrections will come (D13), and if a trained person is still below standard after about three weeks, act through the fair process (see X18). Before the first hire, write how the business behaves with clients (culture is the internal brand), capped at three values, each with concrete behaviours and what gets praised and corrected, including "invite disagreement, keep it about the work, then commit once it's decided"; the owner models it first and every manager reinforces the same things. Hiring: adverts state typical earnings, the hard demands and who it isn't for, in job terms only; describe the worst parts of the role plainly; test skill before interviewing; ask every candidate the same structured "tell me about a time" questions (one per value, one on a real failure), scored on an anchored written scale, no brain teasers; add a coachability role-play with a redo, a paid trial task for close-working roles, and referee rankings given with consent; judge only job-relevant evidence and observed behaviour, never demographics or general liking (see X18); screen for self-drive and respectful challenge; screen out the sure fails and train the unsure middle; hire only someone who would raise the current average on the same test and who respects the skill they'd be led with; give each hire a bar raiser outside the hiring line (for the first hire, someone ahead of the owner, F15) whose veto stands; keep a short file on what strong and weak hires looked like and update the questions from it. For a role the owner doesn't know, interview widely first as learning (F25). In any function, a candidate's skill shows in how many and how good the numbers are that they track for their own area: ask what they measure and why, and prefer the one who names measures the owner has not heard of. Don't settle out of urgency or hire friends or family to scale fast; one failed hire proves the person wrong, never the role. Hire broadly at entry level; for senior roles, hire someone the owner learns from in the interview, found by asking many contacts "do you know anyone like you?", pitched like an offer with time to decide and no exploding offers, with stronger incentives such as a stake; make the first senior hire an operator whose own mission fits the business's. Look first at engaged opted-in contacts; never approach a client's staff. Grow talent from paid junior roles, promote proven people first, and offer progression reviews at 12 to 18 months, never forced exits. Sales team: hire setters first while the owner keeps closing (F13), each role (research and outreach, reply handling, closing, client success) on its own script, scoreboard and pay (L30, L54), with a written setter-to-closer handoff the closer reads back (C4); hire the sales leader before more closers, one who sells and closes before hiring or training; judge candidates by the output metrics they name and how those make money (a role that can't name its output can't drive it); certify new callers (a written check on X2, X4 and C2, plus a role-play scored on the C13 rubric by someone other than the hiring manager) before calls. Keep total sales pay under about 10% of revenue when leads are supplied (more for self-generating reps); pay only on results the person controls that predict client success, simple, paid soon and explained before any change, never mainly for recruiting (L69); back-test any pay plan on last month's real numbers and at higher output; one contest at a time on one behaviour. Choose leaders on written criteria (consistent results, every C5 step certified, mentoring shown, never tenure alone), train them, let them manage one starter first, and have no permanent player-manager roles; managers still take some client calls each month. Run a daily, weekly and one-to-one rhythm with a short daily role-play, give lead-quality feedback one to one as data, and mystery-shop anyone who sells against a checklist of each C5 step. Standards: the weakest performer kept in a function is the standard signed off for it, so judge on output after training and use the fair capability process rather than leave a weak performer; a recurring problem under the same person is a who problem; whoever holds the highest standard for a function decides it; each quarter, ask whether team quality has risen. Before each headcount jump, list which checklists, reports and decision rights change (D5) and expect a dip; when politics or bottlenecks appear, first write down who decides each function and check whether pay rewards a count over the outcome (M38). Each role has a one-page decision list (decide alone, decide and tell, bring to the owner). Staff get least-access views and no client data (X5); monthly numbers are shared with staff, never with variable-fee suppliers (T2). Keep politics and needless meetings away from top performers; deep-work roles get a short daily huddle only. Never script forced cheerfulness; after a hard push, tell people to rest. Managers own their slips (L75). Every exit names one specific thing the person did well; if a role ends through the owner's mistake, say so and leave the door open. All pay, contract, capability, redundancy, working-time, status and reward matters follow the law, with advice first (see X18, X19). Recruiting is selling: strong candidates have options, so sell the role, the people and the path as much as they prove themselves, and move every stage fast, measuring days from first contact to offer, since top candidates leave the market quickly. New hires get a short check-in at the start and end of each day for their first two weeks and shadow other functions. Write each role's path as behaviours: people move up by already doing the next role's work, and having no next step is a common reason good people leave. People are driven mainly by one of lifestyle (what they can buy and do), purpose (the difference the work makes), the next step (being told the next few moves) or competition (a record to beat, an opponent, an injustice to correct): ask each person which, and speak to that one. Know where the problems are: the owner keeps a standing, one-level-deeper view of each area's current biggest problem and its largest recurring costs (by reading the numbers and asking about the work, not only "how is it going?"), and company-wide pay changes, and any recurring outside cost above the F2 spend limit, need the owner's written sign-off; delegating a function (compliance, HR, finance) still means checking what it costs and what it produces each month. Getting clients is one job: put advertising and sales under one owner (the founder until a head of revenue is hired), since sales only fills the gaps the advertising left, so the two are judged on clients won, never against each other. A growing business needs three leaders: one for getting clients, one for delivering the result and one for running the operation (finance, legal, HR, systems), the last serving the other two rather than setting direction. Every hire must raise the average of the team they join, not just meet the requirements: interview 10 to 20 people for a key role, and hire for a role above the owner's own skill only someone the owner learns from in the interview. When someone isn't doing what was asked, check five reasons in order and fix the reason, not the person: they didn't know it was wanted (say it in writing), didn't know how (train, D5), didn't know by when (set a date), didn't want to (reward, praise, autonomy, training in their skills), or something blocks them (remove it). Tie anyone's sales commission to clients who stay and are happy, not just to the signature: hold back or claw back commission on refunds and early cancellations within a set window, and include a client-satisfaction check after onboarding, so no one gains by overselling (X9). For sellers, add a shared team bonus paid when the whole team clears a set level, alongside individual pay, so the team helps its weakest member rather than competing against each other. The first three to five sales hires set the team's culture, so hire them with the most care, and hire sellers in small batches where possible, so a problem shared by all points to the script or the leads and a problem with one points to the person. Pay must still work when the business is full: no revenue or profit share to every person that leaves the delivery unit unprofitable, and at scale no seller should earn more than the business makes from their work. If pay was set wrong, fix it rather than wait: tell the team the truth (the owner set it wrong, and what growth it blocks for them), show the owner is taking a cut too, give the new structure and a dated review, and change any contract terms only lawfully, with advice first (X18); accept that some will leave and recruit to the new structure.
- T2 [considering an agency, or paying any outside supplier] Pay for the output, not the hours: a supplier billing by the hour has a reason to bill more. Outsource a task only if the owner's freed hours, spent on higher-value work, beat the fee plus the cost of any drop in quality (checked on a sample), and not without money to spare. Before any supplier starts, agree one written page: who is involved, who does what, where, by when, how much, for how long, who signs off the work as good, and a proportionate remedy agreed up front if it isn't good or is late (X3); it's for clarity, not suing, so decide what the owner wants first and say it plainly, even to a friend. Keep ownership of the work and the data, keep someone on the owner's side who can judge the work, and never share revenue figures a variable-fee supplier could price against. Never give an open-ended revenue share: cap it with a written review date (affiliate payouts sit under L69). Give each supplier their own login with the least access needed, never the owner's password, removed the day they stop (L36); a supplier handling personal data needs a written contract and never sees client data (X5; see X8). Check employment status before paying any contractor regularly (see X18). Choosing: test skill before interviewing (every candidate gets the same sample task, criteria and deadline, scored on accuracy); for a specialty the owner can't judge, first interview 3 to 5 people who do that job about what good work looks like (F25); pick agencies on referrals, known clients, a waiting list, an honest sales process, long-term strategy, clear requirements, regular meetings, simple reporting and a strong offer, talk to 5 to 10 first, and ask those not picked to refer a good one; ask referees to rank accuracy, speed, communication and reliability and say why; treat hand-picked testimonials as unverified until checked with a client the owner finds; before acting on advice, ask whether they are truly expert on this exact topic and what they gain. When hiring an assistant, screen for reply speed and intelligence and pay above the going rate (T1). Give no exclusivity until a supplier has beaten an alternative on a live batch. For recurring work, agree a written standard, keep a scorecard from day one and give feedback within 2 working days; if a supplier won't meet it after one written request, switch, using the L36 backup and checking notice terms first. Agencies: use them to learn a method or platform, not forever, and name the senior person in the contract. The cheapest route is paying an expert by the hour to watch the owner do the work. Otherwise say up front the work will move in-house, pay for every decision to be explained, set a deadline (about 6 months), run the owner's team alongside until it wins, then drop to cheaper consulting, and end it once the owner is teaching them. Deciding: judge a reduced proposal fresh against the need, budget and payback written beforehand (M19), never against the first number; walk away from a last-minute take-it-or-leave-it demand rather than accept because of time spent; above a spend threshold the owner sets, read the proposal in a distraction-free block and get a view from someone who didn't see the earlier offers (F15); value extras at what they'd cost alone and compare totals in writing. Decline gifts or favours that would create a sense of owing and log any accepted; treat a free trial or "quick call" as the first step of a ladder and write down the bigger ask it leads to; repay favours in proportion, and never turn a small favour into a much bigger ask (M27). If a pitch makes the owner feel trapped by something said earlier, stop and decide later on the F2 test; if unusually charmed, decide only on the written offer, evidence and references (F25); anyone who keeps coming back for small concessions is a red flag. When work slips, put it in writing (thanks for one specific thing, then the specific extra item with its time and pay stated); if they fail outright, go to the decision maker, frame the fault as a risk to their reputation and ask for the fix, never shouting. Praise helpful support staff to their manager when sincere. After a bad supplier or course, write what was learned before judging it. Pay suppliers on time, every time. Before relying on a supplier for anything a launch or the core service depends on: get references from clients like the owner, at the owner's size and use case, and call them; a referral from a friend never skips the checks, so say plainly that the call will be treated as if the owner doesn't know them; ask for a test at the expected load before the date; and treat any doubt from the supplier's own staff as a reason to check again or switch, since bad news before a launch is cheaper than after. Never outsource the core of what the business sells (getting clients or delivering the result) to a supplier no one on the owner's side can judge (T3): where software is built outside, the business owns the code and accounts, the contract requires documentation and a handover, and fee rises are capped and agreed in writing.
- T3 [considering a partner or a first hire] [stage: S3+] Consider a partner or first hire only when the roadmap stage allows it (after the first target, F32), never before. A partner must bring money, experience or time the owner lacks, written down and measurable before any equity moves; never partner out of fear of going alone. Before any equity moves, agree in writing, with a solicitor, what happens on exit, death, disagreements about money and a sale. To make a partner or senior operator act like an owner, first consider a written share of the profit they control (with an optional small, symbolic buy-in to show commitment) before any equity: it is faster, simpler to unwind and gets most of the same behaviour; move equity only where the measurable value justifies it, with advice first (see X17, X18, X19). If the product's core is technical work the owner cannot judge, the first partner or senior hire should be someone who can, with a real stake in the quality of who is hired; until then, have a trusted outside expert interview or test candidates. Split equity by each person's measured contribution of time, money and skill, never equally by headcount; equal skills and equal contributions mean one of the two isn't needed. Have the split conversation on day one, before any work: if a partner balks at a fair split then, that is the answer. Whoever brings in the clients and makes the money gets a share in line with that, because the business can't run without them. A function the business needs (accounts, legal, IT, admin) is hired or bought from a supplier (T2), never paid in equity; equity goes only to people core to getting clients or delivering the result (one making the product exceptional and one telling the world about it is a sound split). A partner who works in the business is paid a salary for the job, separately from what they own as a partner. Never sell a share for less than its value as a growing business (advice first, X17). Red flags in a partner mean stop, believed the first time: a fraud or insolvency history (check public records), asking the owner to personally guarantee their debts or leases, to front all the money, or to do all the work for half, and the pitch "you're leaving money on the table". Never personally guarantee a partner's obligations without advice (X17); keep shared money in an account that needs both signatures for any large withdrawal, with books both can see. Hiring follows T1.
