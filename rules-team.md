## Team and leadership (T)

- T1 [hiring or managing employees who get leads, sell or deliver] [stage: S3+] Not before the first target (T3). Hiring uses the same Core Four as leads (the owner's network, recruiting, job posts, promoted posts, staff referrals, staffing firms) and is a funnel: fix the worst step, and measure time to fill a role, cost to acquire a hire, and whether both sides rate the fit 10 out of 10 at day 90. Every hire is a certain cost and an uncertain return, so each must build (deliver), sell, or directly support one of those; hire only when D11 shows delivery capacity is the constraint (start recruiting deliverers before outreach outruns their hours), check margins can absorb a new hire's first 30 to 60 days at half output, compare the cost to acquire a hire with the gross profit they bring a year, and track revenue per head before and after each hire. Each month a key role stays empty costs a month of the gross profit it would bring, so paying more to fill it sooner is often cheaper. If cost per customer is within 3x the industry average, work on lifetime value; if more, fix advertising or sales. Measure cost per engaged lead as payroll divided by engaged leads. Pay level attracts recruits, pay tied to output changes effort, and fast feedback and training change how they work; the work itself motivates more than pay. Managing beats doing: replace the owner in each role as soon as possible, then hand the managing to a manager. Weak staff are the fault of whoever hired and trained them: train with document, demonstrate, duplicate (D5), give new starters a reduced workload until quality measures are met, tell them how corrections will come (D13), and if a trained person is still below standard after about three weeks, act through the fair process (see X18). Before the first hire, write how the business behaves with clients (culture is the internal brand), capped at three values, each with concrete behaviours and what gets praised and corrected, including "invite disagreement, keep it about the work, then commit once it's decided"; the owner models it first and every manager reinforces the same things. Hiring: adverts state typical earnings, the hard demands and who it isn't for, in job terms only; describe the worst parts of the role plainly; test skill before interviewing; ask every candidate the same structured "tell me about a time" questions (one per value, one on a real failure), scored on an anchored written scale, no brain teasers; add a coachability role-play with a redo, a paid trial task for close-working roles, and referee rankings given with consent; judge only job-relevant evidence and observed behaviour, never demographics or general liking (see X18); screen for self-drive and respectful challenge; screen out the sure fails and train the unsure middle; hire only someone who would raise the current average on the same test and who respects the skill they'd be led with; give each hire a bar raiser outside the hiring line (for the first hire, someone ahead of the owner, F15) whose veto stands; keep a short file on what strong and weak hires looked like and update the questions from it. For a role the owner doesn't know, interview widely first as learning (F25). In any function, a candidate's skill shows in how many and how good the numbers are that they track for their own area: ask what they measure and why, and prefer the one who names measures the owner has not heard of. Don't settle out of urgency or hire friends or family to scale fast; one failed hire proves the person wrong, never the role. Hire broadly at entry level; for senior roles, hire someone the owner learns from in the interview, found by asking many contacts "do you know anyone like you?", pitched like an offer with time to decide and no exploding offers, with stronger incentives such as a stake; make the first senior hire an operator whose own mission fits the business's. Look first at engaged opted-in contacts; never approach a client's staff. Grow talent from paid junior roles, promote proven people first, and offer progression reviews at 12 to 18 months, never forced exits. Sales team: hire setters first while the owner keeps closing (F13), each role (research and outreach, reply handling, closing, client success) on its own script, scoreboard and pay (L30, L54), with a written setter-to-closer handoff the closer reads back (C4); hire the sales leader before more closers, one who sells and closes before hiring or training; judge candidates by the output metrics they name and how those make money (a role that can't name its output can't drive it); certify new callers (a written check on X2, X4 and C2, plus a role-play scored on the C13 rubric by someone other than the hiring manager) before calls. Keep total sales pay under about 10% of revenue when leads are supplied (more for self-generating reps); pay only on results the person controls that predict client success, simple, paid soon and explained before any change, never mainly for recruiting (L69); back-test any pay plan on last month's real numbers and at higher output; one contest at a time on one behaviour. Choose leaders on written criteria (consistent results, every C5 step certified, mentoring shown, never tenure alone), train them, let them manage one starter first, and have no permanent player-manager roles; managers still take some client calls each month. Run a daily, weekly and one-to-one rhythm with a short daily role-play, give lead-quality feedback one to one as data, and mystery-shop anyone who sells against a checklist of each C5 step. Standards: the weakest performer kept in a function is the standard signed off for it, so judge on output after training and use the fair capability process rather than leave a weak performer; a recurring problem under the same person is a who problem; whoever holds the highest standard for a function decides it; each quarter, ask whether team quality has risen. Before each headcount jump, list which checklists, reports and decision rights change (D5) and expect a dip; when politics or bottlenecks appear, first write down who decides each function and check whether pay rewards a count over the outcome (M38). Each role has a one-page decision list (decide alone, decide and tell, bring to the owner). Staff get least-access views and no client data (X5); monthly numbers are shared with staff, never with variable-fee suppliers (T2). Keep politics and needless meetings away from top performers; deep-work roles get a short daily huddle only. Never script forced cheerfulness; after a hard push, tell people to rest. Managers own their slips (L75). Every exit names one specific thing the person did well; if a role ends through the owner's mistake, say so and leave the door open. All pay, contract, capability, redundancy, working-time, status and reward matters follow the law, with advice first (see X18, X19). Recruiting is selling: strong candidates have options, so sell the role, the people and the path as much as they prove themselves, and move every stage fast, measuring days from first contact to offer, since top candidates leave the market quickly. New hires get a short check-in at the start and end of each day for their first two weeks and shadow other functions. Write each role's path as behaviours: people move up by already doing the next role's work, and having no next step is a common reason good people leave. People are driven mainly by one of lifestyle (what they can buy and do), purpose (the difference the work makes), the next step (being told the next few moves) or competition (a record to beat, an opponent, an injustice to correct): ask each person which, and speak to that one. Know where the problems are: the owner keeps a standing, one-level-deeper view of each area's current biggest problem and its largest recurring costs (by reading the numbers and asking about the work, not only "how is it going?"), and company-wide pay changes, and any recurring outside cost above the F2 spend limit, need the owner's written sign-off; delegating a function (compliance, HR, finance) still means checking what it costs and what it produces each month. Getting clients is one job: put advertising and sales under one owner (the founder until a head of revenue is hired), since sales only fills the gaps the advertising left, so the two are judged on clients won, never against each other. A growing business needs three leaders: one for getting clients, one for delivering the result and one for running the operation (finance, legal, HR, systems), the last serving the other two rather than setting direction. Every hire must raise the average of the team they join, not just meet the requirements: interview 10 to 20 people for a key role, and hire for a role above the owner's own skill only someone the owner learns from in the interview. When someone isn't doing what was asked, check five reasons in order and fix the reason, not the person: they didn't know it was wanted (say it in writing), didn't know how (train, D5), didn't know by when (set a date), didn't want to (reward, praise, autonomy, training in their skills), or something blocks them (remove it). Run the check as five questions, the same with a team member, a supplier or the owner's own stalled work: (1) did they know it was wanted: if it isn't written, treat it as never asked, so put each request in a message or task with the date it was given; (2) did they know what was wanted, defined in behaviours or outcomes (the format, where it goes, what done looks like; a label such as "be more professional" names no behaviour, so say what to do instead next time), since two or three extra minutes of clarity save hours; (3) did they know how: break the task into its steps and find the one step they can't do, then document, demonstrate and have them duplicate it (D5); everything can be trained but not everything is worth training, so set real prerequisites for a role rather than training basic skills it assumes; (4) did they know by when: ask how long the work itself takes and what else is on their plate, then agree the order, which also shows the team's real workload; make end of day the default deadline for small asks rather than end of week; (5) did something block them: ask what they did to remove it and whether it was reasonable for it to block them that long, since this shows whether they solve problems on their own; if the block was real, reorder the work. Ask the first four before the fifth, since people blame circumstances first and the earlier questions keep the conversation off the person. Only then look at motivation, which no one admits to lacking when asked directly: raise the value of the outcome to them by linking the task to what they said they want, and reframe a struggle such as boredom with repetition as a skill they are building on the way to that goal. Open the conversation by naming the gap against what the owner believes they are capable of and asking which of the five it is, so both work on the problem from the same side. Someone who still won't do the work after all five, including a relative, is not kept on because no one else would hire them; act through a fair, lawful process (D16, X18). The owner applies the same five questions to their own stalled work, and turns statements such as "no one can sell like me" into questions such as "how do I find or train someone to sell like me?", which can be answered. Tie anyone's sales commission to clients who stay and are happy, not just to the signature: hold back or claw back commission on refunds and early cancellations within a set window, and include a client-satisfaction check after onboarding, so no one gains by overselling (X9). For sellers, add a shared team bonus paid when the whole team clears a set level, alongside individual pay, so the team helps its weakest member rather than competing against each other. The first three to five sales hires set the team's culture, so hire them with the most care, and hire sellers in small batches where possible, so a problem shared by all points to the script or the leads and a problem with one points to the person. Pay must still work when the business is full: no revenue or profit share to every person that leaves the delivery unit unprofitable, and at scale no seller should earn more than the business makes from their work. If pay was set wrong, fix it rather than wait: tell the team the truth (the owner set it wrong, and what growth it blocks for them), show the owner is taking a cut too, give the new structure and a dated review, and change any contract terms only lawfully, with advice first (X18); accept that some will leave and recruit to the new structure. Define what good looks like for every role and task in observable, measurable terms ("reply to client messages within two hours", never "stop being lazy") before giving feedback, coaching or judging; what can't be seen or measured can't be coached. Authority must match responsibility: whoever answers for a number decides the people and actions that drive it, and a manager blocked from those decisions raises it with the person above, in writing. When two team members can't work together, meet them together once: each says calmly how it felt, each names something good about the other and what could be true in the other's view, each says back what they heard and commits to one change; repeated conflict is then a performance issue. When a good performer's results drop for a personal reason, give a stated period of support and say so plainly, then judge whether it is a short setback or a new pattern; if it continues and the team suffers, have the direct conversation, with UK employment rules and any health or disability duties checked first (X18). To be trusted as a leader, never use what someone disclosed against them, now or later, thank them for telling you, and keep every promise, since one broken word can end trust built over years; sharing something real with the team invites them to earn more trust. To grow past the owner, accept a short-term profit cut to hire leaders better than anyone the business has had, and keep raising the level of each new hire; the vision must be big enough for their own goals to fit inside it. Where good local candidates are scarce and one more worker earns far more than they cost, advertise the role nationally with a written relocation or signing package, part paid on joining and part after a set period (X18). Keep cold outbound sellers and inbound sellers as separate teams: inbound leads cost money, so they go only to sellers who have proved they close, as a promotion, on their own commission plan (more volume, lower pay per sale, steadier); while inbound is new, give it to one proven seller so the offer, lead source and process are the only variables, and keep everyone else on outbound. For a key specialist role, look at the businesses whose work in that area the owner admires, find who does it there (professional profiles, credits, their own posts) and approach them directly with a better offer; keep work core to creating value for clients (attracting, converting, delivering, and the brand) in house, and outsource only supporting functions (T2). Judge candidates more on how fast they learn than on where they start. Group interviews save time for front-line roles; for leadership roles, a candidate the owner learns nothing from in the interview will need teaching instead. For commission-led field or sales roles, write the job advert like a sales ad (the pains of the candidate's current job, the earnings range real sellers reach, the hours, the hard parts stated plainly), screen in short group calls, meet the promising ones in person and make offers there, then have each new seller shadow, then sell while watched; the owner never sells alone, so someone is always learning. Pay must still meet X18 (status checked, minimum wage for workers). Judge drive by work history and behaviour in the trial, never by family status, pregnancy or other protected characteristics (X18, X23). Bringing in a senior leader: the manager owns the first two to four weeks and writes an onboarding plan before day one; tell the team the plain truth about why (what the person has done that the business hasn't), say it will be different rather than promising it will be better, and tell each affected person what changes for them and what doesn't. The new leader meets everyone in the first week or two to hear the patterns (what several people praise, what several name as the same problem), learns from the bottom up (every line of the profit and loss, every number in their function), offers help rather than telling people what to do, and ships visible pieces of work early (a memo, a process, a decision) so the team sees results; a strong leader starts adding value within the first week, and one who needs a quarter to get their bearings is a warning sign. People who say "I got it" and deliver every time are the ones to give the most room and support; track each person's say-do ratio. For staff who could leave and take clients, set a written career path: a title and pay step every three to four months over about two years, each step unlocking a new skill the business can charge more for, and a competed-for route to run a new location on a profit-share bonus that rises with tenure (a bonus, not equity, so it can end if the role isn't done). Paying for a top-decile person in a role usually costs a fraction more than the median and returns several times the output, so budget to hire near the top rather than the middle. In interviews, ask a few ways what the candidate does in their own time and what they are obsessed with: someone whose spare time goes into the role's skill will take feedback and keep improving. Hire junior roles for intelligence and work ethic over experience, senior roles for proven results. Give feedback as start, stop and keep: what to start doing, what to stop doing, and what to keep doing that will keep being rewarded; who is hired, who is let go, and which behaviour is rewarded or left unchallenged are the culture. In the stage where the business needs a senior hire but the hire would cost a large share of profit (often the first few million of revenue), one miss can wipe out a year's progress: raise profit first with more of the owner's own unscalable work or a price rise, so the business can afford two or three attempts at the role, and expect the first hire may not work. Once there are several product lines, run each as a unit with its own profit and loss, charged its share of overheads such as rent and shared staff, and pay its leader a share of that unit's profit (profit share, not equity, T3); put specialists such as developers or marketers who work for a unit under that unit's accountability, not on a separate island. Anyone may propose a new line but must make the case and the resource request in writing; orphan ideas and failing units go to a small group of senior leaders, and a line that proves itself in a test is handed to a leader who championed it, usually from inside, since a champion fights for it and is easier to rein in than a reluctant leader is to motivate. Decide in person or remote by role: remote suits defined tasks, while roles where the business wants more than the task done, and people early in their careers, gain from in-person work through unplanned learning across functions, faster trust and feedback; a leader who walks the floor and makes brief personal contact picks up problems early. Whichever is chosen, say it before hiring. For roles that need judgement from experience, consider people near the end of their careers (including the recently retired) alongside early-career people with energy, since both tend to care about the work more than the title. As headcount grows, watch the ratio of managers to people doing the work: a layer of managers each managing one or two people slows the business and adds process; keep managers' spans wide and move people into doing roles. Ask each team member three questions and expect concrete answers: what do you actually do (actions, not labels such as "analyse"), what does the business do to make money, and how does what you do help that (more clients, or clients worth more)? Anyone who can't link their work to that is either in the wrong role or needs the link explained. Before any hire, work out the revenue it must add to pay for itself: its full cost divided by the business's net margin (at a 25% margin, a hire costing a given sum must add four times that sum in revenue), and name the activity that will produce it. Write the job post as an offer (headline, pay, benefits, hours, location, expectations) and match the experience asked for to the pay: underpaying attracts weaker people or people with second jobs. Never give staff a share of revenue for work whose costs they don't carry; share profit they control instead (T3). To change pay or structure, test the new terms on new hires first; if they attract people who meet the standard, use that as the precedent for existing staff, with notice (about 3 to 6 months), a plan showing how they could keep their current earnings, and the law followed (X18). When a team is weak, add stronger people at better terms alongside it rather than replacing everyone at once, accepting a short period of paying both. Running a priority push: (1) name the one priority and say plainly which smaller problems will be left to burn for now, giving each person a line to use with anyone who complains ("the owner said it's fine"); (2) open with a no-blame statement covering everything before today, so questions about causes are for understanding, not fault; (3) clear the priority's owners of other work rather than adding it on top; (4) break it into named steps with an owner and a time in minutes or hours, then set the next check-in by those times (the same day, or twice a day, not weekly); (5) have each person say back their next action and correct it until it matches; (6) show progress where the team sees it, with an agreed shared reward when it is done; (7) regularly ask each person to list everything they are working on, and cancel or table what no longer matters. Share the business's real numbers (revenue, margins, conversion rates) widely enough that people can help solve problems, within any confidentiality the law or contracts require (X8). When a checklist is followed in full and the results are still worse than another person's, the checklist is incomplete: find what the better person does that it misses and add it, rather than blaming the person. Roles with slow feedback (long projects, complex work) need feedback built in: short check-ins, visible milestones and prompt recognition, since behaviour is shaped by what happens after it. Learn what each person responds to (praise, money, autonomy, recognition, time) by asking and watching, and use that rather than one reward for all. Hire through the same funnel as clients: attract applications, nurture them, treat the interview as a sale, onboard, then retain or promote. The best candidates have options, so sell them the opportunity, with a vision big enough for their own goals to fit inside it, rather than making them sell themselves. Before hiring a role the owner has never filled well, learn what good looks like in it by talking to strong people who do it; for a role the business can't succeed without, it is worth delaying the start rather than hiring the wrong person. The less skilled the person, the more specific the instructions need to be; complaints about communication often mean the wrong person in the seat. Culture is what the owner tolerates: let go, lawfully (X18), those who don't hold the stated values, and judge each later hire against them. How vague an instruction can be depends on the person's skill in that area; for low skill, write it step by step, including how to greet and speak to clients, and weigh the training time against hiring someone with more base skill. Pair every output measure with a quality measure so neither is gamed: pay or bonus per completed job with free redos when the work falls short of the written standard, support judged on both resolution and speed (always at least minimum wage for the hours worked, X18). Sell the business to candidates as a product: a short recruiting video, sent before any interview, that shows how the business makes money, why it exists, and the path a strong performer can take (including leading a new line the business backs later). Before any hire, list the 5 to 10 tasks the role would actually do (with hands, eyes and voice) and turn each that can be a written workflow into an automated or AI-assisted one (F34) before adding a person; plan the work as inputs to outputs, not as an org chart. When automation removes work, raise the standard for everyone; a role no longer needed ends only through a fair, lawful redundancy process (X18). Run sales hiring as a funnel parallel to the client funnel (attract, screen, interview, onboard, keep and promote), with its own numbers, and judge a seller hire on lifetime gross profit per seller against total cost to hire and pay them.
- T2 [considering an agency, or paying any outside supplier] Pay for the output, not the hours: a supplier billing by the hour has a reason to bill more. Outsource a task only if the owner's freed hours, spent on higher-value work, beat the fee plus the cost of any drop in quality (checked on a sample), and not without money to spare. Before any supplier starts, agree one written page: who is involved, who does what, where, by when, how much, for how long, who signs off the work as good, and a proportionate remedy agreed up front if it isn't good or is late (X3); it's for clarity, not suing, so decide what the owner wants first and say it plainly, even to a friend. Keep ownership of the work and the data, keep someone on the owner's side who can judge the work, and never share revenue figures a variable-fee supplier could price against. Never give an open-ended revenue share: cap it with a written review date (affiliate payouts sit under L69). Give each supplier their own login with the least access needed, never the owner's password, removed the day they stop (L36); a supplier handling personal data needs a written contract and never sees client data (X5; see X8). Check employment status before paying any contractor regularly (see X18). Choosing: test skill before interviewing (every candidate gets the same sample task, criteria and deadline, scored on accuracy); for a specialty the owner can't judge, first interview 3 to 5 people who do that job about what good work looks like (F25); pick agencies on referrals, known clients, a waiting list, an honest sales process, long-term strategy, clear requirements, regular meetings, simple reporting and a strong offer, talk to 5 to 10 first, and ask those not picked to refer a good one; ask referees to rank accuracy, speed, communication and reliability and say why; treat hand-picked testimonials as unverified until checked with a client the owner finds; before acting on advice, ask whether they are truly expert on this exact topic and what they gain. When hiring an assistant, screen for reply speed and intelligence and pay above the going rate (T1). Give no exclusivity until a supplier has beaten an alternative on a live batch. For recurring work, agree a written standard, keep a scorecard from day one and give feedback within 2 working days; if a supplier won't meet it after one written request, switch, using the L36 backup and checking notice terms first. Agencies: use them to learn a method or platform, not forever, and name the senior person in the contract. The cheapest route is paying an expert by the hour to watch the owner do the work. Otherwise say up front the work will move in-house, pay for every decision to be explained, set a deadline (about 6 months), run the owner's team alongside until it wins, then drop to cheaper consulting, and end it once the owner is teaching them. Deciding: judge a reduced proposal fresh against the need, budget and payback written beforehand (M19), never against the first number; walk away from a last-minute take-it-or-leave-it demand rather than accept because of time spent; above a spend threshold the owner sets, read the proposal in a distraction-free block and get a view from someone who didn't see the earlier offers (F15); value extras at what they'd cost alone and compare totals in writing. Decline gifts or favours that would create a sense of owing and log any accepted; treat a free trial or "quick call" as the first step of a ladder and write down the bigger ask it leads to; repay favours in proportion, and never turn a small favour into a much bigger ask (M27). If a pitch makes the owner feel trapped by something said earlier, stop and decide later on the F2 test; if unusually charmed, decide only on the written offer, evidence and references (F25); anyone who keeps coming back for small concessions is a red flag. When work slips, put it in writing (thanks for one specific thing, then the specific extra item with its time and pay stated); if they fail outright, go to the decision maker, frame the fault as a risk to their reputation and ask for the fix, never shouting. Praise helpful support staff to their manager when sincere. After a bad supplier or course, write what was learned before judging it. Pay suppliers on time, every time. Before relying on a supplier for anything a launch or the core service depends on: get references from clients like the owner, at the owner's size and use case, and call them; a referral from a friend never skips the checks, so say plainly that the call will be treated as if the owner doesn't know them; ask for a test at the expected load before the date; and treat any doubt from the supplier's own staff as a reason to check again or switch, since bad news before a launch is cheaper than after. Never outsource the core of what the business sells (getting clients or delivering the result) to a supplier no one on the owner's side can judge (T3): where software is built outside, the business owns the code and accounts, the contract requires documentation and a handover, and fee rises are capped and agreed in writing. To learn a channel through agencies: start with a basic agency whose main job is to make the owner publish on a fixed cadence; once the owner knows what the audience responds to, hire an advanced agency on stated terms from the first conversation: a set period (about six months), paying extra for them to explain each decision and the steps behind it, co-creating while the owner documents their process (D5) and trains the team, then a lower-cost consulting agreement, ended once calls teach nothing new. Set the in-house date at the start; if the team can't match the agency by then, either the people or the teaching is the problem. Watch for the usual decline: results after the senior person, then a junior takes over and results slip; the named senior person in the contract is the guard. With no money, learn the channel by doing it. Use specialist partners for what they have done for decades rather than learning it, held to written specifications, regular audits (for safety-critical goods, before the first order) and quarterly business reviews that name problems so they are fixed rather than the relationship ended; own in house only the input whose loss would stop all revenue and that no partner can guarantee. Key supplier risk: where one supplier controls a function the business can't trade without (making the product, building the software, running ads, payment processing), keep a qualified backup, own the code, data, designs and accounts, keep getting leads, selling and delivery in-house where possible, and agree in writing a notice period before they stop (about 6 to 12 months for core work), a proportionate exit charge, and service levels with set remedies for missed dates. Treat repeated requests for earlier or larger prepayments as a warning: ask for the goods already paid for before paying more. The bigger a share of the supplier's business the owner becomes, the more leverage the owner has; where a key supplier is excellent and small, consider bringing them in-house by hire or acquisition (T3). Ask an agency for its best practices (funnel, offer, speed to call leads, call script, show-up steps); one that can't share them is a red flag. Agree a short results window before any longer commitment, and keep speaking to experts in the field until calls teach nothing new before choosing. For sporadic or seasonal work (a launch, one-off tasks the owner keeps doing), keep a bench of vetted contractors switched on and off as needed, with one person checking quality, rather than hiring full-time; trade the owner's hours for their few hours task by task.
- T3 [considering a partner or a first hire] [stage: S3+] Consider a partner or first hire only when the roadmap stage allows it (after the first target, F32), never before. A partner must bring money, experience or time the owner lacks, written down and measurable before any equity moves; never partner out of fear of going alone. Before any equity moves, agree in writing, with a solicitor, what happens on exit, death, disagreements about money and a sale. To make a partner or senior operator act like an owner, first consider a written share of the profit they control (with an optional small, symbolic buy-in to show commitment) before any equity: it is faster, simpler to unwind and gets most of the same behaviour; move equity only where the measurable value justifies it, with advice first (see X17, X18, X19). If the product's core is technical work the owner cannot judge, the first partner or senior hire should be someone who can, with a real stake in the quality of who is hired; until then, have a trusted outside expert interview or test candidates. Split equity by each person's measured contribution of time, money and skill, never equally by headcount; equal skills and equal contributions mean one of the two isn't needed. Have the split conversation on day one, before any work: if a partner balks at a fair split then, that is the answer. Whoever brings in the clients and makes the money gets a share in line with that, because the business can't run without them. A function the business needs (accounts, legal, IT, admin) is hired or bought from a supplier (T2), never paid in equity; equity goes only to people core to getting clients or delivering the result (one making the product exceptional and one telling the world about it is a sound split). A partner who works in the business is paid a salary for the job, separately from what they own as a partner. Never sell a share for less than its value as a growing business (advice first, X17). Red flags in a partner mean stop, believed the first time: a fraud or insolvency history (check public records), asking the owner to personally guarantee their debts or leases, to front all the money, or to do all the work for half, and the pitch "you're leaving money on the table". Never personally guarantee a partner's obligations without advice (X17); keep shared money in an account that needs both signatures for any large withdrawal, with books both can see. Hiring follows T1. Before any partnership, investment, joint deal or equity arrangement, run four checks, the four goods: good people (trust but verify, a mutual background check offered both ways, references the owner finds themselves), good intentions (ask what happens if everything goes wrong, and for a past example of how they behaved when it did), good rationale (the deal in a spreadsheet the owner understands, with the downside case), and good contracts (written, advised, enforceable where the other side can actually be found and held to it). A partner who resists any check or rushes the deal is a red flag. Understand the structure before signing: in any equity, option, sale or investment, terms such as preferences, ratchets, vesting and dilution can change what the owner receives many times over, so read them with a qualified adviser (X20). Where the owner has skill or time but no capital, offering to do the work on someone else's deals for a written share of each one (sweat equity) is a lawful way in, agreed in writing before work starts (X18, X20). Trust means giving someone something they could use against you (information, money, your word or theirs relied on); before giving more, check two things: their record of protecting what they were given before, and whether betraying the owner would cost them more than protecting the owner. Grow trust in small steps, and know that one betrayal usually ends it whatever came before. Bring in a partner rather than hire only when the person is a "barrel" (someone who can create results from nothing at the owner's own level), their skill and experience would take years to build or can't be hired, and the owner would gladly spend time with them outside work; years of working together informally first is the best test. When values and expectations are aligned beforehand the terms are quick to agree, so a fight over final terms signals a deeper mismatch to resolve before signing. Equity carries four things: risk, control, profit and proceeds on sale; staff and junior operators usually want only the profit, so give a profit-share bonus rather than ownership. A partner who also works in the business is paid the market rate for that role before profits are split, so the unit's real profit is known before it is copied. When partners' goals diverge, raise it early and choose a clean exit: a shotgun offer (one names a price and terms at which either side buys the other), an equity swap where partners co-own several units (each takes full ownership of some), or a buyout tied to helping recruit a replacement; take advice on tax and contract terms (X19, X20). Never give equity for one-off work, especially at the very start when the business is valued at almost nothing; pay for it or give a capped fee. Where staff hold shares in a business that won't be sold or listed, plan how they can sell: a regular buyback from profit at an independent valuation, or an auction where holders say what price they would sell at, set up with an adviser (X19, X20). When an owner lists many problems at once, the real gap is usually two or three missing senior people. In a profitable but loose business, a strong first senior hire is often a finance lead asked to find savings worth their own salary within about 90 days, which then funds the next hire.
