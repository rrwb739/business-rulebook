## Team and leadership (T)

- T1 [hiring or managing employees who get leads, sell or deliver] [stage: S3+] Not before the first target (T3). Hiring uses the same Core Four as leads (the owner's network, recruiting, job posts, promoted posts, staff referrals, staffing firms) and is a funnel: fix the worst step, and measure time to fill a role, cost to acquire a hire, and whether both sides rate the fit 10 out of 10 at day 90. Every hire is a certain cost and an uncertain return, so each must build (deliver), sell, or directly support one of those; hire only when D11 shows delivery capacity is the constraint (start recruiting deliverers before outreach outruns their hours), check margins can absorb a new hire's first 30 to 60 days at half output, compare the cost to acquire a hire with the gross profit they bring a year, and track revenue per head before and after each hire. Each month a key role stays empty costs a month of the gross profit it would bring, so paying more to fill it sooner is often cheaper. If cost per customer is within 3x the industry average, work on lifetime value; if more, fix advertising or sales. Measure cost per engaged lead as payroll divided by engaged leads. Pay level attracts recruits, pay tied to output changes effort, and fast feedback and training change how they work; the work itself motivates more than pay. Managing beats doing: replace the owner in each role as soon as possible, then hand the managing to a manager. Weak staff are the fault of whoever hired and trained them: train with document, demonstrate, duplicate (D5), give new starters a reduced workload until quality measures are met, tell them how corrections will come (D13), and if a trained person is still below standard after about three weeks, act through the fair process (see X18). Before the first hire, write how the business behaves with clients (culture is the internal brand), capped at three values, each with concrete behaviours and what gets praised and corrected, including "invite disagreement, keep it about the work, then commit once it's decided"; the owner models it first and every manager reinforces the same things. Hiring: adverts state typical earnings, the hard demands and who it isn't for, in job terms only; describe the worst parts of the role plainly; test skill before interviewing; ask every candidate the same structured "tell me about a time" questions (one per value, one on a real failure), scored on an anchored written scale, no brain teasers; add a coachability role-play with a redo, a paid trial task for close-working roles, and referee rankings given with consent; judge only job-relevant evidence and observed behaviour, never demographics or general liking (see X18); screen for self-drive and respectful challenge; screen out the sure fails and train the unsure middle; hire only someone who would raise the current average on the same test and who respects the skill they'd be led with; give each hire a bar raiser outside the hiring line (for the first hire, someone ahead of the owner, F15) whose veto stands; keep a short file on what strong and weak hires looked like and update the questions from it. For a role the owner doesn't know, interview widely first as learning (F25). In any function, a candidate's skill shows in how many and how good the numbers are that they track for their own area: ask what they measure and why, and prefer the one who names measures the owner has not heard of. Don't settle out of urgency or hire friends or family to scale fast; one failed hire proves the person wrong, never the role. Hire broadly at entry level; for senior roles, hire someone the owner learns from in the interview, found by asking many contacts "do you know anyone like you?", pitched like an offer with time to decide and no exploding offers, with stronger incentives such as a stake; make the first senior hire an operator whose own mission fits the business's. Look first at engaged opted-in contacts; never approach a client's staff. Grow talent from paid junior roles, promote proven people first, and offer progression reviews at 12 to 18 months, never forced exits. Sales team: hire setters first while the owner keeps closing (F13), each role (research and outreach, reply handling, closing, client success) on its own script, scoreboard and pay (L30, L54), with a written setter-to-closer handoff the closer reads back (C4); hire the sales leader before more closers, one who sells and closes before hiring or training; judge candidates by the output metrics they name and how those make money (a role that can't name its output can't drive it); certify new callers (a written check on X2, X4 and C2, plus a role-play scored on the C13 rubric by someone other than the hiring manager) before calls. Keep total sales pay under about 10% of revenue when leads are supplied (more for self-generating reps); pay only on results the person controls that predict client success, simple, paid soon and explained before any change, never mainly for recruiting (L69); back-test any pay plan on last month's real numbers and at higher output; one contest at a time on one behaviour; run sales contests over a fixed cycle of about six weeks, long enough to reach the goal and short enough to feel near (the owner's experience, not a tested figure), in small mixed teams that pair a stronger seller with a weaker one so the stronger one helps; give sellers a visible path with small pay steps at closed-sale milestones (for example a small rise in pay per sale at each set number of sales), within X18. Build each sales role (first touch, qualifier, closer) as a ladder of about four titled levels, each reached by an objective count (shows set, qualified shows, sales closed) paced at about eight weeks of normal output, each with a title step and a small, mostly symbolic pay step; keep the ladder visible to everyone, drop a person back a level when they fall below that level's threshold, and make reaching the top level earn the chance of promotion, not promotion itself: when a seat opens, promote the best fit from those eligible onto a short trial with a fixed number of chances (for example ten sales opportunities), and return them to their level if they close none. Advertise the role on the earnings of the top of the ladder and how long the climb takes, stated truthfully (X9). For a first-touch calling role that takes a day or two to learn and costs little per lead, screen lightly and let results decide; book cold callers in half-day call blocks, since few people dial well for a full day. Choose leaders on written criteria (consistent results, every C5 step certified, mentoring shown, never tenure alone), train them, let them manage one starter first, and have no permanent player-manager roles; managers still take some client calls each month. Run a daily, weekly and one-to-one rhythm with a short daily role-play, give lead-quality feedback one to one as data, and mystery-shop anyone who sells against a checklist of each C5 step. Standards: the weakest performer kept in a function is the standard signed off for it, so judge on output after training and use the fair capability process rather than leave a weak performer; a recurring problem under the same person is a who problem; at each review, judge each person against what the owner would do in their role (the initiative they aren't taking, the questions they aren't asking; the strongest ask questions the owner hadn't thought of), and treat an owner who rates everyone as excellent as not looking closely enough; in each new starter's first week, ask them what their manager said the job involves and which standards are optional, since a manager who quietly waives the owner's standards undoes all training; enforce a stated standard (on time, leads followed up, the site left clean) from the first breach through the written, lawful disciplinary process, never by instant dismissal (X18); whoever holds the highest standard for a function decides it; each quarter, ask whether team quality has risen. Before each headcount jump, list which checklists, reports and decision rights change (D5) and expect a dip; when politics or bottlenecks appear, first write down who decides each function and check whether pay rewards a count over the outcome (M38). Each role has a one-page decision list (decide alone, decide and tell, bring to the owner). Staff get least-access views and no client data (X5); monthly numbers are shared with staff, never with variable-fee suppliers (T2). Keep politics and needless meetings away from top performers; deep-work roles get a short daily huddle only. Never script forced cheerfulness; after a hard push, tell people to rest. Managers own their slips (L75). Every exit names one specific thing the person did well; if a role ends through the owner's mistake, say so and leave the door open. All pay, contract, capability, redundancy, working-time, status and reward matters follow the law, with advice first (see X18, X19). Recruiting is selling: strong candidates have options, so sell the role, the people and the path as much as they prove themselves, and move every stage fast, measuring days from first contact to offer, since top candidates leave the market quickly. New hires get a short check-in at the start and end of each day for their first two weeks and shadow other functions. Write each role's path as behaviours: people move up by already doing the next role's work, and having no next step is a common reason good people leave. People are driven mainly by one of lifestyle (what they can buy and do), purpose (the difference the work makes), the next step (being told the next few moves) or competition (a record to beat, an opponent, an injustice to correct): ask each person which, and speak to that one. Know where the problems are: the owner keeps a standing, one-level-deeper view of each area's current biggest problem and its largest recurring costs (by reading the numbers and asking about the work, not only "how is it going?"), and company-wide pay changes, and any recurring outside cost above the F2 spend limit, need the owner's written sign-off; delegating a function (compliance, HR, finance) still means checking what it costs and what it produces each month. Getting clients is one job: put advertising and sales under one owner (the founder until a head of revenue is hired), since sales only fills the gaps the advertising left, so the two are judged on clients won, never against each other. A growing business needs three leaders: one for getting clients, one for delivering the result and one for running the operation (finance, legal, HR, systems), the last serving the other two rather than setting direction. For these leader roles, hire only someone the owner judges clearly exceptional by the end of the process (a candidate who seems merely good rarely becomes exceptional later, while the reverse error is common), then give them real authority over their decisions and a share of the profit they drive, with a way to buy into their part of the business over time (T3). Once the business has several product lines, run each line as its own unit with its own product and acquisition leads and its own profit, all served by one shared core (finance, legal, HR, IT); launch a new line only once a leader as capable as the owner will run it, not by the owner managing it alongside the core business. For these unit leaders, prefer people who like driving growth and new initiatives but want a salaried role over running their own business; when a candidate has run their own business, ask why they now want a salaried role and whether they plan to start again, and keep client and data protections in the written terms lawful in the project's country (X). Change pay to solve a named problem: the current offer no longer attracts good candidates, people are leaving for better offers, or a proven top performer asks for more and losing them would cost more profit than the rise (work the loss out in numbers); a market pay review with no such problem only adds cost that can't be taken back. Pay law still applies: differences between people doing equal work need a lawful, non-discriminatory reason, and some employers must report pay gaps, in the project's country (X18, confirm with a qualified adviser). Before approving a request for a new hire, ask the team lead for the team's current hours by task and whether the work can be cut, simplified or redistributed first. Any team or role created to fix a metric gets that metric and a review date in writing at the start; if the metric hasn't moved by the date, stop the work and handle any role changes lawfully (X18). Every hire must raise the average of the team they join, not just meet the requirements: interview 10 to 20 people for a key role, and hire for a role above the owner's own skill only someone the owner learns from in the interview. When someone isn't doing what was asked, check five reasons in order and fix the reason, not the person: they didn't know it was wanted (say it in writing), didn't know how (train, D5), didn't know by when (set a date), didn't want to (reward, praise, autonomy, training in their skills), or something blocks them (remove it). Run the check as five questions, the same with a team member, a supplier or the owner's own stalled work: (1) did they know it was wanted: if it isn't written, treat it as never asked, so put each request in a message or task with the date it was given; (2) did they know what was wanted, defined in behaviours or outcomes (the format, where it goes, what done looks like; a label such as "be more professional" names no behaviour, so say what to do instead next time), since two or three extra minutes of clarity save hours; (3) did they know how: break the task into its steps and find the one step they can't do, then document, demonstrate and have them duplicate it (D5); everything can be trained but not everything is worth training, so set real prerequisites for a role rather than training basic skills it assumes; (4) did they know by when: ask how long the work itself takes and what else is on their plate, then agree the order, which also shows the team's real workload; make end of day the default deadline for small asks rather than end of week; (5) did something block them: ask what they did to remove it and whether it was reasonable for it to block them that long, since this shows whether they solve problems on their own; if the block was real, reorder the work. Ask the first four before the fifth, since people blame circumstances first and the earlier questions keep the conversation off the person. Only then look at motivation, which no one admits to lacking when asked directly: raise the value of the outcome to them by linking the task to what they said they want, and reframe a struggle such as boredom with repetition as a skill they are building on the way to that goal. Open the conversation by naming the gap against what the owner believes they are capable of and asking which of the five it is, so both work on the problem from the same side. Someone who still won't do the work after all five, including a relative, is not kept on because no one else would hire them; act through a fair, lawful process (D16, X18). The owner applies the same five questions to their own stalled work, and turns statements such as "no one can sell like me" into questions such as "how do I find or train someone to sell like me?", which can be answered. Tie anyone's sales commission to clients who stay and are happy, not just to the signature: hold back or claw back commission on refunds and early cancellations within a set window, pay it only on cash actually received, never on signed sales whose money has not arrived, and include a client-satisfaction check after onboarding, so no one gains by overselling (X9). For sellers, add a shared team bonus paid when the whole team clears a set level, alongside individual pay, so the team helps its weakest member rather than competing against each other. The first three to five sales hires set the team's culture, so hire them with the most care, and hire sellers in small batches where possible, so a problem shared by all points to the script or the leads and a problem with one points to the person. Pay must still work when the business is full: no revenue or profit share to every person that leaves the delivery unit unprofitable, and at scale no seller should earn more than the business makes from their work. If pay was set wrong, fix it rather than wait: tell the team the truth (the owner set it wrong, and what growth it blocks for them), show the owner is taking a cut too, give the new structure and a dated review, and change any contract terms only lawfully, with advice first (X18); accept that some will leave and recruit to the new structure. Define what good looks like for every role and task in observable, measurable terms ("reply to client messages within two hours", never "stop being lazy") before giving feedback, coaching or judging; what can't be seen or measured can't be coached. Authority must match responsibility: whoever answers for a number decides the people and actions that drive it, and a manager blocked from those decisions raises it with the person above, in writing. When two team members can't work together, meet them together once: each says calmly how it felt, each names something good about the other and what could be true in the other's view, each says back what they heard and commits to one change; repeated conflict is then a performance issue. When a good performer's results drop for a personal reason, give a stated period of support and say so plainly, then judge whether it is a short setback or a new pattern; if it continues and the team suffers, have the direct conversation, with UK employment rules and any health or disability duties checked first (X18). To be trusted as a leader, never use what someone disclosed against them, now or later, thank them for telling you, and keep every promise, since one broken word can end trust built over years; sharing something real with the team invites them to earn more trust. To grow past the owner, accept a short-term profit cut to hire leaders better than anyone the business has had, and keep raising the level of each new hire; the vision must be big enough for their own goals to fit inside it. When handing off the role the owner does now, also name the role the owner would grow into next and hire someone who has already done it, instead of learning it first; learn just enough about each role to judge candidates, and treat an interview round that taught the owner nothing about the role as a sign to hire none of them and widen the search. Where good local candidates are scarce and one more worker earns far more than they cost, advertise the role nationally with a written relocation or signing package, part paid on joining and part after a set period (X18). Keep cold outbound sellers and inbound sellers as separate teams: inbound leads cost money, so they go only to sellers who have proved they close, as a promotion, on their own commission plan (more volume, lower pay per sale, steadier); while inbound is new, give it to one proven seller so the offer, lead source and process are the only variables, and keep everyone else on outbound. For a key specialist role, look at the businesses whose work in that area the owner admires, find who does it there (professional profiles, credits, their own posts) and approach them directly with a better offer; keep work core to creating value for clients (attracting, converting, delivering, and the brand) in house, and outsource only supporting functions (T2). Judge candidates more on how fast they learn than on where they start. Group interviews save time for front-line roles; for leadership roles, a candidate the owner learns nothing from in the interview will need teaching instead. For commission-led field or sales roles, write the job advert like a sales ad (the pains of the candidate's current job, the earnings range real sellers reach, the hours, the hard parts stated plainly), screen in short group calls, meet the promising ones in person and make offers there, then have each new seller shadow, then sell while watched; the owner never sells alone, so someone is always learning. Pay must still meet X18 (status checked, minimum wage for workers). Judge drive by work history and behaviour in the trial, never by family status, pregnancy or other protected characteristics (X18, X23). Bringing in a senior leader: the manager owns the first two to four weeks and writes an onboarding plan before day one; tell the team the plain truth about why (what the person has done that the business hasn't), say it will be different rather than promising it will be better, and tell each affected person what changes for them and what doesn't. The new leader meets everyone in the first week or two to hear the patterns (what several people praise, what several name as the same problem), learns from the bottom up (every line of the profit and loss, every number in their function), offers help rather than telling people what to do, and ships visible pieces of work early (a memo, a process, a decision) so the team sees results; a strong leader starts adding value within the first week, and one who needs a quarter to get their bearings is a warning sign. People who say "I got it" and deliver every time are the ones to give the most room and support; track each person's say-do ratio. For staff who could leave and take clients, set a written career path: a title and pay step every three to four months over about two years, each step unlocking a new skill the business can charge more for, and a competed-for route to run a new location on a profit-share bonus that rises with tenure (a bonus, not equity, so it can end if the role isn't done). Paying for a top-decile person in a role usually costs a fraction more than the median and returns several times the output, so budget to hire near the top rather than the middle. In interviews, ask a few ways what the candidate does in their own time and what they are obsessed with: someone whose spare time goes into the role's skill will take feedback and keep improving. Hire junior roles for intelligence and work ethic over experience, senior roles for proven results. Give feedback as start, stop and keep: what to start doing, what to stop doing, and what to keep doing that will keep being rewarded; who is hired, who is let go, and which behaviour is rewarded or left unchallenged are the culture. In the stage where the business needs a senior hire but the hire would cost a large share of profit (often the first few million of revenue), one miss can wipe out a year's progress: raise profit first with more of the owner's own unscalable work or a price rise, so the business can afford two or three attempts at the role, and expect the first hire may not work. Once there are several product lines, run each as a unit with its own profit and loss, charged its share of overheads such as rent and shared staff, and pay its leader a share of that unit's profit (profit share, not equity, T3); put specialists such as developers or marketers who work for a unit under that unit's accountability, not on a separate island. Anyone may propose a new line but must make the case and the resource request in writing; orphan ideas and failing units go to a small group of senior leaders, and a line that proves itself in a test is handed to a leader who championed it, usually from inside, since a champion fights for it and is easier to rein in than a reluctant leader is to motivate. Decide in person or remote by role: remote suits defined tasks, while roles where the business wants more than the task done, and people early in their careers, gain from in-person work through unplanned learning across functions, faster trust and feedback; a leader who walks the floor and makes brief personal contact picks up problems early. Whichever is chosen, say it before hiring. For roles that need judgement from experience, consider people near the end of their careers (including the recently retired) alongside early-career people with energy, since both tend to care about the work more than the title. As headcount grows, watch the ratio of managers to people doing the work: a layer of managers each managing one or two people slows the business and adds process; keep managers' spans wide and move people into doing roles. Ask each team member three questions and expect concrete answers: what do you actually do (actions, not labels such as "analyse"), what does the business do to make money, and how does what you do help that (more clients, or clients worth more)? Anyone who can't link their work to that is either in the wrong role or needs the link explained. Before any hire, work out the revenue it must add to pay for itself: its full cost divided by the business's net margin (at a 25% margin, a hire costing a given sum must add four times that sum in revenue), and name the activity that will produce it. Write the job post as an offer (headline, pay, benefits, hours, location, expectations) and match the experience asked for to the pay: underpaying attracts weaker people or people with second jobs. Never give staff a share of revenue for work whose costs they don't carry; share profit they control instead (T3). To change pay or structure, test the new terms on new hires first; if they attract people who meet the standard, use that as the precedent for existing staff, with notice (about 3 to 6 months), a plan showing how they could keep their current earnings, and the law followed (X18). When a team is weak, add stronger people at better terms alongside it rather than replacing everyone at once, accepting a short period of paying both. Running a priority push: (1) name the one priority and say plainly which smaller problems will be left to burn for now, giving each person a line to use with anyone who complains ("the owner said it's fine"); (2) open with a no-blame statement covering everything before today, so questions about causes are for understanding, not fault; (3) clear the priority's owners of other work rather than adding it on top; (4) break it into named steps with an owner and a time in minutes or hours, then set the next check-in by those times (the same day, or twice a day, not weekly); (5) have each person say back their next action and correct it until it matches; (6) show progress where the team sees it, with an agreed shared reward when it is done; (7) regularly ask each person to list everything they are working on, and cancel or table what no longer matters. Share the business's real numbers (revenue, margins, conversion rates) widely enough that people can help solve problems, within any confidentiality the law or contracts require (X8). When a checklist is followed in full and the results are still worse than another person's, the checklist is incomplete: find what the better person does that it misses and add it, rather than blaming the person. Roles with slow feedback (long projects, complex work) need feedback built in: short check-ins, visible milestones and prompt recognition, since behaviour is shaped by what happens after it. Learn what each person responds to (praise, money, autonomy, recognition, time) by asking and watching, and use that rather than one reward for all. Hire through the same funnel as clients: attract applications, nurture them, treat the interview as a sale, onboard, then retain or promote. The best candidates have options, so sell them the opportunity, with a vision big enough for their own goals to fit inside it, rather than making them sell themselves. Before hiring a role the owner has never filled well, learn what good looks like in it by talking to strong people who do it; for a role the business can't succeed without, it is worth delaying the start rather than hiring the wrong person. The less skilled the person, the more specific the instructions need to be; complaints about communication often mean the wrong person in the seat. Culture is what the owner tolerates: let go, lawfully (X18), those who don't hold the stated values, and judge each later hire against them. How vague an instruction can be depends on the person's skill in that area; for low skill, write it step by step, including how to greet and speak to clients, and weigh the training time against hiring someone with more base skill. Pair every output measure with a quality measure so neither is gamed: pay or bonus per completed job with free redos when the work falls short of the written standard, support judged on both resolution and speed (always at least minimum wage for the hours worked, X18). Sell the business to candidates as a product: a short recruiting video, sent before any interview, that shows how the business makes money, why it exists, and the path a strong performer can take (including leading a new line the business backs later). Before any hire, list the 5 to 10 tasks the role would actually do (with hands, eyes and voice) and turn each that can be a written workflow into an automated or AI-assisted one (F34) before adding a person; plan the work as inputs to outputs, not as an org chart. When automation removes work, raise the standard for everyone; a role no longer needed ends only through a fair, lawful redundancy process (X18). Run sales hiring as a funnel parallel to the client funnel (attract, screen, interview, onboard, keep and promote), with its own numbers, and judge a seller hire on lifetime gross profit per seller against total cost to hire and pay them.Many basic behaviour rules (be on time, no drinking at work, no streaming during client calls) signal a hiring bar that is too low: raise the bar rather than add rules. Give titles that match the real scope (a manager of one area, not a chief officer of a five-person business). When a strong performer promoted into managing struggles, say so early, own the owner's part in promoting them without support, and offer a role that fits their strengths with an experienced leader brought in above or alongside, explained truthfully as an investment in them; any change to role or pay follows their contract and a fair, lawful process (X18). Say truthfully what the work achieves for clients beyond money: a real mission draws stronger candidates.To raise the quality of anyone's work, ask after each draft: "if you had another two hours on this, what would you do?", have them do it, and repeat; then ask what they would do with a week, which often shows the better approach that is worth the time.Effort beyond the minimum comes from reward, not fear: punishment buys only enough work to avoid it, fades when the punisher is absent and has to keep escalating, while specific praise keeps working. Praise finished work the same day, in the team's work tool, for the exact behaviour; keep a short profile of each person's likes from a voluntary starter form to make thanks personal (any gift within X18 and X19). To get the truth, ask "what would you tell me if you knew I wouldn't be upset?", thank them and act on it. Extend trust inside the team quickly and take it back quickly when it is broken. When someone the business invested in is a better fit elsewhere, help them leave well (a fair, lawful process, X18, and a reference where honest); good exits send back referrals, candidates and later work.Offer two ways to grow: a management track and an expert track with its own pay steps for people who want to get better at the craft rather than manage (ask each person which they want). Give the team one decision rule from the mission (such as "which option is better for the client?") so decisions made without the owner still line up. Share the business's results with the whole team each quarter (what made money, what was spent and didn't work), keeping personal pay, client details and anything confidential out (X8). The owner stays in the detail on the few things that matter most rather than managing only from a distance. Pay client-success staff a variable bonus on the leading measures that keep clients (share reaching the activation point, completion, retention, ascension), scaled by how far into the target range they land, alongside base pay that meets X18; once those measures are tracked per person, the same numbers show who needs coaching. Judge an operator or manager by whether the business as a whole does less unnecessary work and the owner has more time: each new rule or form must remove more work elsewhere than it adds locally, and a manager who only adds tasks is making things worse. Each employee writes their own quarterly goals in the F2 form, starting from the company's goals and working down: which of the three results their work serves, the hypothesis, the input, the expected output; review at quarter end on two questions, did they do it and did it happen. It shows each person how their daily work ties to growth. Never let a newly promoted manager without experience set headcount alone: size each team from the workload numbers, since overhiring forces layoffs that damage the business's reputation as an employer for years. When the business can afford it, hire experienced leaders from the top down so they teach the owner; every growing business carries some debt (financial, management, cultural or technical) and should choose on purpose which one to take on. When someone still doesn't do the work after the expectation was set in writing, they were trained and the reasons and pay were aligned, the problem is motivation: don't default to moving them into another open role, unless they are the person the business would hire for that role anyway (the struggling, previously strong performer promoted beyond their strengths is the exception noted above). Speed of growth depends on knowing what excellent looks like in each role and the order to hire them in: before hiring a department head, get the requirements from someone who has built and run that department several times, not someone a step ahead whose last hire may not have lasted. Don't promote the best individual performer to manager by default: test whether they can recruit, train and hold others to a standard first, or keep them as a highly paid individual contributor. Strong hires usually show it within their first days or first cycle (C13); one who doesn't rarely becomes one, so a mis-hire costs the hiring time, the time to find out and the growth that role would have produced. A value is non-negotiable and must exclude someone: if no good candidate would fail it, it is a platitude; state each in the words the team already uses, hire, correct and part ways by all three (two out of three is a no), and apply the same values to clients and partners: if the owner wouldn't work with someone for ten years, don't start for a day. In interviews, how a candidate answers matters more than what they answer, so ask questions that show character under pressure, honesty with money and taking feedback; a high performer with a large ego who won't take feedback damages the team more than they add. Run hiring as a funnel with the same care as sales: applications per month, the share booked for interview, interviews that become hires, onboarding, training, the career path and staff retention; size weekly interviews to cover front-line turnover plus the growth target, since a hiring plan that only replaces leavers never grows the team. Within that funnel, contact applicants within a day, since strong candidates are often hired elsewhere within days, and give the interviewer a written script; aim for the business to become a name that marks a strong candidate's CV because of the training it gives. When the step that limits hiring is interview time for a high-volume front-line role, run short group interviews and pick from them, rather than adding more one-to-one rounds. Plan communication on four fronts, each with its own calendar: prospects (to become clients), clients (to buy again), staff (the vision, the mission and why the work matters, repeated often) and candidates (the hiring funnel above). For roles not paid on sales: for leaders, split variable pay between company results and objectives within their own control (for example half each), so they still earn part of it when the business has a flat period; for front-line roles, an option to test is a small share of target pay (about 10 to 20%) paid each month against a goal the person sets themselves, measurable, shown to the team and tied to the department's goal, which adds autonomy and peer pressure to set a fair goal. Any variable pay sits on top of a contractual base that never falls below the minimum wage, and changing existing pay into variable pay needs the person's written agreement (X18).
