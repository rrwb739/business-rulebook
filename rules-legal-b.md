## Legal and tax (X), continued

### United States

- X23 [choosing who to serve, writing who an offer is not for, refusing a buyer, setting different terms for different buyers, or picking a side on a social or political question in public] [Fixed] Targeting an avatar by business criteria (industry, size, revenue, problem) and saying who an offer is not for is lawful positioning (L2, O12). Refusing service, giving worse terms or harassing a person because of a protected characteristic is unlawful: in the UK, the Equality Act 2010 Part 3 covers people buying services from the business, with its own list and exceptions for services (age has special rules, and marriage is not covered there), and religion or belief includes a philosophical belief, which tribunals have accepted can include a political philosophy that meets the Grainger test; disabled buyers are owed reasonable adjustments. Content that appeals to one side is a positioning choice (O2), but a policy of turning buyers away for their beliefs or party is checked first. (UK.) In the US, federal law protects some characteristics in public accommodations and states and cities add more; some places protect political affiliation too (for example Washington DC in public accommodations), so check the buyer's state and city before any refusal policy. - X21 [a US owner or US business setting the legal form or owner pay, renting the owner's home to the business, selling investments at a loss, giving to charity, choosing health cover and savings, or selling shares to employees or borrowing against the business or its shares to take cash out] [Fixed] US owner tax items, each legal only on its conditions; never present any of them as a "hack" or as free money, and none replaces X1. (1) Owner pay in an S corporation: an owner who works in the business must first take a reasonable salary at the market rate for the work done, with payroll tax on it; only profit above that, taken as distributions, avoids employment tax, and the IRS can reclassify distributions as wages when the salary is too low. (2) Renting the owner's home to the business (Internal Revenue Code section 280A(g)): rent received for fewer than 15 days in the year is excluded from the owner's income, and the business may deduct it only for a genuine business use at a fair market rent an unrelated party would pay, with dated records of each use; inflated rent or routine use is disallowed, and one day over the limit makes all of the rent taxable. (3) Capital losses: losses on investments sold below cost first offset capital gains without limit, then up to the annual limit set by the IRS against other income, with the rest carried forward until used; they do not carry back. Stock or securities rebought within 30 days before or after the sale trigger the wash sale rule and the loss is disallowed for now; directly held crypto is outside that rule under current law, but bills to extend it were pending in late 2026, so check the law in force at the date of sale. (4) Giving to charity: shares held more than one year and given directly to a public charity or a donor-advised fund are deductible at market value without tax on the gain, for those who itemise, within the percentage-of-income limits and floors in force that year; shares held one year or less are deductible only at the lower of cost and market value. (5) Health savings accounts: open only with qualifying high-deductible or other eligible health cover; contributions are deductible, growth is untaxed, and withdrawals are tax-free only for qualified medical expenses, otherwise taxed and, before 65, penalised; keep receipts. Check every limit, threshold and date against current IRS guidance before acting, and keep each item out of M19's reinvestment and payment split until a qualified adviser has confirmed it for the owner's situation. (US, to check.) Selling shares to an employee stock ownership plan (ESOP) is not tax-free: under Internal Revenue Code section 1042 a seller of C corporation stock can defer federal capital gains tax only if the plan owns at least 30% of the company after the sale, the stock was held at least three years, and the proceeds are reinvested in qualified replacement property within the set window; S corporation sellers get no such deferral. Taking cash out by borrowing against the business (a dividend recapitalisation) loads the business with debt and the payout is normally taxed as a distribution; borrowing against shares the owner holds is a loan, not income, but must be repaid and can be called if the shares fall. Each needs a valuation, lender terms and tax advice before any step. Confirm with a qualified adviser.
