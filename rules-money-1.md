## Money models (M)

- M1 [pricing, offer design, monthly review] Growth comes only three ways: more clients, a higher value per purchase, more purchases per client. Track all three monthly. Price is usually the strongest of them for profit: on large-company averages, a small price rise with no loss of volume lifts operating profit several times more than the same percentage rise in volume, so check the price before chasing more buyers (M2, M4). Judge every client on lifetime gross profit: (price minus direct delivery cost) times months retained, excluding overheads. Monthly revenue is capped at new clients per month times lifetime gross profit: raise the weakest lever. Delivery cost includes the owner's own delivery hours at the owner's hourly rate, not just spend: leave them out and lifetime gross profit looks like revenue, so the 3:1 check (M23) passes when it shouldn't. Delivery cost also includes any spend passed through inside the price (such as ad spend run for the client) and the pay of everyone who delivers, divided by the clients each one serves at full load; model it at scale (each deliverer's capacity, when the next hire is needed) rather than treating a team already on payroll as free. Net margin can never be higher than gross margin. For a service, aim for a gross margin of at least 80%, the owner's hours included, and check it on the first clients: a few points of gross margin multiply gross profit per client. The owner's hourly rate (used in M19, M20, M23 and L46) is the higher of the owner's best alternative hourly earnings and the business's gross profit per business hour (gross profit in the last 12 weeks divided by business hours logged in F13), reset each quarter. From the first paid sale, read the profit and loss and cash flow line by line once a month, with a one-line note on why each line moved. Test the smallest delivery unit at full capacity (one deliverer, one location, one batch of clients): if it doesn't make a healthy profit when full, the model is broken, so cut delivery cost or raise the price before adding volume, locations or product lines; more of a model that loses money only scales the loss (D11, M15). Quote from a written pricing formula, never a price made up on the spot: the formula gives the floor, delivery cost (people's time at their full cost, materials, pass-through spend) plus the target gross margin, so every deal is profitable by design; the actual price is then set on value above that floor (M2), and any quote below the floor needs the owner's written yes. Price by the job, outcome or package rather than by the hour, since an hourly price caps revenue, invites comparison and punishes doing the work faster; give quotes the same day where possible (C3). Every person in the business should know the gross profit per unit sold (per order, box, job or client month), fully loaded with every direct cost (goods, packing, shipping, payment fees, materials), and every line item is negotiated. Build the formula as a quote calculator the sellers use live on the call: costs that scale with the job's size, plus each fixed person's share of capacity, so every quote is instant and every job's margin is known. A price lock can be offered within stated scope limits (a size band, the date, the venue or equivalent) with a written true-up if the client goes outside them (X3).
- M2 [setting or raising any price] Price-to-value gap: clients buy to get a deal, so value must stay clearly above price. Price on the value the client receives, never on delivery cost. Aim for a big gap between delivery cost and price (up to 100x, not 2 or 3x) while it still feels like a steal to the client: charge as much as the value allows. Never price at or below the market average: being second-cheapest has no benefit. Raise the price only after raising the value. Never cut the price to win a client. Aim to be priced so far above the alternatives that buyers assume something different is going on: that creates a category of one. If the owner feels unable to charge more than the M4 comparables, the work isn't better yet: fix delivery results (D8, L32), not the price talk. Aim for the most clients times gross profit per client: while delivery is mostly the owner's hours, choose fewer, dearer clients, and revisit once delivery is templated (M22, O34). Price is a zone, not one number: re-check it as the market moves (M4). One published price for everyone at a time: never vary it by how prosperous a buyer looks. Take views on price only from buyers in the avatar or people who sell to them at this level, never from friends or family. Price on the client's value, not on how easy the work feels to the owner. The price and its tax basis read the same in outreach, the offer doc, the call and the agreement, and never rise between quote and signature (see X3). Never raise the price or add a charge because a deadline is close or a buyer is desperate: rises follow M5 with notice to the pipeline, and where true the owner can say so. Charge for the part buyers value most and can't get elsewhere (often the diagnosis, idea or plan), and price commodity execution separately or hand it off. Value scales with the buyer's size: the same work is worth more to a bigger buyer, so before calling an offer weak, check whether the avatar can appreciate and afford its return, and whether a larger buyer in the same market would (L1, L2). Never lower the price for existing clients, or add more for the same price, in reaction to a cheaper competitor: a price cut comes straight off profit (with a 40% profit margin, a 20% cut in revenue halves the profit), and buyers complain about any price change, including a cut. Answer a competitor by asking current clients what they value and fixing that (D9, F25), and use the competitor only as one input, never as the plan. If charging the price feels like posing, the claim is wrong, not the price: say exactly what the owner has and hasn't done (X9), then price on the value to the buyer. Sell the outcome at a fixed price rather than hours, and say why on the call: hourly billing rewards taking longer, so a fixed price puts the business on the buyer's side. For a programme aimed at a measurable goal, price by the goal rather than by package length: estimate the time to reach it (at a safe, realistic rate), price per week or month times that time, and sell reaching the goal, optionally with a conditional guarantee to keep working until it is reached if the client does the agreed actions (O8, X rules on claims). Pricing low in order to help more people serves neither aim: price for the avatar with the most gross profit, and help others separately, with a lower-end, more scalable offer once the core is profitable or by giving from profit. Payment terms show leverage: move up from paid after the work, to paid as it goes, to paid before it starts (full upfront, a retainer drawn down, or layaway, M30), to paid when a stated outcome happens. When paid on an outcome, tie the fee to revenue or another number the client can't move by changing their costs, rather than to profit. Pay is proportionate to the risk taken on, so take on a risk buyers fear more than it deserves (a deadline, a result, extra difficulty) for a premium, within X3, X11 and O42.
- M3 [setting a revenue goal or a price] Work backwards: goal divided by price gives the clients needed. Check that it fits the owner's set hours and outreach capacity. Compare the shapes the goal could take, a higher price with fewer buyers or a low price with many, on audience size, acquisition cost against lifetime gross profit (M23), fulfilment load and refunds: buyers expect more delivered at a higher price, so delivery work rises with it, while a very low monthly price reaches a large goal only with an audience or ad engine already able to win tens of thousands of buyers, so mass low-price continuity is an option after the first target, never a starting model (F3, M2, M40). The fastest route to any revenue goal is usually something expensive sold to a small number of buyers: the goal needs fewer clients, so fewer leads, calls and deliveries, and the price must still be earned by value (M2, O21); start there unless this comparison or the price tests say otherwise. Most business types can reach a large goal, so pick one soon and adjust from real feedback rather than waiting for the perfect choice (F22, S0). No price point is right in general: the business's own price tests decide (M4, M5).
- M4 [setting or reviewing the price] Competitor price check: find what the avatar pays for comparable help (direct rivals and substitutes) and report where the price sits; the offer must be clearly the premium option. Once early clients prove the result, test pricing at 2 to 3 times the top comparable. For each rival still reaching the avatar, ask how it affords that: better outreach or a bigger back end. Ask how many others could deliver the same result, and at what price: if many could do it cheaper, narrow the offer (O2) rather than cut the price (M2). If the check finds 3 or more similar offers targeting the avatar, narrow one step (a sub-segment or client type) and re-check O2's "only" claim rather than leave the market (L3), within L2's list-count floor. Review the price at every quarterly L1 re-score (close rate on calls held, flinch rate, the comparables) even when nothing changes, and log the decision. As one input to that review, never as the price setter, ask avatar buyers only (M2), after they have seen what the offer delivers, four questions: at what price would it be so cheap you'd doubt it works, a bargain, getting expensive but still worth considering, and too expensive to consider. Ask in genuine F25 interviews, early-client reviews or no-show and lost-deal follow-ups, never on a sales call before the price is given (it anchors low, C2). Read the answers as a range of what feels acceptable, not as demand or profit: the "too cheap" figure warns where a low price breeds doubt (M6), the "too expensive" figure is checked against the premium anchor (M7, M33), and a small sample gives a rough range only. Where it clashes with M2's premium position or M5's close-rate bands on real calls, the real calls win: what buyers pay beats what they say.
- M5 [first clients of any offer, pilot pricing, discount requests or negotiation] The first few clients of any new offer (include at least one sceptical buyer inside the avatar; tag each early client's proof by buyer type and lead outreach to similar buyers with it, labelled as early-client proof, O15) get it free in exchange for written commitments: attendance, feedback throughout, before-and-after measures, and an honest testimonial (video and written) if the results warrant it (L37). Early on the goal is learning, not earning. Frame it honestly: state the planned price, that the owner is new to this, and that it is free in exchange for honest feedback throughout and, if the job is good, a testimonial and an introduction to another buyer. Ask for feedback as "tell me how I can make this better". Free clients who came by referral, with no relationship with the owner, often do none of the work; give them a real stake instead of a fee, such as a set donation paid by them directly to a registered charity of their own choice before starting, with proof of payment; the owner never holds or routes the money, never presents the business as a charity or the work as charitable, and checks X15's charity rules before mentioning it in marketing. Moving free clients to paid is always at full price (M21, M2); setting the stake at the full price makes the switch an easy ask ("pay me what you paid the charity"). The real test is whether they stay and pay full price afterwards. If the first round gives too little proof (prospects still stall on proof, C9, C14), run a second free round with the same commitments rather than cut the price. Start charging once early clients refer others, or once the planned free places are used, whichever comes first; full price after that. Then raise the price about 20% every 5 sales, but only once value has been raised too (M2). Judge each step on total cash and gross profit, not on the count of noes. No discount steps and no plain money off. If every prospect says yes at a price, raise it on the next call, once value supports it. After at least 10 full-price offers, use close rate on calls held (not on offers made), read only once pre-call education (C2) is live: about 30 to 40% is right; 40 to 50% means slightly underpriced; above 50% means raise it, the higher the rate the bigger the rise, in steps with a re-check. Below 30%, never cut the price: fix the avatar or the sales process first (L2, F12, C2, C5). A full delivery batch with a real waiting list (O36) means raise the price for the next batch rather than add hours beyond F1. Before every price rise, tell everyone in the pipeline the date it goes up. Past clients and members get the same notice first, personally: they keep the current price if they buy again before that date; the date is real and the same for all (O37 [Fixed]), and existing terms are kept until they end (M22). Present any instalment total as the price and paying upfront as the saving (M37). Launch each new offer cheaply, then keep raising the price until the extra cash from each yes no longer covers the lost noes. Judge any price test or money model change on gross profit per 100 prospects contacted (and per 100 calls held), counting the back end at real retention; for recurring offers, compare close rate times (price per period divided by churn per period) after delivery cost. When noes cluster on affordability, answer with M37 payment options rather than reading it as overpricing, within F12's red flags. Review every price at least once a year against the rise in the business's own costs and general inflation: a price left unchanged for years quietly shrinks the margin each year, so a small yearly rise is the floor, not a bonus. Bigger tests (double the price or more) are allowed once value supports it (M2): before the test, write the current close rate and the number of calls the new price gets before judging it, and don't react to the first few noes, which would have come at the old price too. A higher price wins when price times the new close rate, minus delivery cost per client, beats the old figure (for example, a cost of 500 sold at 1,000 earns 500 a sale; at 2,000 it earns 1,500, so even a third fewer sales nearly doubles gross profit). For an ordinary service (not a status good), a higher price can raise the close rate, because buyers who doubt a cheap provider can deliver believe a dearer one more (O21 likelihood); so a high close rate is a reason to test a rise, not to fear one. Before any rise, work out and write down the break-even close rate: the lowest close rate at which the new price still earns more gross profit than the old one; keep the rise while the measured rate stays above it. A price ending is a lever: in the middle market a price just under the round number (16.99 rather than 16) usually reads as the same price, while premium positions keep round prices (O2); on high-volume, low-margin businesses a small rise per item can lift profit sharply (a 6% rise in price on a 19% net margin, with volume unchanged, adds about 30% to profit), so test it over a set period and watch item volume. Where prices were set by adding a margin to cost, move to pricing on what buyers will pay: test rises first on the highest-volume items, aiming for the most gross profit across units sold, not the most units, and test each buyer segment separately, since some are far less price sensitive than others; on a thin net margin a small rise can nearly double profit if volume holds (a 6% rise on a 6.4% net margin takes it to about 12.4%), so a rise is usually worth testing, and it can be reversed if volume falls further than the arithmetic allows. Raise prices for new clients first; once the market accepts the new price, raise it for existing recurring clients in this order: a short heads-up the day before; then the message: a reminder of the measured value they have had (from their own records, O47), the rise stated plainly, why (what the money will be reinvested in and what they get from it), and, for loyalty, their current price kept for 3 to 6 months before the new one applies; end with a line inviting anyone for whom it causes real hardship to talk, and offer them a longer delay, a slower step-up or a lighter version at the old price. Announce it in a community space with comments off and questions taken privately. Existing contract terms still hold until they end (M22).Judge each price step on gross profit per sales conversation (close rate times price, less delivery cost), not on close rate alone; move prices up in steps rather than down, since lowering a price that earlier buyers paid means refunding or upgrading them.
- M6 [pricing, or clients not doing the work] Price builds commitment: a price that stings a little makes the client show up and follow through, which improves results. Cheap prices attract clients who are never satisfied. A higher price also raises perceived value by itself. For free early clients, M5's written commitments do this job. If prospects doubt it can work while the value is clearly there, check whether the price reads as too cheap to the larger buyers in the avatar (M4).
- M7 [pricing or running a sales call] Show three tiers: a free or low-cost DIY version below the core offer, the core offer, and a premium tier at about 10x the core price, said out loud as an anchor. The premium is a real offer the owner would happily deliver, capped at the number the owner can serve, and sold properly as in M33 (definition in the instructions box). Present the tiers as routes to the same destination at different speeds and levels of support, without implying a guaranteed result (O15). Every tier targets a different buyer and sits about 5x or more from its neighbour, never a few percent apart; price from the buyer's value, not the owner's wallet (C7). Put speed and risk extras inside the premium tier first rather than leaving the avatar before the first target (L2, F3). Barbell check at each F12 review: high-touch work goes only to buyers who pay high-end prices, and small buyers get templates or DIY; the middle (high-touch help at a mid price for small buyers) is easy to sell and hard to keep, so if small buyers take most of the owner's hours, move them to the DIY or a templated version. The premium is premium, not luxury: its price is justified by a better result or experience, never by price as status. Build from the top down: after the first target, step down from the premium position rather than launching a cheaper version first. Before adding any tier, forecast its gross profit after the owner's hours, and add it only if it would bring about as much as the existing tiers combined. The higher the price, the higher the reputation risk if it doesn't deliver. In any written price table, list the premium first, then the core offer, then the DIY tier, never cheapest first. Never drop the premium for low take-up alone: judge it on core-offer take-up when it was shown, and replace it before removing it. If the premium sells, the core offer's onboarding runs as its phase 1. Where few buyers are expected to take the premium tier, keep it as close to the core offer as it can be while still a real, deliverable step up, so the core price reads as the obvious choice; the difference must be stated plainly and never imply the core is weaker than it is (X9). Where the business helps the buyer win a sum of money, offer two real options: a lower fixed fee plus a stated share of what they win, or a much higher fixed fee with no share; the higher fee anchors the first. Check the rules on fee shares in regulated professions first (X4). With three tiers, the price of the middle one steers choice: close to the top tier's price, it pushes buyers up; close to the bottom's, it makes the middle the best value; set it from where most buyers currently land and which tier the business wants them in, judged on revenue per buyer, and keep every tier a real option (X9). When buyers can check out on their own, price the route that needs the owner's or a seller's time higher, and say truthfully why (it takes someone's time); if most still choose the assisted route, raise the base price. To design the premium tier, use three prompts: what would be included at 10 or 100 times the price (then strike what has real cost and keep the rest); what would the experience be if this one client's word of mouth were the only marketing; and how could it be worth 10 times as much with nothing unscalable in it. Speed is the strongest lever for buyers with money: priority, faster delivery and first response. Design it for the buyer who can pay that much, not for today's buyer stretched; state the full price, then stop talking; if they decline, offer the scalable version with most of the elements. Owner-delivered one-to-one work is a fine early premium: capped hours, near-total margin, faster learning and the best case studies, and its cash funds growth. A seasonal premium drop: a far higher-priced, fuller version offered to a fixed small share of current clients (start at about 5%, so it sells out), announced in advance with the true number of places and a true live count as they go (O37 [Fixed]).
- M8 [the core offer is ending for a client, designing an entry offer that leads into continuity, or buyers arrive through a low-price automated sale] Offer continuity as the next step of the core offer, timed to the client's recurring needs. Never let a finished client leave without the offer. Remind clients to buy more after each big win and make the next offer more compelling than the first: a neglected back end is why clients drop off, and more to buy also means more to refer friends to. A done-for-you version is a second offer and waits until the first target. If the core offer makes the client independent (graduate and leave), the client has no built-in reason to stay, so design continuity in from day one. The continuity offer at the end is a sales call the owner takes, with a short video before it and the usual sales script, not a support check-in. For a later done-for-you offer, show the full real step list so they see the work they avoid; for done-with-you, keep it to three simple pillars. A short, fixed-length programme with a visible before and after (a few weeks) works as an entry offer: it lowers the risk of saying yes, and its end is the natural moment to offer continuity while the result is visible (K1). Where buyers arrive through a low-price automated sale, a person contacting recent buyers to offer the higher next step usually reaches the buyers who can afford more and are already warm; do it only where the bigger offer pays for the calls and the channel is lawful (X1, X6, K7). Education (coaching, courses, training) is easy to sell and high in margin but not sticky, since buyers are meant to learn and leave; pair it with something that is sticky, a done-for-you service that keeps running (management, maintenance, upkeep) or a physical product used up and re-bought, so education brings buyers in and the sticky part keeps them paying. When buyers of the education keep asking the business to do it for them, that is demand for the done-for-you version: sell it as a fixed-term build or setup (a set number of weeks for a set price) followed by continuity for running it, and lead with it if retention by batch is high (M9); if those clients leave quickly, stay with the education and keep done-for-you as a premium tier (M7). A service or physical part is also harder to copy than pure information (F30 point 10). While scaling, keep the front end as leveraged as possible and add no options that need the owner one to one (M22, O34). For services clients repeat on a natural cycle: (1) book the next visit before or during the current one (not after, when they want to leave), treated as a scripted sales step with practised answers and tracked as the rebook rate; (2) then offer the membership to regulars framed truthfully on their own visit history ("you've been in six times; members get these extras at the same price"), with a perk they would otherwise lose, such as a standard price held at peak times (D11); (3) then, at checkout, offer to prepay six or twelve months, stating the true saving (if the saving equals today's bill, say exactly that; never call it "today free" unless today is actually free, X9). Any members' monthly draw needs a free entry route under prize-draw law (X3). For businesses with walk-in or one-off visits (restaurants, salons, shops): identify first-time visitors discreetly at the start (a question, then a marker staff can see), end the first visit with a small unannounced free item, since people remember the best moment and the ending most, and hand over a personal card from the manager for a free item on the next visit; returning regulars get the review ask instead (M45, X12). A loyalty card works better with a running start (some stamps given on joining for the same number needed), its main perk is something scarce that costs little (priority when there is a wait), the sign-up asks where they heard of the business (L14), and marketing messages need separate consent (X2).
