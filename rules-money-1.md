## Money models (M)

- M1 [pricing, offer design, monthly review] Growth comes only three ways: more clients, a higher value per purchase, more purchases per client. Track all three monthly. Price is usually the strongest of them for profit: on large-company averages, a small price rise with no loss of volume lifts operating profit several times more than the same percentage rise in volume, so check the price before chasing more buyers (M2, M4). Judge every client on lifetime gross profit: (price minus direct delivery cost) times months retained, excluding overheads. Monthly revenue is capped at new clients per month times lifetime gross profit: raise the weakest lever. Delivery cost includes the owner's own delivery hours at the owner's hourly rate, not just spend: leave them out and lifetime gross profit looks like revenue, so the 3:1 check (M23) passes when it shouldn't. Delivery cost also includes any spend passed through inside the price (such as ad spend run for the client) and the pay of everyone who delivers, divided by the clients each one serves at full load; model it at scale (each deliverer's capacity, when the next hire is needed) rather than treating a team already on payroll as free. Net margin can never be higher than gross margin. For a service, aim for a gross margin of at least 80%, the owner's hours included, and check it on the first clients: a few points of gross margin multiply gross profit per client. The owner's hourly rate (used in M19, M20, M23 and L46) is the higher of the owner's best alternative hourly earnings and the business's gross profit per business hour (gross profit in the last 12 weeks divided by business hours logged in F13), reset each quarter. From the first paid sale, read the profit and loss and cash flow line by line once a month, with a one-line note on why each line moved. Test the smallest delivery unit at full capacity (one deliverer, one location, one batch of clients): if it doesn't make a healthy profit when full, the model is broken, so cut delivery cost or raise the price before adding volume, locations or product lines; more of a model that loses money only scales the loss (D11, M15). Quote from a written pricing formula, never a price made up on the spot: the formula gives the floor, delivery cost (people's time at their full cost, materials, pass-through spend) plus the target gross margin, so every deal is profitable by design; the actual price is then set on value above that floor (M2), and any quote below the floor needs the owner's written yes. Price by the job, outcome or package rather than by the hour, since an hourly price caps revenue, invites comparison and punishes doing the work faster; give quotes the same day where possible (C3).
- M2 [setting or raising any price] Price-to-value gap: clients buy to get a deal, so value must stay clearly above price. Price on the value the client receives, never on delivery cost. Aim for a big gap between delivery cost and price (up to 100x, not 2 or 3x) while it still feels like a steal to the client: charge as much as the value allows. Never price at or below the market average: being second-cheapest has no benefit. Raise the price only after raising the value. Never cut the price to win a client. Aim to be priced so far above the alternatives that buyers assume something different is going on: that creates a category of one. If the owner feels unable to charge more than the M4 comparables, the work isn't better yet: fix delivery results (D8, L32), not the price talk. Aim for the most clients times gross profit per client: while delivery is mostly the owner's hours, choose fewer, dearer clients, and revisit once delivery is templated (M22, O34). Price is a zone, not one number: re-check it as the market moves (M4). One published price for everyone at a time: never vary it by how prosperous a buyer looks. Take views on price only from buyers in the avatar or people who sell to them at this level, never from friends or family. Price on the client's value, not on how easy the work feels to the owner. The price and its tax basis read the same in outreach, the offer doc, the call and the agreement, and never rise between quote and signature (see X3). Never raise the price or add a charge because a deadline is close or a buyer is desperate: rises follow M5 with notice to the pipeline, and where true the owner can say so. Charge for the part buyers value most and can't get elsewhere (often the diagnosis, idea or plan), and price commodity execution separately or hand it off. Value scales with the buyer's size: the same work is worth more to a bigger buyer, so before calling an offer weak, check whether the avatar can appreciate and afford its return, and whether a larger buyer in the same market would (L1, L2). Never lower the price for existing clients, or add more for the same price, in reaction to a cheaper competitor: a price cut comes straight off profit (with a 40% profit margin, a 20% cut in revenue halves the profit), and buyers complain about any price change, including a cut. Answer a competitor by asking current clients what they value and fixing that (D9, F25), and use the competitor only as one input, never as the plan. If charging the price feels like posing, the claim is wrong, not the price: say exactly what the owner has and hasn't done (X9), then price on the value to the buyer.
- M3 [setting a revenue goal or a price] Work backwards: goal divided by price gives the clients needed. Check that it fits the owner's set hours and outreach capacity. Compare the shapes the goal could take, a higher price with fewer buyers or a low price with many, on audience size, acquisition cost against lifetime gross profit (M23), fulfilment load and refunds: buyers expect more delivered at a higher price, so delivery work rises with it, while a very low monthly price reaches a large goal only with an audience or ad engine already able to win tens of thousands of buyers, so mass low-price continuity is an option after the first target, never a starting model (F3, M2, M40). The fastest route to any revenue goal is usually something expensive sold to a small number of buyers: the goal needs fewer clients, so fewer leads, calls and deliveries, and the price must still be earned by value (M2, O21); start there unless this comparison or the price tests say otherwise. Most business types can reach a large goal, so pick one soon and adjust from real feedback rather than waiting for the perfect choice (F22, S0). No price point is right in general: the business's own price tests decide (M4, M5).
- M4 [setting or reviewing the price] Competitor price check: find what the avatar pays for comparable help (direct rivals and substitutes) and report where the price sits; the offer must be clearly the premium option. Once early clients prove the result, test pricing at 2 to 3 times the top comparable. For each rival still reaching the avatar, ask how it affords that: better outreach or a bigger back end. Ask how many others could deliver the same result, and at what price: if many could do it cheaper, narrow the offer (O2) rather than cut the price (M2). If the check finds 3 or more similar offers targeting the avatar, narrow one step (a sub-segment or client type) and re-check O2's "only" claim rather than leave the market (L3), within L2's list-count floor. Review the price at every quarterly L1 re-score (close rate on calls held, flinch rate, the comparables) even when nothing changes, and log the decision. As one input to that review, never as the price setter, ask avatar buyers only (M2), after they have seen what the offer delivers, four questions: at what price would it be so cheap you'd doubt it works, a bargain, getting expensive but still worth considering, and too expensive to consider. Ask in genuine F25 interviews, early-client reviews or no-show and lost-deal follow-ups, never on a sales call before the price is given (it anchors low, C2). Read the answers as a range of what feels acceptable, not as demand or profit: the "too cheap" figure warns where a low price breeds doubt (M6), the "too expensive" figure is checked against the premium anchor (M7, M33), and a small sample gives a rough range only. Where it clashes with M2's premium position or M5's close-rate bands on real calls, the real calls win: what buyers pay beats what they say.
- M5 [first clients of any offer, pilot pricing, discount requests or negotiation] The first few clients of any new offer (include at least one sceptical buyer inside the avatar; tag each early client's proof by buyer type and lead outreach to similar buyers with it, labelled as early-client proof, O15) get it free in exchange for written commitments: attendance, feedback throughout, before-and-after measures, and an honest testimonial (video and written) if the results warrant it (L37). Early on the goal is learning, not earning. Frame it honestly: state the planned price, that the owner is new to this, and that it is free in exchange for honest feedback throughout and, if the job is good, a testimonial and an introduction to another buyer. Ask for feedback as "tell me how I can make this better". Free clients who came by referral, with no relationship with the owner, often do none of the work; give them a real stake instead of a fee, such as a set donation paid by them directly to a registered charity of their own choice before starting, with proof of payment; the owner never holds or routes the money, never presents the business as a charity or the work as charitable, and checks X15's charity rules before mentioning it in marketing. Moving free clients to paid is always at full price (M21, M2); setting the stake at the full price makes the switch an easy ask ("pay me what you paid the charity"). The real test is whether they stay and pay full price afterwards. If the first round gives too little proof (prospects still stall on proof, C9, C14), run a second free round with the same commitments rather than cut the price. Start charging once early clients refer others, or once the planned free places are used, whichever comes first; full price after that. Then raise the price about 20% every 5 sales, but only once value has been raised too (M2). Judge each step on total cash and gross profit, not on the count of noes. No discount steps and no plain money off. If every prospect says yes at a price, raise it on the next call, once value supports it. After at least 10 full-price offers, use close rate on calls held (not on offers made), read only once pre-call education (C2) is live: about 30 to 40% is right; 40 to 50% means slightly underpriced; above 50% means raise it, the higher the rate the bigger the rise, in steps with a re-check. Below 30%, never cut the price: fix the avatar or the sales process first (L2, F12, C2, C5). A full delivery batch with a real waiting list (O36) means raise the price for the next batch rather than add hours beyond F1. Before every price rise, tell everyone in the pipeline the date it goes up. Past clients and members get the same notice first, personally: they keep the current price if they buy again before that date; the date is real and the same for all (O37 [Fixed]), and existing terms are kept until they end (M22). Present any instalment total as the price and paying upfront as the saving (M37). Launch each new offer cheaply, then keep raising the price until the extra cash from each yes no longer covers the lost noes. Judge any price test or money model change on gross profit per 100 prospects contacted (and per 100 calls held), counting the back end at real retention; for recurring offers, compare close rate times (price per period divided by churn per period) after delivery cost. When noes cluster on affordability, answer with M37 payment options rather than reading it as overpricing, within F12's red flags. Review every price at least once a year against the rise in the business's own costs and general inflation: a price left unchanged for years quietly shrinks the margin each year, so a small yearly rise is the floor, not a bonus. Bigger tests (double the price or more) are allowed once value supports it (M2): before the test, write the current close rate and the number of calls the new price gets before judging it, and don't react to the first few noes, which would have come at the old price too. A higher price wins when price times the new close rate, minus delivery cost per client, beats the old figure (for example, a cost of 500 sold at 1,000 earns 500 a sale; at 2,000 it earns 1,500, so even a third fewer sales nearly doubles gross profit).
- M6 [pricing, or clients not doing the work] Price builds commitment: a price that stings a little makes the client show up and follow through, which improves results. Cheap prices attract clients who are never satisfied. A higher price also raises perceived value by itself. For free early clients, M5's written commitments do this job. If prospects doubt it can work while the value is clearly there, check whether the price reads as too cheap to the larger buyers in the avatar (M4).
- M7 [pricing or running a sales call] Show three tiers: a free or low-cost DIY version below the core offer, the core offer, and a premium tier at about 10x the core price, said out loud as an anchor. The premium is a real offer the owner would happily deliver, capped at the number the owner can serve, and sold properly as in M33 (definition in the instructions box). Present the tiers as routes to the same destination at different speeds and levels of support, without implying a guaranteed result (O15). Every tier targets a different buyer and sits about 5x or more from its neighbour, never a few percent apart; price from the buyer's value, not the owner's wallet (C7). Put speed and risk extras inside the premium tier first rather than leaving the avatar before the first target (L2, F3). Barbell check at each F12 review: high-touch work goes only to buyers who pay high-end prices, and small buyers get templates or DIY; the middle (high-touch help at a mid price for small buyers) is easy to sell and hard to keep, so if small buyers take most of the owner's hours, move them to the DIY or a templated version. The premium is premium, not luxury: its price is justified by a better result or experience, never by price as status. Build from the top down: after the first target, step down from the premium position rather than launching a cheaper version first. Before adding any tier, forecast its gross profit after the owner's hours, and add it only if it would bring about as much as the existing tiers combined. The higher the price, the higher the reputation risk if it doesn't deliver. In any written price table, list the premium first, then the core offer, then the DIY tier, never cheapest first. Never drop the premium for low take-up alone: judge it on core-offer take-up when it was shown, and replace it before removing it. If the premium sells, the core offer's onboarding runs as its phase 1.
- M8 [the core offer is ending for a client, designing an entry offer that leads into continuity, or buyers arrive through a low-price automated sale] Offer continuity as the next step of the core offer, timed to the client's recurring needs. Never let a finished client leave without the offer. Remind clients to buy more after each big win and make the next offer more compelling than the first: a neglected back end is why clients drop off, and more to buy also means more to refer friends to. A done-for-you version is a second offer and waits until the first target. If the core offer makes the client independent (graduate and leave), the client has no built-in reason to stay, so design continuity in from day one. The continuity offer at the end is a sales call the owner takes, with a short video before it and the usual sales script, not a support check-in. For a later done-for-you offer, show the full real step list so they see the work they avoid; for done-with-you, keep it to three simple pillars. A short, fixed-length programme with a visible before and after (a few weeks) works as an entry offer: it lowers the risk of saying yes, and its end is the natural moment to offer continuity while the result is visible (K1). Where buyers arrive through a low-price automated sale, a person contacting recent buyers to offer the higher next step usually reaches the buyers who can afford more and are already warm; do it only where the bigger offer pays for the calls and the channel is lawful (X1, X6, K7).
- M9 [designing or running continuity] Find the months when most members leave (check the early months first), make the first period excellent and plan a reason to stay past each of those points. Aim for net revenue retention above 100%. Don't scale outreach while members are leaving: fix the leak first. Log every cancellation by reason: structural churn (the client closed, merged, sold or retired) is fixed by avatar choice (L2), not delivery; tag the rest as didn't use it (fix onboarding and engagement, or offer a lighter tier, M39) or used it but not good enough (fix the product, L65). Tag "overwhelmed" as its own reason and answer it by removing or replacing material, never by adding sessions or templates; in the core offer too, if a client falls behind, cut its task list before adding support. Compare tenure by client segment and cut the segment that leaves first. If members average under two billing cycles, treat it as a fit problem first (F12 red flags), not a content problem. Track take-up and renewals by delivery batch, not in aggregate, and track both logo retention (share of members still paying after 12 months) and revenue retention (last year's members' revenue now divided by their revenue then); set the retention target from the business's own data. Build legitimate stickiness through integration (what is built lives in the client's own systems and is updated each cycle), but never hold back their work or data when they leave (X5, M27): they keep everything built. Results and experience are separate: after each session and at each cycle, ask one 1 to 10 question ("how easy was working with us this week?") alongside the D8 counts, log reply times, and act on low scores before renewal; a good experience never excuses missing a guaranteed result (O8, M27). At each cycle, tell each member one specific, true thing it did well, from its D8 counts. At each cycle check-in, ask the client's lead contact how the period went, what went well and what to improve, then show each D8 count against their own day-1 baseline and ask "what do you see?" before giving the owner's view. After the first paid batch, send early clients, clients and members a yearly one-page letter in the owner's voice: consented, labelled results in counts (L32), what the owner got wrong and fixed (L75), what changes next and the original promise restated, with no other client's details (X5, M12) and an opt-out for anyone not a current client (see X2). Offer a short call to everyone who cancels or asks for a refund, to learn what went wrong, never to block or delay the cancellation (it goes through as asked, X3): let them say it all, never minimise it, agree plainly where the business fell short, then ask "what would it take to make this right?"; log the reason and the fix, and tell them what changed (D9). Members who feel overwhelmed cancel: keep continuity to the fewest things that deliver the main reason they joined, each worth the price on its own, and cut or merge what few members use rather than adding more.
- M10 [the core offer's demand depends on something that will fade] When demand for the core offer rests on a deadline, wave or trend that will fade, plan the next promotion and ascension offer before it fades (a window opened by a new technology that is still hard to use well counts: the difficulty is the advantage, so move while it is hard and plan for when it becomes easy for everyone), and start building that offer and its proof during the current stage, not when demand drops. List the client needs that will still hold in 10 years and put D15 product hours and the next offer only into those; score the next offer and any idea-list candidate (F22) on whether clients would love it, it can grow large, it returns the hours and money (M19), it lasts, the need is underserved and the business can make it clearly different (O2): size alone is never enough.
- M11 [a client has just bought] Offer zero-delivery-cost add-ons that recover acquisition cost.
- M12 [planning the year for continuity members] Run a short, defined extra programme for existing members about once per billing cycle, around their point of greatest need. After the first target: make one of them free with "bring another business", genuinely free (see X3), the guest introduced three-way with consent (L66), and the add-on offered at a second meeting. Later, after the first target, test events: a periodic members' meetup as a bonus; a small breakfast where consenting paying clients and invited prospects sit together, prospects told plainly it is a sales event, with no scripted testimonials; opt-in introductions between members who don't compete for the same customers; a paid in-person workshop day before a peak, capped at the real room size (O36), filmed only with every attendee's consent. Hospitality stays modest, the same for everyone and never conditional (see X16). Where members may compete, never share prices, customer lists or customer details between them, and share anyone's details only with consent (see X20, X8). Always announce the next event at the current one, and consider making events a reward for members who stay 6 to 12 months. Upsells at events go to qualified people in the room; judge conversion on them, not the whole room. At events only the owner sells, a warm room needs a short pitch, and it goes just before a break; test it against just after a modest meal. In any members' space, pin one line on what members are asked to share and thank each who does personally; test a challenge on members' own results, with no league table of prices or customer data and any prize run under X3. At any event, webinar or members' space: no planted attendees, no pre-arranged questions or testimonials presented as spontaneous, no friends posing as buyers, and a recording never pretends to be live; a live-only bonus is fine if true and stated in advance (C2) (see X9).
- M13 [a client asks for something outside the core offer] Stay productised: the same packaged steps for every client, no custom work. Turn repeated requests into package features only if it helps most clients and removes more effort than it adds (O21); otherwise keep it out or make it a separate template. Repeated out-of-scope requests from one client are a fit flag (F12). When declining a client's idea, say what's genuinely good in it, explain why it doesn't fit (most clients, the guarantee conditions), and log it on the D9 list. Stay narrow because most buyers don't yet know what they need; wide choice is only for buyers who already know (members picking from a bonus library). Never answer a client with "that's not how it works" alone: if a fixed step would hurt its result or its customers this week, do what's right, log it, and decide at the next D15 review whether the checklist changes for everyone; [Fixed] rules are never bent this way.
- M14 [opening a new delivery batch or offer] Pre-sell: fill the batch before delivery starts, and never take money for something the business won't deliver.
- M15 [a full delivery batch is running] One full batch must make enough before adding offers or batches. If it doesn't, raise the price.
- M16 [designing an upsell] [stage: S3+] Price an upsell at about 5x the core price, not before the first target (F3). Rollover upsells must be at least 4x the credit (M35); anchors sit at 5 to 10x (M33).
- M17 [leads feel too expensive or slow] The usual fix is higher lifetime value (continuity, price rises), not cheaper leads.
- M18 [considering affiliates or partners] Partners who sell the offer (affiliates): not before the first target, then peel off one part of the offer for them to sell at or above a minimum price. Partner bonuses (O41) are different: allowed from the first paid batch, if each takes under 2 hours to set up. Every referral commission partners pay the business is extra profit on clients already paid for. Prefer offers the business owns and can add a back end to over a better-looking split on someone else's product: any joint venture or white-label must leave the business free to sell its own next offer to the client.
- M19 [spending business money or deciding what to do with profit] Early money only on tools, implementation help and paid tests. Reinvest profit, keep a personal cash buffer, keep living costs low. Business revenue isn't personal wealth, and the first real milestone is personal savings that cover years of living costs. Build a business that runs without the owner, so it becomes an asset worth a multiple of profit. Money is wasted, stored or multiplied, and reinvested profit compounds. After the first target, reinvest first in people (T3, T1), then brand: moments that get talked about without paying directly, which wait under L4. Brand shows in three numbers, checked at each quarterly L1 re-score: cold reply rate over time, continuity take-up and renewal, and the price the business can hold. Plan personal wealth from profit taken and saved, not from a future sale. Growing revenue usually beats cutting costs. Before any spend above a threshold the owner sets in the instructions box, convert it into hours at the M1 hourly rate, estimate the extra monthly gross profit it should bring, and spend only if it pays back within 12 months on cautious inputs (cost doubled and sales halved, still paying back within 12 months and within M26's runway); log the estimate and check it on the date set (F2). If a countdown, "last places" or rival buyers create an urge to buy a tool, course or list now, wait until the next day, then run the payback check; for anything bought or bid on, write the top figure and the reason before talking, and treat bidding against others as a cue to walk away. When a client payment lands, split it straight away: tax reserve first, then a fixed personal pay, then buffer and reinvestment; keep personal pay fixed so every rise in income goes to the buffer or reinvestment, and check reserve rates with a tax adviser (see X19). As an alternative to a fixed split, keep a watermark: leave in the account the monthly cost of the next growth step plus reserves, and pay out everything above it. Take some cash out every month even while reinvesting, and treat a rising personal balance with lower personal spending as a standing goal: the cash forces real cash flow and less stressed decisions. Hold an annual-bills reserve (insurance, regulatory fees, renewals) divided by 12 and set aside monthly. After the first target, set a written split of profit invested against profit consumed. Write these money decisions once, as standing rules, so they never take attention from selling. While the business can use extra cash at this payback, it goes back in; only true surplus beyond the buffer goes to the passive side of a barbell (all active risk and attention in the business; low-risk, passive holdings run by regulated managers on the other side), aiming for a steady return. Never take an investment pitch on the spot: score it on four questions (does it keep the capital safe, is it tax efficient, does it pay out while held, does it grow), size it so a total loss wouldn't touch the buffer, and take regulated advice before acting (see X4); this is a question list, not investment advice. The further a bet sits from what the owner is skilled at (for most owners: getting clients, selling, pricing and delivering), the likelier the loss, so put active money and attention only inside that skill and treat anything outside it as a gamble, sized so a total loss is small against the owner's savings and income. When a pitch comes from a friend, or everyone seems to be making money from it, treat the urge to act fast as the cue to wait a day (as above). Before any opportunity brought by someone else, ask: if the returns are as claimed, why are they selling it rather than keeping it all; how many years they have done this exact thing themselves (under two or three years, decline); and will they put their own money into the same deal alongside the owner (if not, decline). A friend's gains are not evidence. Test with the smallest unit first, buy only what has been checked by someone independent, and prefer a surer, smaller return to a riskier, larger one. Taking no pay while "reinvesting everything" counts as reinvesting only when the owner can state the return on that capital; if they can't, the business isn't making a profit yet, so fix margin and price (M1, M2) before calling it growth. Every expense line must answer how it makes money; an upgrade (a bigger or nicer site, tool or fit-out) passes only if it beats the plain version on the payback test above. Once the business pays the owner (from S1), check a wealth ladder each quarter, one rung at a time: active income covers costs; a fixed share of personal pay goes automatically to long-term holdings once the buffer is in place; the owner holds something that pays to own it (something that pays only when sold is speculation); passive income starts to cover costs. Start small, never with borrowed money. Tax: know the law, follow the rules, document the truth; once profit is large enough, get planning from a tax adviser separately from year-end preparation, during the year, never by prepaying costs in the last weeks just to cut the bill. A business's value grows with its retained, recurring profit or revenue times the multiple buyers pay, so keeping clients and raising lifetime value often builds more wealth than taking cash out; that growth is not taxed while it is unsold, but it is taxed when the business or shares are sold, so treat it as tax deferred, never tax-free, and take advice on how a sale would be taxed (X1).
- M20 [designing the money model, or reviewing a new client] Get paid to get clients: cash collected in a client's first 30 days should exceed what it cost to win and deliver to them (the owner's hours at the M1 hourly rate plus any spend). Track it for every client; if it doesn't, fix pricing or payment terms before adding volume. The 30-day window lets cash be recycled into the next client without outside funding; the usual fix is an immediate upsell or upfront payment. Choose the way of pulling cash forward that adds the least operational drag.
- M21 [free early-client or delivery places are full] Offer free clients the choice to pay the current price or step aside for a paying client, and collect their review either way.
- M22 [deciding how much to deliver, or making delivery more efficient] Sales to fulfilment continuum: the more the business delivers, the easier it sells and the harder it is to fulfil. Create flow, monetise flow, then add friction: overdeliver on the first offers to get cash and demand moving, then use that cash to systemise, keeping the same promise while changing the how. Only once people are saying yes, add friction or deliver less for the same price. Overdelivering stays within the owner's set hours (F1). When delivery outgrows those hours, first move more of the core offer into one-to-many assets and group sessions; hiring deliverers is the second route, not the default (D5). Before adding a delivery batch, audit session length and the gaps around sessions to fit more clients into the hours cap (F1); keep a change only if results hold. Where it fits the offer, paid delivery defaults to a small group, with one-to-one kept for the premium tier (M7); check it against the M1 margin bar on the first clients. Label every early-client extra (one-to-one time, fast replies, extra sessions) in writing from the start as temporary with an end date; cut service levels only for new batches, and existing clients keep what they were sold until their term ends (see X3): taking back what people were given provokes more than never giving it. When the business does something extra, say briefly and truthfully what it took, never inflated (O11) and never as a debt to repay (L46, M27). Free early clients follow the paid offer's structure (sessions, tasks, timeline), with any extra support logged separately in hours, so the gap to the paid version is known.
- M23 [judging whether advertising or outreach is profitable] Measure lifetime gross profit (everything a client ever pays minus everything it costs to deliver, including the owner's hours at the M1 hourly rate) against the full cost to acquire them. 3:1 is the minimum, not the target. Each human step in attraction, conversion or delivery raises the ratio needed: 3:1 with no human steps, 6:1 with one, 9:1 with two, 12:1 with all three. Count the business's human steps: 3:1 stays the floor until the first target, and the matching higher ratio is needed before scaling with hires. Lifetime value is roughly annual gross profit divided by annual churn, capped where demand is expected to fade (M10). If cost per customer is within 3x the industry average, work on lifetime value; above that, fix advertising or sales. Of the two levers, raising lifetime gross profit beats cutting cost per customer, which hits diminishing returns first. Whoever makes the most per client can spend the most to win them. Work out lifetime gross profit simply: last year's total gross profit divided by the number of clients (it underestimates repeat buyers, which is the safe side). A very high early ratio (10:1 or more) signals room to scale: push volume while it lasts, because ratios fall as volume rises and absolute return is what counts. An unusually low cost per customer from a new method is usually temporary: plan the money model on a normal cost per customer, not the best month. Once members have left, cross-check lifetime value with the average total paid by churned members, and the implied churn as the periodic fee divided by that average. On the first clients, compute lifetime gross profit against full acquisition cost and record the ratio: human outbound must clear 3:1 on it. Acquisition cost is everything spent to win clients in the period (media, marketing and sales pay, commissions, tools) divided by new clients won; use the last 12 months where there is the history, to smooth out good and bad weeks. With little history, estimate churn from one cohort: of the clients paying at the start of last month, how many still pay 30 days later. To cut acquisition cost, work the funnel front to back (cost of attention, the hook and the offer, then later steps), because early steps touch the most people; jump to a later step only when it is far below the norm for the field. Two speed checks sit beside the ratio. First, cash in the first 30 days: aim for gross profit collected from a new client within 30 days of at least twice what it cost to win and deliver to them, so each client pays for themselves and for the next one and cash stops limiting growth (M20 sets the 1x floor; prepayment with a real bonus, M37, M40, and a fitting next offer, M31, raise it). Second, the cost of a permanent client: divide the cost to win a client by the share who stay two years or more (if 1 in 10 stays, the real cost is 10 times the cost per client); compare that with what a permanent client is worth, and aim outreach at the traits the permanent clients share, even if each lead costs more.

### Daily numbers
- M46 [every working day, any "what next" question, any plan, or any result reported] Daily numbers: the business runs on one ratio, lifetime gross profit to acquisition cost (M1, M23), and every day starts by updating it and choosing the next action from it. Keep one numbers sheet, one row a day: new clients won; spend to win them (media, sales and marketing pay, commissions, tools); acquisition cost, rolling 30 days and 12 months (M23); price, delivery cost per client and monthly churn, giving lifetime gross profit per client (M1); the ratio against its bar (3:1, higher with each human step, M23); and the funnel counts (reached, replied or clicked, booked, showed, closed, L30). Missing figures are estimated, marked "Proposed, not decided", and replaced by real ones as they come. Then pick the next action by this order and name one action and the number to check tomorrow: (1) too little data to read (below L43's sample) means keep the daily inputs (L41) and log; (2) ratio below its bar with acquisition cost far above the field's norm, or one funnel step far below its norm, means fix acquisition front to back (offer, hook, targeting, then the weak step, M23); (3) ratio below its bar otherwise means raise lifetime gross profit (price, delivery cost, churn, then upsells and the back end, M1, M24, M31); (4) ratio at or above its bar with delivery capacity free means more volume on what works (L62, F3); (5) ratio high but delivery full means fix capacity before more volume (D11, O34). Any activity that does not raise lifetime gross profit or cut acquisition cost waits (F2).
