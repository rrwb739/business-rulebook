## Sales (C), continued

- C8 [a prospect hesitates, or the call gets lost in detail] Best case, worst case close: best case, the problem is solved for good; worst case, the guarantee protects them (O8); the only sure loss is changing nothing. When a call gets lost in detail, say "let's zoom out" and go to best case, worst case.
- C9 [a prospect asks for more proof] [Fixed] Ask how much proof would be enough and whether that's reasonable, then find the real fear behind it. Raise the doubt first ("you're probably wondering whether this works for someone like you. I'd wonder too. What would you need to see?"), then meet that criterion if it's reasonable and true. Once a client agrees in writing, offer a short reference call with them (ideally outside the prospect's market), with no undisclosed reward to the referee (X3), no introduction not cleared, no deadline unless real (O37), and contact details shared only with consent (see X8). Once 2 or 3 have agreed, offer the list and let the prospect pick, never two direct rivals; for a sceptic, suggest a reference who had the same doubt. Lighter version: the prospect sends one written question to a consenting client, answered in the client's own words and passed on unedited, with any perk disclosed (X3), and the question logged under L59.
- C10 [a prospect says no or stalls] Resolve the concern, then ask again, as many times as each concern gets resolved, and only once the reason is found and resolved. Use a past client story and play the future out. Stack answers from different angles while rapport holds; if they close off or want to end the call, end it and book a follow-up (M27): whoever asks most sells most, but rapport sets the limit. Expect a first no; it starts the close rather than ending it. The objection playbook is C14. Universal closes come before any objection-specific answer, since most sales happen at the first ask or after one or two quick logistical concerns, and most real losses happen earlier (discovery, the frame, preparation): train these few lines before a tree of answers per objection. In order, whatever the prospect says: (0) "Totally hear you. Mind if I ask a quick question? Do you think this can get you closer to your goal?", and if yes, ask again; (1) "What's your main concern?"; then one of these, none of which tells the prospect they're wrong: the reason close ("that might be the very reason to do it", used only where it is true for this buyer); the hypothetical ("if this were a 10 out of 10, would you do it? What's different between that and what you have in mind?"); the zoom out ("you want X, and this is built to get you X; what's getting lost?"); the 1 to 10 ("why not a 10?", which lists every reason left); best case and worst case (only where a real guarantee or refund exists, O8, X11). Ask for the sale again after each. After three or four noes, say "no worries" and book the next call (C11). Never use the reason close on someone who has said they can't afford it, and never push past a clear no (M27, X9); the type-specific answers in C14 are for concerns that are a real gap in information, such as a partner who needs to see the terms.
- C11 [the end of any call] Book the next step before hanging up, and ask "who do you know who'd benefit from this?" Ask for the next step with low-pressure yes-framed questions ("would it help if...?"), and test a "would you be opposed to...?" version against it (L63). After a no or a "not now", one light ask for a three-way introduction is fine only if the call gave them something useful: ask once, never for a list of names, and drop it at the first hesitation (L66 consent and X2 apply before contacting anyone named). After a no on a call, ask once before hanging up whether they would give 15 minutes later to say what would have made it a yes, and offer to pay for their time where that is normal in the market; treat it as research, never a second pitch, and log the answers for C14 and L59. Never contact anyone who has opted out (L12, X2).
- C12 [negotiating price or terms] [Fixed] Have an alternative first: whoever needs the deal less has the upper hand, so never look as if the owner needs the sale. Anchor first and high, but credible: an opening the other side sees as absurd loses good faith and its concessions aren't returned. Move in small steps, trade on terms (start date, payment timing, scope) rather than price, and offer two or three equal options. When asked "can you do it for less?", answer with the opposite question, "can you do it for more?", meaning what more would make it worth it to them, and if a lower price is still needed, take something real out of the scope or terms to match it, never the same thing for less (M2). Frame the price as an investment with a return. Every step down is a real trade, never a staged retreat from a position never held (M27, O11; see X9); when a real trade comes from the buyer's request, say so plainly, and log outcomes for those deals against the rest (L63). Name one small, true weak point in the terms early, then trade on what is strong. When another party proposes terms the owner can't accept, don't argue: say the owner would likely do the same in their place, set out one page of the true advantages and disadvantages of each option for them, and let them decide (O11). When a buyer compares on price with a cheaper rival, ask: "If we were the same price as them, which would you choose?" and then "Why?"; their own reasons are the answer, said back lightly: "Right, and that's why we're more." Use it only where the reasons they give are true.Before any negotiation, list every term that could move (price, payment timing and method, start or completion date, speed, scope, risk carried by each side, effort asked of each side, extras included) so there are more things to trade than the other side expects; concede one term at a time in return for movement on theirs, holding the price as long as possible. When buying, get several bids before choosing a supplier, and borrow good terms seen in one bid when talking to the others. When offered several options, counter with one that combines the parts of each that matter most to the owner. When the other side makes a mistake (a defect, a missed date), ask what putting it right would cost them, then ask for that amount as the reduction. After the owner accepts the other side's first number, say plainly if true that the owner would not have gone further, so they don't feel they left money behind; never say it if untrue (X9).
- C13 [before a block of sales calls, or practising sales] Practise selling like any skill (F24), scaled to the owner's set hours: 15 to 20 minutes before each call block reading the script aloud with the tone marked, drilling replies to the top objections and reviewing part of one recorded call. Know the script and stories so well they come without thinking, so attention stays on listening; for objections, learn the reasoning per type and rehearse two or three answers in the owner's own words (C14). Fix one line at a time with 10 to 30 reps, then three clean runs. Train back to front: rehearse taking payment and the ask first. Role-play before going live, especially price, guarantee and top objections, with the partner told to stop the owner at the first slip and make them redo the line. Rehearse in call conditions (the real device, standing, the real call sheet and lead record, the real opener). Rotate each session through intro, discovery, pitch, close and objections, then the weakest step, about 10 reps each; a clearly broken step gets every session until fixed. Work on one skill for 1 to 2 weeks, chosen monthly from an L30 number and written down with the rep plan. Write a rubric for each CLOSER step (what a 1, 5, 7 and 10 look like), score each role-play and one recorded call a week against it, and once a month have someone outside the business who is ahead in sales (F15) score one role-play. Tone: questions rise, the ask goes slow and low, emphasis changes meaning, so mark it in the script and pick up the pace just before the close. Keep a one-line approach per common buyer type: same script, facts and price (M2), different pace and emphasis; match the buyer's pace and terms in the owner's own voice, never mimicking (W1). After each call, critique first, then what went well, then what to change. Score each recorded call against a yes or no checklist of every C5 step, count interruptions and fix the first missed step. Keep a "don't say" list of lines that lost momentum, each with its replacement, read before each block; start it with "I'll prove to you...", "let me show you why..." and opinion words such as "obviously" or "clearly" in discovery, and mark any sigh or tone that signalled disagreement. Once a week, role-play the hostile call (a hang-up, "how did you get this number?", "take me off your list", a dismissive buyer), answering every opt-out per X2. Add a drill-down role-play that asks ever deeper questions until the owner reaches the L16 "I'll check and reply in writing" line (X4). Practise on lower-scoring prospects inside the avatar, never outside it (L2), to the full standard and never prejudging who can pay. Keep the diary full so no call feels make-or-break. Before the first live block, run one concentrated practice week; after any F12 update or new offer version, a focused week on the changed lines. Rehearse the origin story and three belief stories weekly, each under 60 seconds (F13). Monthly, review that month's consented recordings, won and lost together, one question per CLOSER stage, and turn the patterns into script lines, don't-say entries and next month's skill (no client data, X5; see X8). On calls: stand and smile, keep self-view or a mirror, act as if when the feeling isn't there, and never let manner stand in for substance (C4, L16); when an objection lands, take one slow breath and a one-word reset ("huh, tell me more"); load the next lead between calls, never during; take 1 to 2 minutes of movement between calls; cap each block's length and keep research and admin out of it; log dials per hour (L30). Judge every call, the owner's or a rep's, first on adherence to the script and the C5 checklist, not on whether it closed: a close won by going off-script is a false positive and is never copied into the script, and a lost call that followed the script is a script problem, not a person problem. Change the script only once adherence is steady and the close rate still lags (L16). With a team, recognise adherence each day with the manager's specific approval and, if wanted, a small token reward paid whether or not the call closed, alongside pay for results (T1; see X18, X19). After a lost sale, ask what wasn't found out, never as a reason to push harder (M27). Never pose as a buyer to shop rivals' calls. Within the owner's set hours, practise curiosity in ordinary conversations (questions only, nothing logged about the person). Train new sellers first on recordings of good calls (recorded with consent, X8), then on a reduced call schedule, reviewing each call with the manager by asking the seller first what they would do better; in weekly team reviews peers name only what went well, and the manager drills one part of the script at a time, repeated until it comes without thinking, before moving to the next part. When the owner closes far better than the sales reps, move the reps up the pipeline: they set qualified appointments and the owner closes, while each rep practises on recordings and role-play until their close rate on the same script nears the owner's, and only then takes their own closes (T1). To memorise a script, read it aloud, black out one word, read it again, and repeat until the whole page is blacked out. In role-play, stop the rep at the moment of each mistake, show the better line, have them say it again correctly, then repeat it a few times to lock it in; feedback saved for the end of a full role-play is mostly forgotten. To turn a sales team around: first get everyone saying the same script (cut it hard, often by half), since a script nobody follows can't be improved; then train by playing recordings of the best calls and drilling one part of the script at a time with instant correction ("good, again") until it is automatic, before moving to the next part. The best closer is often a poor manager: pick a sales lead who enjoys teaching and developing people. In a sales-led business the owner should be able to run the process, so no one who runs sales can hold the business to ransom. Record calls from the start (lawfully, X8) so a seller's best streak is captured, and keep a playlist of the best calls to study what changed in pauses, questions and tone.Coach delivery with five plain controls only. Three stay constant on every call: loud enough to hear easily, steady and unhurried pace, every word fully pronounced (which also slows a nervous fast talker). Two vary with meaning: pauses and pitch. Mark them in the script itself: "..." for a short pause before a point, a full stop for a full stop, "?" where the voice rises to hand the turn over; the longest pause in the call comes straight after the ask. Give feedback in those terms ("speak louder", "slow down", "pause after the price"), never in labels such as "sound more confident" or "use a curious tone". A script that sounds read is a script not yet memorised. Time the full pitch at the owner's normal pace so call lengths become predictable.To make every rep follow the best rep's process, turn it into a short sequence of screens shown to the buyer, read in the same words each time, or a checklist on a clipboard the rep ticks as they go and files with the signed agreement; test it at one site or with one rep before rolling it out. Size the sales team to its calendar: if sellers take far fewer calls than they can handle, reduce the team to the strongest closers (lawfully, X18) rather than spreading calls thin, since a seller with too few calls gets desperate, talks more and listens less. Keep prospecting and show-up work as separate jobs: callers who set appointments shouldn't also chase reminders; one person owns show-up (reminders, a three-way introduction between the caller and the closer, and the closer's personal message on the morning of the call, C3).
- C14 [an obstacle, objection or stall on a call] [Fixed] Understand the reason behind each type rather than memorising closes, and keep two or three answers per type in the owner's own words, rehearsed (O9, C13). Five types: time, money, fit, other people and self (fear of choosing wrong, or not knowing how to decide). Expect several layers, each answer revealing the next; blame moves from outside in (circumstances, other people, self), so the layer shows how close the real reason is, and "I need to think about it" means the owner is now talking to the decision maker. Choose the answer by whether the hesitation is about the past, present or future, and answer on its own dimension (money with money, time with time, fit with fit). With any of them: curiosity first ("tell me more"), then acknowledge (saying their last phrase back is enough), associate (a true similar case or story, or "fair question"), then ask; never answer in the same breath, and mark this on the call sheet (L16). When stuck, slowly say their question back. If the owner wins the argument, the sale is lost. Never answer a fear with "don't worry": give one claims-file fact (O47), the real support terms (O8) and one true story. For a factual objection, agree fully with the condition first, then ask true yes questions from published sources or their own numbers so they reach the conclusion themselves, never a trick (M27). Ask "why?" until the underlying reason appears, then show how the offer serves it better; the reason given not to act is usually the reason to act. Belief-breaking formula, for the pre-call video, emails and calls: what many people think, why that's not quite right, what works instead, an example, then one plain line restating the new belief from the claims file (O47); on a call, said to the buyer's own view, use "in some cases that holds; here it looks different, because...". Examples, associations and quoted sources must be real, accurate and never imply endorsement (O11, O15, O25; see X9); name a past client only with written consent, otherwise describe them. Keep a story bank: one true story per objection type (consenting clients or the owner's own experience, never composite or invented), each a one-line plot (who, what they wanted, what stood in the way) plus where and how it felt, in a 30 to 60-second and a 2 to 5-minute version; grow it to 10 to 20 false beliefs from the objection log and L59, leaving a slot empty rather than invent one. Nudges: "you'll fix this eventually, so would you rather it pays off faster or slower?"; for perfectionists, "will this get you closer to your goal than what you're doing now?"; not deciding is itself a decision. After the price, light humour can point out a contradiction, then a serious "what's really going on?". Before the close they must want the goal and believe that this gets them there in the way they want, that the people around them will back it, and that it will work for someone like them. Answers by type: time is priority (everyone has the same hours, so ask how important this is and what it would replace); preference, wanting the result their own way, means changing the method's variables changes the outcome; money goes logic (is the result worth the price?), then proof from buyers as similar to them as possible, then payment terms; a stall gets "what are you afraid of having happen?", then three plain questions (more likely to succeed doing it than not? closer to the goal with help than alone? access to the money?), stopping at any honest no; a past bad experience gets "one bad provider doesn't make every provider wrong; don't let it cost you twice". Other people: where the purchase needs someone else, invite them to the call; otherwise ask what that person would object to and answer those points, and leave the decision with the buyer and their partner. Never press a buyer to decide without or against a partner who shares the money, and never use lines like "better to ask forgiveness than permission" or "you're asking for permission, not support" (see X9). A delayed payment or cooling-off period is offered only on real terms (X3). Ask questions rather than make statements when a concern comes up: a statement gives them something to argue with, a question lets them reason it through. Agree with whatever is true in the concern first ("you're right, it isn't cheap, and it's real work"), then ask what would make them feel completely sure, and meet those criteria only where the owner truly can. Stay relaxed and willing to walk away: if it isn't right for them, say so; a clear decision either way is a good call. A story of a past client in the same position is used only if it is real and consented (X13, O25). When the offer looks like a commodity and the objection is price, widen the comparison before discussing price: ask about the things the buyer hadn't considered (how staff are vetted and trained, insurance, what is included, whether the total is capped), each one true of this business, then offer the cheaper tier if they still only care about price; claims about rivals stay general and true (X9).
- C15 [selling to a large organisation with several people in the decision] Thread through the organisation from any contact who engages (someone who downloads a report, attends a session or replies): ask what initiative it is for, what the goal is and who else is involved, and get introductions upward and sideways. Pitch so each person looks good to their own boss and carries no job risk: say what the change does for their targets and how the downside is covered. Frame the buying cycle as a set number of working hours (a fixed list of meetings and approvals), not months on the calendar, and agree that list with the buyer so it can be done in weeks; every meeting ends with the next one booked (C11), and a deal waiting on a reply with no meeting in the diary counts as at risk. Land in one division, deliver, then expand to sister divisions and regions with the first one's result as proof. Hiring people who used to work in a target organisation can open doors, but only with no confidential information or client lists brought across and any restrictive terms in their old contract respected (X8, X18). Gated industry reports and research suit large-organisation buyers as lead magnets (L44); give the finance approver the cost case in their terms (M19, C7).
