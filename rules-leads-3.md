## Leads and marketing (L), continued

### Lead magnets (continued)
- L47 [writing any call to action] [Fixed] A call to action has two parts: exactly what to do (clear, simple, direct, such as "reply YES and I'll send it") and reasons to do it now. Use only real scarcity (the owner's real capacity, O36) and real urgency (bonuses that truly expire, rotated, O37) (see X10), and give a reason after "because": a weak reason can carry a small ask, but a big decision needs a real one. Ask often, one call to action at a time: a confused mind doesn't buy. Make acting quick and easy (a short, memorable web address and booking link). For a prospect who is keen but busy, send one line they can complete: "Reply with a day, a time and how many minutes you can give me." Make the ask about personalisation, not more of the same ("want me to help apply this to your business?" beats "want more of these?"). Put a call to action inside the lead magnet itself, and before and after it. For the next step, use consistency: remind them what they just did and show the next step follows from it. Build small say-do loops through the process ("do this, and X happens"), each completed before the big ask, and check each against four tests: they do something rather than just agree, someone else sees it, it takes some real effort, and they chose it freely without a big reward or pressure; fix the weakest first. After they act, give one true line of approval for what they did, never a line that presents others' inaction as normal (O47). In long content (videos, podcasts, live streams), place short calls to action of a few seconds at the start, the middle and the end (more mid-points in long pieces), and keep a bank of several wordings for each kind of ask (review, share, lead magnet, event, offer) to rotate, so the asks feel natural and the audience doesn't learn to tune them out; judge them on the action counted (reviews, shares, opt-ins), never on views.

### Cold outreach
- L48 [cold outreach in general] Strangers create three problems, solved in order: (1) there is no way to contact them, so build a targeted list; (2) they ignore the owner, so personalise and give big, fast value; (3) they aren't interested yet, so contact them more times, in more ways, until they're ready. Sell expensive things in steps: first get a raised hand, then book a time to sell. When the lead magnet gives value on its own, the next call is about the value they got from it. It's a numbers game: once the owner knows how much outreach makes one engaged lead, the job is to do more. A rude reply or a no-show changes nothing: finish the day's list.
- L49 [building the cold list] Get lists in this order: lead-scraping software (test a sample of a few hundred from each tool; if details are current and people reply, pull all you can); list brokers (test their samples, keep what performs); lists built by hand from directories and groups (freshest because least contacted, but slowest; outsource once the owner understands it). Aim to assemble the first 1,000 names; with more time than money, start by building lists by hand. Interest groups and communities where the avatar gathers are the warmest cold audience; prefer contacting members outside the group so the owner doesn't look as if they're milking it. Target as precisely as L2 allows. Bought or scraped lists still need checking before use (see X8, X2).
- L50 [writing any cold message] Personalise: 1 to 3 facts about the prospect's business that a friend might know, a genuine compliment, and ideally how their work helped the owner; make the cold message look like a warm one, never disguised, always identifiable as the owner with an opt-out (see X2). Plain text only, on every messaging channel (no emojis, images, banners, bold or heavy templates): one person writing to another. Before sending a new template, read it on a phone as well as a computer: the first line, the ask and the opt-out must all show without zooming or scrolling. Research every lead before messaging, in batches. Then give big, fast value within 30 seconds: give away free something the avatar actually pays for, not something they "should" pay for. When the owner is unknown, a genuinely useful give before any ask does more than rapport: lead with the named asset, never small talk. If the offer or lead magnet isn't working, add value until saying no feels stupid. Scripts: calls one to two pages at most, emails under half a page. Send before polishing (L16): with the right callout and a strong offer little copy is needed, and simpler words win (L11). Follow-up call opener: say their name and pause; one personal detail and a compliment; who the owner is; once pilots exist, a similar nearby client and its real result (O11); ask whether they face the same thing; a soft yes-framed question ("would it help if...?"); then offer two set times and ask which works. For prospects who have already acted (used the asset, replied), open on that action with one specific question about it, and test that against the similar-client opener once pilots exist (L63). Give one true local anchor early (region or town), never a home address. Where true, add one line of genuine common ground from the prospect's own business sources, stated once, never stretched, invented or personal (C4, F21). When someone has replied or booked, remind them exactly what they did. On follow-up calls: at most one immediate redial; voicemails left by hand, about 20 seconds (name, local anchor, the message being followed up, one useful line, the number twice, no pitch; any reason to call back is a true, specific change that affects them, never invented urgency or a vague "you won't believe what happened"), with callbacks logged; call from a local number, always shown, and log pick-up rate by number type (see X6). No exclamation marks. Assume busy, not rude: answer small talk warmly and briefly, then move on. Every compliment rests on one checkable, specific fact from the prospect's own site, reviews, published roles or public history, ideally something they visibly put work into or that the owner can credibly judge, saying why it stood out; keep it plain, never lavish or generic, and with no true specific, leave it out (W1, O11). Use names, the business's exact name and any post-nominals as the prospect publishes them (L8 check). When a prospect asks where the owner got their number or details, answer at once and truthfully: name the actual source (the directory, register, website or person who introduced them), then the true fit criteria from L2 that put them on the list, so the call turns to whether they fit rather than why they were called; never hide, blur or put off the source, never suggest a referral that didn't happen, and offer to remove them on request (see X8, X2). Treat whoever answers a call (receptionist, junior staff) as warmly as the decision maker: ask their name, use it, thank them, greet them by name next time, log name and role only (see X8), and use no tricks to get past them. Tests (L63): the yes-framed question against a no-framed version; an informal against a formal greeting for the avatar's usual age group, signing off with the first name either way; a stated short time on the call opener, kept to; and, for prospects within reach, one true dated window when the owner will actually be nearby, within the owner's set hours (O11). When a prospect has seen a message and not replied, the warm close-off (L12) may go as one short voice or video note, only on a channel the conversation already uses (C3) and only where messaging them is lawful (X2: in the UK a voice or video message counts as electronic mail, so an individual needs consent or the soft opt-in). It is said in the owner's own voice (W1): no pressure and no guilt about not replying, a plain line that it's fine, then one sentence naming what the owner does and for whom, so they can file the contact under it and get in touch when they need it. Say only what is true: if the owner plans the 3 to 6 month recontact (L12), don't promise silence. Never fake familiarity or affection the relationship doesn't have, and use read receipts only where the platform openly shows them to both sides. Test (L63): the voice or video close-off against the same words as text, on replies and bookings per 100 seen-but-silent prospects; never assume it books, since a claimed rate from someone else is not evidence for this business. A strong opener for a service the prospect may already buy: ask whether they already have someone doing it, with an answer ready for both replies; someone already paying for the service is a better prospect, not a worse one, because they already buy the category (offer what beats their current provider, O6). Where a nearby or peer client has given permission, name them in the first seconds ("I look after [client]'s ..."), since a shared contact makes the stranger more polite and willing to listen; never name a client without their permission (X9).
- L51 [deciding how much to automate] Automate delivery (templates, recorded videos) and distribution (sending tools) as far as is ethical, but personalisation wins replies, and the smaller the universe of leads, the less to automate: estimate the universe before choosing. Research depth scales with deal size: hours of research per lead suit a small universe of high-value buyers only if lifetime gross profit per client still clears L54's 3:1 after counting the research time for every lead needed to win one client (a low reply rate multiplies it); log minutes per lead (L8) and cap them from that sum. If messages are AI-personalised and tool-sent at volume (L7), a checked lead detail and a daily hand-checked sample carry the personalisation. Spend 10 to 20% of outreach effort testing new tools (for example one day a week on a new research or sending tool), compared with the standard.
- L52 [following up] [Fixed] Contact every lead more than once, in more than one way: for example email first, then a follow-up email, then a call that follows up on the email (L4). Plan for 2 to 3 conversations before a prospect buys. Act like the owner is really trying to reach them. Anyone who asks to stop is never contacted again (see X2, X6). When the list is finished, start again from the top, and recontact non-responders after 3 to 6 months: they may have missed it, been busy, or their situation changed. When pilot places open, recontact earlier non-responders (never anyone who said no or opted out, L12) with an honest admission: there was no proof to show them before, so they had no reason to believe the owner, and the owner will run it free for a few clients in exchange for honest feedback.
- L53 [cold outreach benchmarks] Treat any published outreach benchmark (open, reply, pick-up and acceptance rates) as a placeholder only, and replace it with the owner's own after 100 to 300 contacts per channel; any figure for a new medium (such as letters, L4) is likewise a placeholder. Judge email on replies, not opens: mail privacy features inflate open rates. Later, test personal video or voice memos inside emails (their name, one personal line, the standard message): film in a setting the avatar would recognise, dressed as they would be, with the owner's name, one true line on how they got good at this (F21, O11), a numbered free asset, then "want me to apply this to your business?"; claim it takes less time than they think only once pilots prove it (O15).
- L54 [cold outreach costs and allocation] Nearly all the cost is labour plus software and data: add them up and divide by customers won to get the cost per customer, and aim for at least 3:1 lifetime gross profit to that cost, more where steps need a human (M23). If anyone else works the leads: pay a base rate plus commission per shown appointment, give every rep the same weekly allocation of about 70% of what they could handle, set absolute quotas (leads in, appointments out), and work every lead fully: no lead left behind. Pay commission on cash collected, and celebrate only processed sales. Watch for cherry-picking, especially by outsourced closers. At team stage, rate two alternatives: give the best leads to proven top closers, or specialise setters by lead source with general closers and named accounts for every rep.
- L55 [deciding if cold outreach is working, or scaling it] Benefits to remember: it needs one great message, not a stream of content; competitors can't see it; it's reliable; it doesn't depend on platform rules or on the owner being on camera, which makes the business more sellable; and it's hard to copy. Work backwards from the sales goal: customers needed x contacts per customer = total contacts, divided by working days = daily contacts; allow for efficiency falling as volume rises by planning for up to twice the straight-line figure, and in a small market widen the region sooner (L2). Most people badly underestimate the volume and time it needs. Shadow whoever is best and double their inputs before experimenting: replicate before you iterate, ideally alongside someone who has already done it. Once a month, compare the owner's outreach count with someone ahead in cold outreach: volume habits from other work don't carry over.

### Warm outreach
- L56 [contacting people who already know the owner: replied prospects, pilots, members, opted-in contacts; or the warm list runs low] Warm outreach is the cheapest, easiest source of engaged leads. Ten steps: (1) get the list (every contact on every platform); (2) start on the platform with the most; (3) personalise with a genuine reason to get in touch; (4) reach out to 100 people a day, up to 3 times each, once a day, until they reply (by post, once a week); (5) when they reply, acknowledge what they said by restating it in the owner's own words (echoing their exact word for any feeling, L13), compliment them, ideally on a character trait, and ask a question that steers towards the offer, letting them talk about themselves; (6) after 3 to 4 exchanges, make an offer to test interest, ideally asking "do you know anyone who...?" rather than pitching them; (7) make yes easy; (8) when the list is done, start the next platform; (9) start charging once people refer others; (10) keep the list warm with regular value. When the warm list runs low, build a lukewarm list inside this same channel (L4): each day write one short post on the owner's main platform, then leave about 100 thoughtful, useful comments (never a pitch or link, within each group's or page's rules) on posts where the avatar already gathers, such as groups or communities for their trade; everyone who likes or replies to those comments, or likes or comments on the owner's post, has taken an action and joins the list. Open with one question about what drew them to the post or comment, then follow steps 5 to 7. A private message on a social platform is electronic mail under PECR, so to an individual it carries no marketing until they ask or consent, and to a business contact it follows X2 (identify the owner, offer an opt-out); log each source (L14) and judge this list on replies and calls booked per 100 contacts, against the warm list (L30).
- L57 [re-engaging the owner's own list] The nine-word email: "Are you still looking to [their desire in about four words]?", with no links or images. Treat everyone who replies as an engaged lead and contact them first. Send only to contacts the owner may lawfully email (see X2). After the first target: a short, tactical email every fortnight to the opted-in list only (L36). Format to test there: "I made this for you", what it does, enjoy, and one call to action, in plain text with one link and the opt-out; never for cold email (L10). [shape: e-commerce] For a product without natural repeat purchases, keep the opted-in list warm so past buyers come back for the next project: about two emails a week, rotating a before-and-after, a striking example and one answer to a question non-buyers often ask; each with a clear subject, an immediate reward at the top (an image or result), one link and a light P.S.; judged on repeat orders and revenue per email. For online shops: automated sequences triggered by behaviour (product viewed, basket abandoned, purchase) plus a regular plain-text email to the whole list at least weekly, raising the frequency only while open, click and complaint rates and the sending domain's health hold; the first email after sign-up or purchase asks for a reply (it helps delivery) and links to something worth clicking; content can be entertainment or the outcome the product gives (recognition, a gift landing well) rather than the product itself; marketing email needs consent or the soft opt-in (X2). In follow-up to people who haven't bought, answer one objection per email, in order of how common it is; image-led emails suit low-price physical products, plain text suits complex or high-price offers; birthday or anniversary offers give a true personal reason to buy (with consent for the data, X2, X8).
