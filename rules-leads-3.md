## Leads and marketing (L), continued

### Lead magnets (continued)
- L47 [writing any call to action] [Fixed] A call to action has two parts: exactly what to do (clear, simple, direct, such as "reply YES and I'll send it") and reasons to do it now. Use only real scarcity (the owner's real capacity, O36) and real urgency (bonuses that truly expire, rotated, O37) (see X10), and give a reason after "because": a weak reason can carry a small ask, but a big decision needs a real one. Ask often, one call to action at a time: a confused mind doesn't buy. Make acting quick and easy (a short, memorable web address and booking link). For a prospect who is keen but busy, send one line they can complete: "Reply with a day, a time and how many minutes you can give me." Make the ask about personalisation, not more of the same ("want me to help apply this to your business?" beats "want more of these?"). Put a call to action inside the lead magnet itself, and before and after it. For the next step, use consistency: remind them what they just did and show the next step follows from it. Build small say-do loops through the process ("do this, and X happens"), each completed before the big ask, and check each against four tests: they do something rather than just agree, someone else sees it, it takes some real effort, and they chose it freely without a big reward or pressure; fix the weakest first. After they act, give one true line of approval for what they did, never a line that presents others' inaction as normal (O47).

### Cold outreach
- L48 [cold outreach in general] Strangers create three problems, solved in order: (1) there is no way to contact them, so build a targeted list; (2) they ignore the owner, so personalise and give big, fast value; (3) they aren't interested yet, so contact them more times, in more ways, until they're ready. Sell expensive things in steps: first get a raised hand, then book a time to sell. When the lead magnet gives value on its own, the next call is about the value they got from it. It's a numbers game: once the owner knows how much outreach makes one engaged lead, the job is to do more. A rude reply or a no-show changes nothing: finish the day's list.
- L49 [building the cold list] Get lists in this order: lead-scraping software (test a sample of a few hundred from each tool; if details are current and people reply, pull all you can); list brokers (test their samples, keep what performs); lists built by hand from directories and groups (freshest because least contacted, but slowest; outsource once the owner understands it). Aim to assemble the first 1,000 names; with more time than money, start by building lists by hand. Interest groups and communities where the avatar gathers are the warmest cold audience; prefer contacting members outside the group so the owner doesn't look as if they're milking it. Target as precisely as L2 allows. Bought or scraped lists still need checking before use (see X8, X2).
- L50 [writing any cold message] Personalise: 1 to 3 facts about the prospect's business that a friend might know, a genuine compliment, and ideally how their work helped the owner; make the cold message look like a warm one, never disguised, always identifiable as the owner with an opt-out (see X2). Plain text only, on every messaging channel (no emojis, images, banners, bold or heavy templates): one person writing to another. Before sending a new template, read it on a phone as well as a computer: the first line, the ask and the opt-out must all show without zooming or scrolling. Research every lead before messaging, in batches. Then give big, fast value within 30 seconds: give away free something the avatar actually pays for, not something they "should" pay for. When the owner is unknown, a genuinely useful give before any ask does more than rapport: lead with the named asset, never small talk. If the offer or lead magnet isn't working, add value until saying no feels stupid. Scripts: calls one to two pages at most, emails under half a page. Send before polishing (L16): with the right callout and a strong offer little copy is needed, and simpler words win (L11). Follow-up call opener: say their name and pause; one personal detail and a compliment; who the owner is; once pilots exist, a similar nearby client and its real result (O11); ask whether they face the same thing; a soft yes-framed question ("would it help if...?"); then offer two set times and ask which works. For prospects who have already acted (used the asset, replied), open on that action with one specific question about it, and test that against the similar-client opener once pilots exist (L63). Give one true local anchor early (region or town), never a home address. Where true, add one line of genuine common ground from the prospect's own business sources, stated once, never stretched, invented or personal (C4, F21). When someone has replied or booked, remind them exactly what they did. On follow-up calls: at most one immediate redial; voicemails left by hand, about 20 seconds (name, local anchor, the message being followed up, one useful line, the number twice, no pitch; any reason to call back is a true, specific change that affects them, never invented urgency or a vague "you won't believe what happened"), with callbacks logged; call from a local number, always shown, and log pick-up rate by number type (see X6). No exclamation marks. Assume busy, not rude: answer small talk warmly and briefly, then move on. Every compliment rests on one checkable, specific fact from the prospect's own site, reviews, published roles or public history, ideally something they visibly put work into or that the owner can credibly judge, saying why it stood out; keep it plain, never lavish or generic, and with no true specific, leave it out (W1, O11). Use names, the business's exact name and any post-nominals as the prospect publishes them (L8 check). When a prospect asks where the owner got their number or details, answer at once and truthfully: name the actual source (the directory, register, website or person who introduced them), then the true fit criteria from L2 that put them on the list, so the call turns to whether they fit rather than why they were called; never hide, blur or put off the source, never suggest a referral that didn't happen, and offer to remove them on request (see X8, X2). Treat whoever answers a call (receptionist, junior staff) as warmly as the decision maker: ask their name, use it, thank them, greet them by name next time, log name and role only (see X8), and use no tricks to get past them. Tests (L63): the yes-framed question against a no-framed version; an informal against a formal greeting for the avatar's usual age group, signing off with the first name either way; a stated short time on the call opener, kept to; and, for prospects within reach, one true dated window when the owner will actually be nearby, within the owner's set hours (O11). When a prospect has seen a message and not replied, the warm close-off (L12) may go as one short voice or video note, only on a channel the conversation already uses (C3) and only where messaging them is lawful (X2: in the UK a voice or video message counts as electronic mail, so an individual needs consent or the soft opt-in). It is said in the owner's own voice (W1): no pressure and no guilt about not replying, a plain line that it's fine, then one sentence naming what the owner does and for whom, so they can file the contact under it and get in touch when they need it. Say only what is true: if the owner plans the 3 to 6 month recontact (L12), don't promise silence. Never fake familiarity or affection the relationship doesn't have, and use read receipts only where the platform openly shows them to both sides. Test (L63): the voice or video close-off against the same words as text, on replies and bookings per 100 seen-but-silent prospects; never assume it books, since a claimed rate from someone else is not evidence for this business.
- L51 [deciding how much to automate] Automate delivery (templates, recorded videos) and distribution (sending tools) as far as is ethical, but personalisation wins replies, and the smaller the universe of leads, the less to automate: estimate the universe before choosing. Research depth scales with deal size: hours of research per lead suit a small universe of high-value buyers only if lifetime gross profit per client still clears L54's 3:1 after counting the research time for every lead needed to win one client (a low reply rate multiplies it); log minutes per lead (L8) and cap them from that sum. If messages are AI-personalised and tool-sent at volume (L7), a checked lead detail and a daily hand-checked sample carry the personalisation. Spend 10 to 20% of outreach effort testing new tools (for example one day a week on a new research or sending tool), compared with the standard.
- L52 [following up] [Fixed] Contact every lead more than once, in more than one way: for example email first, then a follow-up email, then a call that follows up on the email (L4). Plan for 2 to 3 conversations before a prospect buys. Act like the owner is really trying to reach them. Anyone who asks to stop is never contacted again (see X2, X6). When the list is finished, start again from the top, and recontact non-responders after 3 to 6 months: they may have missed it, been busy, or their situation changed. When pilot places open, recontact earlier non-responders (never anyone who said no or opted out, L12) with an honest admission: there was no proof to show them before, so they had no reason to believe the owner, and the owner will run it free for a few clients in exchange for honest feedback.
- L53 [cold outreach benchmarks] Treat any published outreach benchmark (open, reply, pick-up and acceptance rates) as a placeholder only, and replace it with the owner's own after 100 to 300 contacts per channel; any figure for a new medium (such as letters, L4) is likewise a placeholder. Judge email on replies, not opens: mail privacy features inflate open rates. Later, test personal video or voice memos inside emails (their name, one personal line, the standard message): film in a setting the avatar would recognise, dressed as they would be, with the owner's name, one true line on how they got good at this (F21, O11), a numbered free asset, then "want me to apply this to your business?"; claim it takes less time than they think only once pilots prove it (O15).
- L54 [cold outreach costs and allocation] Nearly all the cost is labour plus software and data: add them up and divide by customers won to get the cost per customer, and aim for at least 3:1 lifetime gross profit to that cost, more where steps need a human (M23). If anyone else works the leads: pay a base rate plus commission per shown appointment, give every rep the same weekly allocation of about 70% of what they could handle, set absolute quotas (leads in, appointments out), and work every lead fully: no lead left behind. Pay commission on cash collected, and celebrate only processed sales. Watch for cherry-picking, especially by outsourced closers. At team stage, rate two alternatives: give the best leads to proven top closers, or specialise setters by lead source with general closers and named accounts for every rep.
- L55 [deciding if cold outreach is working, or scaling it] Benefits to remember: it needs one great message, not a stream of content; competitors can't see it; it's reliable; it doesn't depend on platform rules or on the owner being on camera, which makes the business more sellable; and it's hard to copy. Work backwards from the sales goal: customers needed x contacts per customer = total contacts, divided by working days = daily contacts; allow for efficiency falling as volume rises by planning for up to twice the straight-line figure, and in a small market widen the region sooner (L2). Most people badly underestimate the volume and time it needs. Shadow whoever is best and double their inputs before experimenting: replicate before you iterate, ideally alongside someone who has already done it. Once a month, compare the owner's outreach count with someone ahead in cold outreach: volume habits from other work don't carry over.

### Warm outreach
- L56 [contacting people who already know the owner: replied prospects, pilots, members, opted-in contacts; or the warm list runs low] Warm outreach is the cheapest, easiest source of engaged leads. Ten steps: (1) get the list (every contact on every platform); (2) start on the platform with the most; (3) personalise with a genuine reason to get in touch; (4) reach out to 100 people a day, up to 3 times each, once a day, until they reply (by post, once a week); (5) when they reply, acknowledge what they said by restating it in the owner's own words (echoing their exact word for any feeling, L13), compliment them, ideally on a character trait, and ask a question that steers towards the offer, letting them talk about themselves; (6) after 3 to 4 exchanges, make an offer to test interest, ideally asking "do you know anyone who...?" rather than pitching them; (7) make yes easy; (8) when the list is done, start the next platform; (9) start charging once people refer others; (10) keep the list warm with regular value. When the warm list runs low, build a lukewarm list inside this same channel (L4): each day write one short post on the owner's main platform, then leave about 100 thoughtful, useful comments (never a pitch or link, within each group's or page's rules) on posts where the avatar already gathers, such as groups or communities for their trade; everyone who likes or replies to those comments, or likes or comments on the owner's post, has taken an action and joins the list. Open with one question about what drew them to the post or comment, then follow steps 5 to 7. A private message on a social platform is electronic mail under PECR, so to an individual it carries no marketing until they ask or consent, and to a business contact it follows X2 (identify the owner, offer an opt-out); log each source (L14) and judge this list on replies and calls booked per 100 contacts, against the warm list (L30).
- L57 [re-engaging the owner's own list] The nine-word email: "Are you still looking to [their desire in about four words]?", with no links or images. Treat everyone who replies as an engaged lead and contact them first. Send only to contacts the owner may lawfully email (see X2). After the first target: a short, tactical email every fortnight to the opted-in list only (L36). Format to test there: "I made this for you", what it does, enjoy, and one call to action, in plain text with one link and the opt-out; never for cold email (L10).

### Content
- L58 [making any content, including sales tools and the lead magnet] A content unit hooks, retains and rewards. Hook with the topic (best from the owner's own experience: far past, recent past, present, trending, or manufactured), the headline (use at least two of: recency, relevance, celebrity, proximity, conflict, unusual, ongoing) and the platform's best-performing format. Retain with curiosity: lists, steps and stories, nested; state the number of list items up front. Keep a bank of retention devices and use one wherever attention could drop: show the result first, then each step that got there; name two options the avatar already weighs and reveal the real answer is a third; state a belief the avatar holds, then show why it is wrong; rehook mid-piece with a better, then the best, way; and flag the one condition without which the method fails, then explain it. Each device must be true: the options and belief are ones the avatar really holds, the better way is really better and the condition is real (X9), and devices are judged by where viewers drop off, never assumed to work (L60, L63). Every video carries accurate on-screen captions and must still make sense with the sound off, since much short video is watched muted; check each piece once on mute before posting. Reward by fully delivering what the hook promised: nothing is too long, only too boring. Make every piece for strangers, as if they've never heard of the owner: in every piece say who the owner is and why to listen (one true proof line, F21), title it so a stranger can tell the speaker's role without knowing the name, explain every reference to other pieces, and leave out inside jokes. Say "how I" and "my favourite ways", not "how to" and "the best way": no one can dispute the owner's experience. Mine calls and meetings for stories and keep notes and recordings; capture ideas the moment they come and make more of whatever does well. Documenting is cheaper and more sustainable than manufacturing; never teach what the owner hasn't done: until pilots prove it, document ("this is what I'm doing now") rather than educate. Use the strongest true proof available: clients like the viewer the owner has helped (consented, L32, X13), then the owner doing the thing the viewer wants, then the owner doing work the viewer won't (state the true time, money or attempts spent, so the piece offers a saving of time), then a factual breakdown of a named public figure's published, checkable results, never implying their endorsement (O25, X9). Long content is short units linked together, so start short. The audience decides: if it isn't bringing opt-ins, booked calls and sales from the avatar, the content isn't good enough yet. Wider list titles work when every item is a step of the core offer's method (O19). Humour is optional: entertainment widens reach, education changes behaviour, and humour is the first thing to cut. Viewers of a style or topic mostly want more of the same, so never assume entertainment or off-topic viewers move on to the business's teaching or buy; check it with the L60 per-post numbers before making that kind of piece a habit. Titles and thumbnails say plainly what the piece delivers, so the right viewer knows it is for them; curiosity goes into how, never into what the piece is about (headlines for messages and emails follow L73). Production serves understanding: words on screen, and charts or diagrams that show scale or change over time, stay; effects, changing backgrounds and decoration that help no one learn are cut. Periodically repost or re-record the owner's own best pieces from the past year where the platform's rules allow (some treat re-uploads as duplicate or reused content), since newer followers haven't seen them, and judge each repost like any post. A repost or re-record must earn its place by being better or meaningfully changed (a new hook, edit or angle); never make cosmetic changes (speed, music, caption, watermark) only to slip past a platform's duplicate or originality detection, and never post the same piece many times a day hoping one copy escapes it. A short, recognisable sign-on after the hook, with small variations, is an option to test (L63), never a warm-up before the hook (L73).
- L59 [using content alongside cold outreach] Keep a master list of the best content and tools, labelled by the problem each solves and the benefit it gives, and send the right piece before or after calls to handle common concerns. When sending a case study or testimonial, point to the exact part that answers their concern (a timestamp, or a screenshot with that line highlighted), only from consented, true proof (M45, L33; see X13). People need reminding more than teaching: repeat the message in new formats (written, video, a one-page sheet), new contexts (different client types) and new stories from what the owner actually did that week. Log every question from replies and calls: each is the next piece of content, an FAQ line or a line in the pre-call video. Keep the claims file's blocks (O47) as reusable modules and build every message, page, video segment and letter from them, so the wording stays consistent and true everywhere (D15). After about 100 logged questions, group them and name sessions and playbook chapters from the top 6 to 10, in the avatar's words. After every 10 calls or replies, write one page on the challenges that came up repeatedly (their words), how they reacted to the positioning line and which prospects leaned in, feeding O5, F12 and L3. Once a month, scan sent replies for answers written 3 or more times and turn each into a pre-call video, FAQ, C14 or playbook line checked against O47. Keep a rough grid of avatar type against stage (problem aware, weighing options, choosing), with one asset and one next step per cell.
- L60 [if content becomes a channel after the first target] [stage: S3+] Give, give, give until they ask: keep the give-to-ask ratio well above 3:1, give in public and ask in private. Once paid ads run, count them as asks in the ratio across everything a viewer sees and lean organic content further towards gives; a pointer to something free counts as a give. In short pieces, keep the ask out of the video or in its last seconds, and put it in the description, the first comment and the profile; the profile tells a stranger in one line who the owner is and why to listen (F21, L58), with one link or keyword. Asks offer what more content can't: the personalised version (a review or plan for the viewer's own situation), since more of the same is free everywhere. A comment or message keyword may trigger an automated reply through the platform's own permitted tools, sending only what was asked for, with at most one follow-up; contacts join email or text marketing only with consent (X2, X8). Test ideas in the cheapest format first (short text posts), then promote winners up the formats: a short video read from it, clips, then a long piece built from several winners (L59, L63). Decide on purpose whether the owner is the face: public recognition is hard to undo, and an owner who doesn't want it can keep content brand-led or rely on the Core Four methods that already work (L40). The audience, not the content, is the compounding asset. Asks are integrated (a short plug inside content, after a valuable moment or at the end, added one at a time while checking growth doesn't slow; best for long form) or intermittent (an ask after every 10 give posts; best for short form); point to the lead magnet if unsure, and set an ask cadence alongside a fixed posting cadence. The first post may include an ask; if it gets nothing, give for a while, then ask again. Content is harder to personalise, faces more competition and gets copied, so keep innovating. Batch record, and post manually rather than scheduling. Scale depth then width (master one platform, then add the next), or width then depth if the business is bigger. Start narrow (one small audience and topic) before wider topics; short pieces may cover adjacent topics, long pieces stay on the core problem. Before making a piece, name its job: reach (new strangers, the awareness value posts below) or conversion (opt-ins, calls, sales), and estimate how many people could find the idea relevant at all. To raise a reach piece's ceiling, widen one of three things in the hook: the outcome (a broader stated result instead of a narrow metric, still true and evidenced, O15; see X9), the avatar frame (one level up the label, so the avatar is still inside it, L73), or the words (terms more people already know, L11). Each widening trades conversion rate for audience size, so conversion pieces stay narrow (O3, O12), and a reach piece never widens past the market that holds the avatar (L2); judge both kinds by the avatar results below, not views. Judge content on opt-ins, booked calls and sales from the avatar, never likes, views or followers; use a platform's ranking signals (watch time, sends, likes per reach, or whatever the platform's own published statements name, dated in the claims file, O47; never a creator's ranking or benchmarks), the share of viewers who leave in the first 3 seconds (the hook check, L73) and followers gained only to diagnose why a post didn't reach the avatar; where a platform pays per view, revenue per 1,000 views (on YouTube, RPM) is a fast check on audience quality, also a diagnostic only. Weigh comments by who writes them: praise from outside the avatar doesn't count (F14), so ask avatar members directly whether recent pieces still help them. For each post, track attributable opt-ins and sales per 1,000 views, using a post-specific comment keyword or link so each lead traces to its post (L14); that is the conversion number content is judged on. Hashtags are labels for search, never a reach tactic: use a few that describe the piece exactly, within the platform's current limit, and spend no testing time on them. Once content is a channel: leave the most useful comment on posts the avatar reads, giving value first (F15) and within each platform's self-promotion rules; pair short videos with long captions that carry the teaching, and use short clips to feed long pieces, more often across platforms than within one, checked with the per-post numbers by format before relying on it, since short and long viewers are often different audiences; re-score platforms for the avatar; run content on a fixed weekly slot with a named producer and a checklist, never willpower; before blaming the platform, the niche or the content, count last week's posts on each platform the business uses and those of the leading accounts in its space, since slow growth is most often too few posts; then raise volume on the main platform by one post a day for 30 days, only at a pace that can be kept up for months, with every extra post still meeting the L58 standard, and judge the change on the avatar numbers below, never reach; if good ideas run out before time does, the constraint is ideas, so feed the idea list from the O5 phrase bank, the O10 objection list and the L59 log rather than lowering the bar; the cheapest way to keep that cadence is a default format: one everyday part of the work the avatar wants to see (making, packing, fulfilling, a day in the job), filmed fresh each time to one fixed script structure, opened with a hook type already proven in the niche (borrowed as a structure, never wording, L73), and run as a series; the footage is new every time (never the same clip reposted, see L58), only one element changes between runs (L63), and the series stays only while its avatar numbers hold; any outside writer works only from the claims file (O47, T2); pages target the avatar's own narrow phrases from the O5 phrase bank and the L59 log; earn links and shares honestly (a guest article after months of reading, never implying endorsement, O25; never bought links or keyword stuffing; anything given in return labelled as an ad, see X14); borrow hook structures, never wording, from rivals' outlier posts (at least 4 times their usual views) (W1); keep a mix of four post types (awareness value, conversion value, social proof, connection); film 3 hooks for each video, post all 3 for 24 hours and keep the best; where a platform offers a test feature that shows a piece to non-followers before followers, use it for hooks, new topics and formats (the cheapest test, L63), compare each test with the account's own earlier tests rather than with ordinary posts, judge it on the avatar numbers below, and promote a winner with the platform's own share option rather than uploading a second copy; run a wording audit of the account's own pieces, with a story-audit skill or tool that reads their transcripts and performance data, or by an outsider or a fresh AI chat working from transcripts (the owner is too close to their own words): every working day, check whether at least 3 pieces have settled (14 or more days old) since the last audit, and if so rerun it on one topic and platform; hold out and name every piece under 14 days old; split winners and losers by each piece's result against the account's own average, never raw views, top and bottom third, with at least 3 in each or a plain statement that the sample is too thin; compare the hook, the first 45 words, any escalation beat, where the payoff line sits and the call to action in seven passes (frame verb, such as turning something the viewer has into something better against giving them a new task; the noun the subject is turned into; how early a concrete, nameable object appears; whether the escalation adds a new capability or only a bigger adjective; whether the payoff line comes after the demonstration or before it; word count of the opening sentences; exact figures against vague quantifiers), each stated as hit counts per bucket; look first for controlled pairs (two pieces alike on most passes but far apart in result), and treat near-identical pieces far apart in result as distribution, not wording; try to kill every finding (one outlier carrying a bucket, topic or timing differences between buckets) and report only a pattern holding in at least 80% of both buckets, saying plainly when nothing survives; save every run to the business's findings log beside the claims file (O47): date, pieces, holdouts, the finding with its counts, what showed no signal, confidence, the next test and its status (hypothesis, testing, confirmed or killed); each finding stays a hypothesis until a one-variable test (L63) with readable samples (L43) confirms it on avatar opt-ins, not views, and only confirmed findings change hooks or scripts; end every value post with the lead magnet, which itself ends with the core offer's call to action (L44, L47); and pick formats from a proven bank, skipping any that don't fit the avatar. Posts meant to win new prospects aim for sends (something they'd forward because it surprises them, always true and backed by the claims file, O47, never fear or shock for its own sake, O11); posts for existing followers aim for likes. A movement (an attractive character, a future-based cause, a new opportunity), interviews with partner prospects, and recorded F25 interviews cut into one long and several short pieces are options, judged like any content. The audience needs reminding more than teaching: the owner tires of a message long before the avatar has noticed it, so keep repeating the few core messages that resonated, in new forms, rather than chasing novelty; someone not buying now will think of the business first when they are ready. Choosing and re-ranking platforms: score each platform for the avatar on five things, with the evidence written down: who is there (the share of the avatar, from published survey data dated in the claims file), how many of them, how easily a new account is discovered, depth (time spent per piece), and how easily a viewer can act (messages, stories, links, keyword replies); then add the account's own results per platform: share of site visits or leads against share of followers, time on the site from each platform, and leads and sales per 1,000 followers. Put time where avatar results per hour are highest and re-rank each quarter from the data, cutting platforms that earn least per hour even if they have the most followers. Make content for each platform's own format, since what works on one rarely transfers unchanged. Give in public and sell in private: the public post gives, and the offer happens in messages or on a call. Never buy followers, likes or views, or build an audience outside the avatar to look established: platforms show new posts first to existing followers, so a bought or irrelevant audience suppresses reach to the real avatar (F14). An owned list (email, L44, X2) is where content viewers convert best: move followers onto it and send to it regularly with value. A marketplace where buyers already trust the platform (a book or product listing) can be a discovery channel: a genuinely good product there, with a lawful pointer inside it to the owner's free resource, brings in buyers who would never have found the owner (X14 where commissions apply).

### Paid ads
- L61 [considering or running paid ads] [stage: S3+] Paid ads rent strangers' attention: reach is guaranteed, return isn't, so they're a game of efficiency. They're the fastest way to scale but not the most efficient: they speed the business towards wherever it's heading, including somewhere bad, so add them only once the offer, the message and the opt-in already convert, not before the first target (L4, F3, F32). Four steps: pick a platform with the avatar on it; target it (lookalikes built from the best lists, then filters); make the ad repel everyone else (callout: labels, yes questions, if-then statements, unusual results; contrast, likeness and scene in the visuals; then value using What-Who-When, O5, and a clear call to action); get permission to contact them on a simple landing page that matches the ad. The first 5 seconds and the headline matter most: record about 10 ad bodies and 30 opening lines a week and combine them, and check hooks and awareness level before campaign structure or bidding (L73, L76). Build ads as three interchangeable parts: hooks (the first 5 seconds, each a different angle), bodies (a deeper explanation, a teaching point, a belief-breaking piece in C14's formula, or a demonstration, which must be real, see X9) and calls to action; record each part once and cut every hook with every body and every call to action, so a batch of hooks, bodies and calls to action multiplies into many ads. Make only as many combinations as the testing budget can give readable samples (L43); if it can't, test hooks first on one body and one call to action, then bodies, then calls to action (L63, front to back). Read results by part, not by ad: pool each hook, body and call to action across the ads that used it to find the best few of each. Next round, keep the winning body and call to action, re-record the best hooks in a new wrapper (setting, clothing, background), and write new hooks to beat that control (L63). Study competitors' ads in the platform's ad library and break down about 50 long-running ones; an ad running a month or more is probably profitable. Read the library by hand or through a tool the platform permits, never by automated collection its terms forbid; borrow formats, structures and angles from what rivals run, never their wording, images or footage (W1; see X9). An AI tool may draft ad variants and copy from the claims file (O47) and propose budget moves, but the owner approves every ad before it runs and every budget change against the three phases below and M19; give any tool connected to the ad account the least access it needs (D17). Three phases: track money (set up tracking before spending), lose money (testing: allow each new ad up to 2x the cash a customer pays in the first 30 days, and kill it at 1x with no leads), print money (when returns beat cost, spend as much as capacity allows). Set the budget backwards from customers wanted x cost per customer, plus 20%. Learn with a small spend on one ad, from a monthly learning budget the owner can afford to lose. Choose a platform the owner uses, can target on, knows the formats of and can afford the minimum spend for, starting with the placements competitors use. Build lookalikes from clients first, then the warm list, then cold leads, keeping all lists in one place (including ad leads who didn't buy); if lookalikes aren't possible, target by interests. Start in a puddle and grow to a pond, lake and ocean: the ratio falls but total profit rises; with local targeting add few filters, because narrow audiences burn out faster. The person shown should look like the avatar. Run the best organic pieces and testimonials as ads with a simple banner call to action rather than re-recording them, chosen by saves and follows, not views: the flow is organic to paid. Native-looking is fine, disguised is not; never target sensitive categories, and where group targeting isn't allowed, let setting, props and dress attract the matching audience; run a client's or partner's post as an ad only with written consent, labelled, with any payment or gift disclosed (L37; see X14, X8, X13). For colder audiences, test a short article-style bridge page between ad and offer page. Ad platforms are auctions, so whoever converts best can outbid the rest. Search ads are harder to crack but steadier once working, and a good-enough agency can run them under T2; social ads need fresh creative about every 2 to 3 weeks for a small audience, so plan that creative load before starting. Later test: ads aimed at the followers of a Dream 100 name who won't promote the owner, never using sensitive targeting. Paid clipping (third parties cut the owner's long pieces into clips and post them on their own accounts, paid per 1,000 approved views) is paid reach and follows this rule: only once there are long pieces with proven clips (L60) and a funnel that converts; set a total budget, a rate and a per-clip maximum, so spend is capped whatever the views; write a brief from the claims file (O47) and approve only clips that follow it, carry the ad label (X14) and make no claim beyond it; trace each clip's opt-ins with its own link or keyword (L14), and judge it like any ad on avatar opt-ins and sales per pound spent, never views, since clippers' audiences may be far from the avatar (F14).
