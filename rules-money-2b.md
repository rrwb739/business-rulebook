## Money models (M), continued

- M37 [a buyer says it costs too much] Payment plan downsell: "too much" often means too much today. Present options from most cash upfront to least and stop as soon as they buy: (1) reward paying in full: state the instalment total as the price and the upfront figure as the saving, with at least 10% off for paying upfront; (2) "Would you rather I set the payment terms or you set them?", then suggest their business credit card, so they set their own terms and the business is paid today; (2b) layaway: they pay in instalments, split as they like, before delivery starts, and delivery begins once it's paid in full (no credit risk, and it fits pre-selling, M14); (3) half now, half when their cash lands ("When does your income come in?"; "What's the most you can put down today?"); (4) a 1 to 10 check: 8 or more, "we'll find a way" and keep going; 7 or less, ask what would make it a 10 and move to a feature downsell; (5) three payments; (6) payments spread evenly over delivery. Seesaw version for short calls: "Would you rather have big regular payments or small ones? Pay upfront and there are none; the more you put down now, the smaller they get." During any plan, offer the upfront saving again. People pay slower if allowed and faster if rewarded. Schedule collections for just after their income lands, use automatic payment collection, and let the provider retry failures within card and scheme limits; never retry a hard decline. Check plans add buyers: overall close rate should rise while the share paying upfront stays the same. [Fixed] Instalments, credit and third-party finance only within X3. Never call it "paid in full": it's the upfront saving. Alternative to test: a smaller saving for half upfront, so each step down costs a little more. Time any instalments to delivery milestones (O42 delayed second payment). Instalment plans (not M30's pay-later offer) never run past the end of delivery (layaway before, or payments within it): payments after the value is delivered feel like paying for nothing. Upfront payers can get first pick of places and session slots, if that priority is real and limited (O36). A partial version: delivery starts once a set share is paid (for example the first quarter), with monthly payments starting immediately after; offer full layaway as the downsell for those who can't pay that share now. Option to test for recurring offers: a larger upfront payment with a lower recurring fee after it tends to keep clients longer than the same value spread evenly, and the upfront part can cover acquisition cost, commission and onboarding; state the total and the recurring terms plainly before signing (X3).
- M38 [a buyer declines continuity, or for any recurring offer that needs their work] Trial with penalty, only as a downsell after a no: they start without paying if they do the success actions, taken from the M30 (b) criteria (attend the check-ins, do the tasks, log results, and one that advertises the business, such as a disclosed case study, X3), and pay a stated fee for each missed action (per action is fairer than one lump fee, unless a single miss wrecks their result). What they get is the stripped-down version, or more if affordable. Steps: (1) offer it last; (2) take a payment mandate, because people who won't commit and do the work complain more and convert less; (3) sell staying: "If this gets you the result, will you stay?"; if no, don't offer the trial; if they want more explanation, say they won't hit the long-term goal during the trial but will build the habits, and there is no honest quick fix; (4) explain every criterion, its cost if missed and its benefit if met, and the check-ins, which are offer points: "we charge if you miss them because it's the only way we get you results"; (5) they initial the fee terms. [Fixed] All terms are stated before they give payment details (see X3). Never say "it's how we've always done it" while it isn't true. Afterwards: liked it, offer longer or higher; hated it, ask what should have been different, take the blame, ask to make it up, offer the better-fitting tier; didn't use it, contact them several times before the end and waive the fee if they meet the owner. Let people make up a slip before billing. Troubleshooting: nobody takes it, lower the requirements or fees; nobody follows through, explain the fees better and make meetings mandatory; nobody stays, sell staying, deliver better and fit the back end to the front end; it's printing money, change nothing. For one-off services, use pay less now or pay more later (M30 (a)) as this downsell instead. Before any reward or fee tied to a count (here, M30 (b), and later T1 and L69 pay plans), write down how it could be met without the real result and measure the outcome as well as the count, then check the drop-off in the month after any clawback, payout or reward window ends, stretching tiers past the known churn months (M9) rather than one cliff.
- M39 [price objection after payment-plan options] Feature downsell: lower the price by changing what they get. First remove something valuable with a small price drop, which makes them reconsider the original; then keep removing features, highest value first, until they buy: something beats nothing. Remove what they dislike for a big drop; remove what they love for a small one. Levers: quantity (fewer sessions or deliverables), quality (response time, call length, one-to-one or group, live or recorded, done-for-you, done-with-you or DIY, personalisation, guarantee length, conditional or unconditional terms, priority or waiting, fixed or flexible slots, who delivers, how long access lasts, rescheduling terms), dropping a whole feature ("keep chat and email, drop the calls"), removing the guarantee ("pay less without the results guarantee, or keep it: which would you prefer?"), and finally the DIY route: "Even though we won't work together on this, I still want to help: come to a free walkthrough", then give them the free DIY tier at the end. Name the top package aspirationally and the bottom "the Minimum package" ("so nothing more than the minimum?"). After two refused changes, ask the 1 to 10 question: 8 or more, move to payment plans; 7 or less, "What would a 10 look like?" and recombine. Standardise the combinations once the owner knows what buyers value. Continuity declined: offer a minimum continuity tier (core materials plus email support at a lower rate) alongside the trial with penalty (M38). Downsell current members before they cancel: a lower price for only what they use. Barter for advertising on a price objection: a stated amount off for an honest review, a case study, progress posts and two introductions, disclosed and never conditional on being positive [Fixed] (see X12, X3).Match the delivery model to what the buyer's time is worth: learning to do it themselves suits buyers who earn less than the skill would save them; buyers who already earn well pay for it done for them. A separate low-touch or do-it-yourself product is offered on a call only to people who don't meet the core offer's written qualifying criteria (so the marketing spent on them is partly recovered and they can ascend later); a buyer who qualifies is never offered it, only the payment-plan and feature options above, so the team has a black-and-white rule and the core promise isn't watered down. Before adding any downsell to the sales process, record the core offer's close rate, and check after a few weeks that it hasn't fallen; if it has, the team is downselling buyers who would have bought the core.
- M40 [designing or pricing continuity] Continuity gives ongoing value for ongoing payments until they cancel: sell once, get paid repeatedly, and end with more clients to upsell. Use it only for ongoing value. It brings less cash today, so it goes last in the sequence, after attraction, upsell and downsell cash; continuity on its own is hard to advertise, so lead with the valuable thing they get. Its tools are bonuses (M41), discounts (M42) and waived fees (M43); prefer rewards to punishment. Bill less often: default to the longest billing period that fits the client's natural cycle (quarterly or annual over monthly), and offer annual prepay; where billing is by weeks, every 4 weeks gives 13 payments a year. Present it as a plan (support through each cycle), not a subscription; continuation terms are still agreed upfront (X3). Price continuity below the core offer per period, because value per period falls once the pain is solved; the core offer, sold at peak pain, carries the high price and is collected upfront where possible (M37). The reason they buy isn't the reason they stay: at each renewal and exit interview ask why they stay, and sell and price continuity on that reason. Ask for one or two reasons ("what's the main thing that's changed?"), never "list all the reasons", here and in D12, M45 and C5. Alternative to test (after the first target): a lower base plus a fee per unit used, so smaller clients don't cancel in a quiet period; check how buyers react to variable bills first. Continuity must deliver something used up each period (a fresh pack, updated templates, a short "what changed" brief); a period with nothing new invites churn. If the core offer makes the client independent, design continuity as the next level, judged on ascension (M31), not more of the same teaching, and don't make peer community its core value. Later only, after the first target: a private members' space can be tested as a support add-on, kept in a system the business owns (L36), with the same limits as M12; judge any platform on own data. Frame it as keeping the core investment working only where that's true, never leaning on sunk cost (M27). After the first target (M18), negotiate member-only partner discounts worth at least the periodic fee and show the saving at each renewal, disclosing any commission (O41, X3). After 12 months of members, test a higher price or a higher-touch tier. Features buyers tend to pay most for: exclusive access to the owner, exclusive content, exclusive events and a regular briefing. Frequency of access sells; a fixed question format lets one call answer many. Busy buyers may value quick written access over calls. Price recurring support so a client can afford it in its worst month, not just its average one (test after early clients). List every deliverable as one-time or ongoing: one-time items justify the core price, only ongoing items justify the continuity fee, and a thin ongoing column gets fuller before any price rise. Launch with the minimum and build more only from what members ask for in the first two periods (L59 question log); beyond the core pack, send material only when there's a real problem to fix. Price changes, stated in the terms before they sign (X3): on a rise, existing members keep their rate for a stated period with written notice; on a cut, they move to the lower rate from their next period, so nobody has to cancel and rejoin. Before removing or replacing anything members rely on, tell them first and keep the old version for a stated period (M22). No lifetime deals: every paid service has a stated term (materials the client keeps are fine, M9); if the business stops serving a segment, finish fixed-term commitments as sold and refund the unused part of anything open-ended. Hold member Q&A when clients are free, never in their busiest weeks. Recurring revenue lifts the business's value (F30 step 9). In a launch, present only the annual plan, with a premium the buyer needs anyway (a physical starter kit) included only with it, and open the monthly plan after the cart closes; a paid trial of the membership as the launch's own entry offer is a bigger test to run as a split with everything else the same. [shape: info] In teaching and coaching, the skill is a one-time value worth far more than what keeps coming each month, and once a client has the skill they stop paying for it monthly: so charge for the skill up front and keep continuity to what is still used each month after the skill is learned (community, fresh leads, updates, reviews), priced low enough to be an easy yes (big head, long tail). To set this up, list every deliverable in two columns: one-time value (used once, after which it is worth nothing more to that client, such as a taught skill) and consumables (used again every month, such as fresh assets the business tests once and supplies to many clients, a curated list of new opportunities refreshed each month, a community, or an ongoing service). Price the one-time column as high as buyers will pay, and price the consumables at what a client would pay if they were the only thing sold, which is usually far below a spread-out share of the upfront price. An upfront price split into monthly instalments is a payment plan, not continuity: clients who stop after the term have graduated, not churned, so never judge or price continuity from instalment buyers. The one kind of education that keeps clients paying is continued education: recertification or updates a profession must keep up with throughout a career. Beyond that, sell higher levels of the same path to the share who want them, expecting fewer buyers at each level (M31). Where the main offer has a large set-up fee, offer two real options: the set-up fee waived in return for a year's commitment (with the fee payable if they leave early, stated plainly before signing, X3), or the set-up fee paid and continuity month to month or quarter to quarter. For products bought occasionally, a credit-based membership can create recurring revenue: each paid month adds a credit to spend on anything, members can skip a month without paying within a stated window, selections are personalised from past purchases, and members get a standing member price; it only works within consumer law: the recurring charge, the skip deadline, what happens to unused credit and how to cancel must be stated clearly before the first payment, cancelling must be as easy as joining, joining can never be the pre-selected default at checkout, and subscription rules in the project's country apply (in the UK the DMCC Act's subscription contract regime, X3; in the US negative-option rules, check with an adviser). Buyers judge a recurring fee each billing period on what they got since the last payment: an earlier big win does not prepay later months, so plan a visible, logged result or useful contact in every billing period. Small and beginner buyers leave for reasons outside the business's control (they stop, close or run out of money); where the avatar churns that way, either price to its worst month with a near-zero cost to serve and a very large value gap, or sell to the steadier business that serves those buyers and stays when its own clients come and go (M9 worst-month pricing, L2). For very small buyers with structural churn, consider a low entry fee plus a lawful usage or transaction-based charge, so most income comes from the clients who grow. [shape: service] Recurring services to very small businesses inherit their volatility: a mid-priced monthly service with real labour tends to stall as acquisition cost rises and clients churn within months. Pick one end: a low monthly price for something the client can see working with almost no delivery cost (local map ranking, reviews, a hosted site), which stays for years, or a high price for clients who already know their numbers and have a working sales process (M2, F12).An annual fee on top of a monthly price adds revenue from clients who stay, but it is never hidden: for business clients state it in the proposal and agreement next to the monthly price; for consumers in the UK, mandatory fees belong in the headline price and a subscription must show its total price, so it cannot be used to make the monthly price look lower (X3).A one-off product can still earn repeat revenue without a subscription: offer past buyers a yearly check, a seasonal add-on or an upgrade when the next model arrives, and track the share of past buyers who buy something again within 12 months; a steady repeat share makes the business more valuable even with no membership.In every client report, show the running total of value delivered since the start (results, hours saved, money found) next to the total paid, as well as this period's figure, so one weak month is judged against the whole.For a paid community or membership, ask members to rate each feature on how much they care about it and whether they would pay more for it: build the offer around what scores high on both (usually direct access to the expert, exclusive content beyond what is public, and meeting in person), keep what they care about but won't pay for (a regular newsletter) as the expected basics, and drop what scores low on both. Build it around the identity the product moves people towards (the "I am a..." statement), so members join others who see themselves the same way. [shape: info] Education or coaching that teaches people to earn: admit only buyers likely to succeed (C2), and say who is turned away; publish outcome data instead of income testimonials (the share of buyers reaching set milestones at about 30, 90 and 365 days, and the median against the top fifth); give the know-how away and sell the implementation (O19); any certificate is earned by meeting a stated standard, never by paying; and every earnings claim is substantiated and shown with typical results (X9; in the US deceptive earnings claims are already unlawful under the FTC Act, and in January 2025 the FTC proposed extending its Business Opportunity Rule to business coaching, not final at the last check; confirm with a qualified adviser).
- M41 [selling continuity] Continuity bonus: give a valuable bonus for joining today, worth more than the first payment, related to the core offer and built from what the business already has. Sell the bonus and its genuine value first, ask "Do you want to know how to get this free?", then "Join today and it's a free gift, or buy it on its own for its stated price: which would you prefer?", then stack the other member bonuses with their values. A bigger gap between the bonus's one-off price and the first payment pushes more buyers into continuity. Bonus types: more of the same, something complementary, an upgrade, limited availability. Making bonuses members-only pushes everyone into continuity. Bonuses only for longer commitments trade fewer sign-ups for more committed members. Present the prepaid block first, sold sincerely as a real option, then the regular plan as the step down (alternative to test: join first, then "Want to save even more?"). Work out the real lift in 30-day profit from the billing period, not from monthly examples. A prepaid block rolls automatically into regular continuity when it ends, with a renewal reminder beforehand (X3); line the last core-offer instalment up with the first continuity payment so payments never stop. Give titles by tenure. Test a physical bonus on a digital offer. Use realistic values only: anchors from prices actually charged. Alternative to test: continuity starts by default when the core offer ends, with every term stated and initialled before signing and an easy way to opt out (X3), against offering it at the end. Set take-up expectations from own data. A bonus given for as long as the client stays (a free item in every order or every period) lowers acquisition cost and raises retention, since leaving loses it; it can be sold later to existing clients as a paid add-on, stacked, and limited to a period such as a year once it is common; cost every such bonus into the gross profit per order (M1). Time a loyalty reward to land just after the point where most members cancel (for example a free item after the fifth payment), so the reward gives a reason to stay past it.
- M42 [offering free time or a discount for commitment] Continuity discount: before offering free time, decide how the discount applies and the cancellation policy. Methods: upfront (delays cash; only where contracts are reliably honoured and churn is low), at the end (earned if every payment is on time), spread evenly (the default: it keeps cash flowing; option to keep the lower rate for life after the term), or after the first one or two payments (take the first and last period upfront or a small activation fee, so cash covers costs and the payment method is proven; a nominal first payment sits better than "free" when asking for payment details). Extend the term rather than eating into it, and use the shorter version as a feature downsell. Front-loaded discounts convert more and churn more; back-loaded, the reverse. Soft cancellation terms bring more sign-ups and more leavers; strict terms, fewer of both. Option to test: give the discounted time as a voucher, usable after the first period or passed to another buyer with an introduction. Start a lifetime discount just past the month most members leave, and remind them as it approaches. Cancellation fee: the discount they received, so leaving early returns them to the normal rate. Make cancelling easy and visible, and complete without any call; afterwards, invite everyone to an exit interview, waiving the cancellation fee if they come: open with the goal they set at kickoff (D13), let them vent, be angrier about the problem than they are, fix what's fixable, and if they wanted more, roll them into a higher tier (M35). Ask for a backup payment method, with no fee for it [Fixed] (see X3). End every cancellation, and every early client that doesn't continue, with one specific thing they did well, a warm line and an open door, and treat leavers as a "not now" under L12 unless they asked not to be contacted (X2).
- M43 [long-term continuity terms] Waived fee: rolling terms with a set-up fee of 3 to 5x the monthly rate, or the fee waived for a 12-month commitment, payable only if they leave early. Justify it by real onboarding cost: "If you only want to test us, you cover the set-up; commit for a year and we cover it." They initial the early-exit line. The fee ends when the commitment is met. A bigger fee pushes commitment; a smaller one (1.5 to 3x) brings more rolling cash. If more than 5% want to leave early, investigate the product: nudge, don't handcuff. Fees follow X3.
- M45 [asking for a review or testimonial] Specific testimonials with a before, an after and a number build the most credibility. Many genuine reviews beat a perfect average. Invite every client to review, not only the happy ones, and never publish only the good ones; label any chosen quotes as testimonials (see X12). Make the ask small and urgent, with exact steps ("it takes less than 60 seconds: click here, pick 1 to 5 stars, write one line"). Follow L37 and X3. Success interviews (asked as the favour they are, any perk disclosed, never tied to a positive answer): ask about life before (internal feelings and external numbers), what made them act, their biggest concern before buying and why they went ahead anyway, then life after, inside and out. The concern-and-decision part speaks straight to prospects. One interview becomes a testimonial, content and later an ad, with written consent and none of their customers' data (L33). [Fixed] A clip cut from a testimonial must keep the speaker's meaning; any claim in it needs documentary evidence; consent must cover use in ads; personal details beyond the business result stay out (see X13, X8). Never draft testimonial wording for a client to sign: quote their own words from the success interview, edited only for length with their approval. Ask for the testimonial and success interview (and repeat the L66 referral ask) within a few days of a clear, measured result; after a narrow one, wait for the next. Link the ask to what they and their team have already done, true only, never a guilt line (L66). [Fixed] No actors, composites, AI-generated faces or voices, or stock photos presented as clients; any illustrative person is labelled as such (see X13). Run the review ask as a standing system, never a campaign: each early review moves buyers more than a later one, so start it from the first client and keep it running. Ask at the handover or payment moment, with one link or code placed where they already are (beside the bill, in the box, in the delivery message), pointing to the one platform buyers search most now; move the link to the next platform once that one has a healthy count. Track the share of each team member's clients who leave a review as an internal satisfaction measure, ask the best what they do differently and teach it, but set no review quotas or review bonuses and never ask customers to name a staff member in a review (platform rules, X12). Any surprise gift after delivery (L46) stays unconnected to the review ask.When collecting a testimonial for an ad, ask for their worst moment before they started, with the specific detail, not "what was life like before?"; a concrete moment makes the strongest opening line.
- M47 [any client, or related group of clients, near or above 20% of revenue; or a large client is on offer] Key-client risk: if any one client or connected group left tomorrow and revenue fell by 20% or more, the business has key-client risk, because at normal margins that loss takes a large share of profit and cuts what the business is worth. Four fixes, pick the one most likely to work: (1) add enough smaller clients that the big one falls under 20%; (2) lock the big client in with a longer written term (with a real benefit for committing, such as a named contact or a price for the term) plus a notice period and a proportionate exit charge that only recovers real loss or the discount given back (a charge out of proportion can be struck out as a penalty, X3); (3) win several more clients of the same size, if they come through a repeatable channel, since the business may really be in the large-client business; (4) turn down or ring-fence a large client that arrived by chance, with no repeatable way to win more like it, when serving it would mean custom pricing, delivery or terms that don't serve the other clients. Check the share of revenue from the top client and top 3 clients at each monthly review.
- M48 [preparing to sell all or part of the business] A sale takes about a year from finding an adviser to cash received and takes much of the owner's attention, so a management team must run and grow the business meanwhile (F28, T1). Before starting, fix the deal killers: too small for the buyers wanted, client concentration (M47), no repeatable way of winning clients, the owner as key person, one channel, flat or falling numbers, and no believable growth story; buyers pay for future sales discounted by how likely they are (M19), and a small decline is read as a trend, so sell while the numbers are rising. Before any adviser or buyer conversation, write down the price and terms the owner would accept and why, and what matters most (cash at close, staying or leaving, the scope of any non-compete), and give that list to whoever represents the owner. For larger deals use an investment bank, for smaller ones a business broker; find them through introductions, send a one-page teaser, meet several, and choose one that has recently sold businesses of the same type and size. Negotiate its fee on cash actually received rather than headline value, with a minimum price below which no success fee is paid, and push back on upfront and break-up fees; the adviser is paid to close a deal, not the best one, so insist on a wide buyer list, repeated follow-ups and a fight for every point the owner named. The usual steps: an anonymous teaser, confidentiality agreements, an information memorandum, a data room, indications of interest, then competing letters of intent. Judge offers on the cash the owner keeps after earn-outs, seller finance, rolled equity, escrow, fees, team payouts and tax, never the headline number; strategic buyers usually pay more than financial ones; and weigh whether the owner and team want to work with the buyer, since a slightly lower offer from people they trust is often the better deal. Exclusivity usually starts at the signed letter of intent and the buyer then controls the pace: never accept less for a promised fast close, and keep growing, since growth during diligence is the main leverage left. Prepare for quality-of-earnings and legal diligence (compliance gaps can end a deal, so fix them first, X rules), interviews with clients and staff, and background checks on management. In the purchase agreement, negotiate indemnities beyond the fundamental ones, the non-compete's scope and its definitions (know the next venture and say so plainly, so the wording leaves it open), schedules that record every claim the deal relied on, the working capital left in the business, and the escrow amount and release dates; be ready to walk away on the points that matter, since only a credible walk-away moves the other side. Use a specialist lawyer and tax adviser; confirm with a qualified adviser. Before preparing a sale, decide whether to sell at all: write the reasons and strike out any that are about status or proving something; compare the after-tax cash plus what the owner can realistically earn with it against what the stake kept would be worth in five years; and name what the sale is meant to give (attention, time, a new focus), then check whether changing the owner's role in the business would give the same without selling. Once a sale is on the table, keep making decisions for the clients' long-term good, not for how a buyer would see them: cutting prices to show lower churn or hiring to look bigger shrinks the profit a buyer pays for. Possible buyers include the business's own staff or clients, often funded by a bank loan (with a government-backed scheme where one exists) plus part seller finance, which keeps the price workable without draining the business's cash; take legal and tax advice on the structure (X20).
