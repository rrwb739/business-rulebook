## Writing (W)

- W1 [drafting any message, email, post or copy] Write in the owner's own voice, following the language and style set in the instructions box; plain and concise. A plain, honest, human voice stands out: drafts may be AI-assisted (L7), but every message must read as the owner, with a real point of view, and never sound synthetic. Before sending any message with tone risk (a no, a correction, humour, a reply to a complaint), reread it once as the recipient and cut sarcasm and in-jokes.Edit anything that matters (copy, a page, a deck, a script) in separate passes, each looking for one kind of problem (structure, facts and figures, spelling, tone), ideally at different times of day; the value is made in the edit, judged by how much the reader gets per second, not by length. In stories and copy, show rather than tell (the detail that implies the fact), include only details that would change the reader's picture, and raise the stakes by saying what was at risk. In long pieces (a guide, a book, a long page), put a short true story where the teaching starts to feel heavy, and test each framework against new situations until no case breaks it, rewriting from the start when one does. Before publishing anything long, send it to four to eight honest readers from the avatar; change what several of them flag, and treat a point only one raises as optional. Sell the outcome, never the tool: leave "AI" and other method words out of headlines and offers unless the buyer asks, since naming the tool adds nothing for the buyer and anchors the price lower.
- W2 [presenting or explaining an idea: a talk, a meeting update, a slide deck, a pitch or a video script] Give each piece at most three main ideas, since listeners hold about three; put any extra point inside one of them or cut it. Order it why, what, how, now: why it matters to this audience now and why the speaker can speak on it, then the overview (the map of the main ideas), then the steps, then the first action and what happens next. Most people jump straight to the steps; give the why and the map first, so listeners know why to listen and where it is going. Where it helps, show the idea as a simple shape (a triangle with three labelled sides, a circle in four parts, a two-by-two grid), so listeners can picture it and pass it on. To prepare, speak the whole idea out loud or into an AI assistant, then have it rearranged into why, what, how, now before writing slides or a script. For a point listeners should remember and repeat, use insight, story, lesson: state the insight, tell one true story with concrete detail (what happened, then what changed), and close on the lesson in one line; a list of events with no change in it is not a story. To explain a result or a complex recommendation, use situation, complication, resolution (from Barbara Minto's pyramid principle): the situation everyone agrees on, the problem or tension in it, then what was done or is proposed. When advising someone, give the way of thinking with the answer (for example "a healthy business needs traffic, a way to convert it, and the skill to deliver; yours is strong on the first and last, so fix conversion"), so they can reuse the judgement without the adviser. Keep a phrase notebook: when someone explains something clearly, write it down, rephrase it in the speaker's own words, and file it by topic for reuse.
