## Offers (O)

### Building the offer

- O1 [designing or reviewing the offer] Make an offer so good the avatar would feel stupid saying no. It has five parts: an attractive promotion, unmatched value, a premium price, a strong guarantee, and a money model that pays for acquisition. Audit all five whenever the offer changes, list any weak part and propose a fix. Bonuses for becoming a client (playbooks, templates, scripts) count as value. Without such an offer only exceptional persuasion wins, which is the hardest path. A better offer lifts response rate, close rate and price together, and the effects multiply, so track all three for every version of the offer.
- O2 [positioning, branding, choosing partners, endorsers or public appearances, or comparing with competitors] Sell in a category of one, so the choice is the offer or nothing, never the offer or something cheaper. Sell the result and the process, never position against the tools the client already uses, and work with whatever they have. Show how the offer fits the guidance and tools the client already trusts, never as a rival view: when two authorities seem to disagree, buyers freeze. The offer should explain itself, so the owner never has to argue why it is different. To find what is unique, get narrower (avatar, place, problem) until something true is unique, and lead with it; second best is a true step rivals also do but never mention, said plainly. Being the best in a small pond beats being average in a big one. The lasting moat is a name the avatar trusts (reputation, proof, referrals): anyone can copy the method, nobody can copy that, so protect it in every choice (L75). Name the three things the avatar should link with the owner's name, check every partner, testimonial, public post and side venture under that name against them, and keep other ventures off the offer's domain, signature and pages. If a rival copies the wording or method, don't respond in public (F27): treat it as evidence the method works and put the effort into client results and proof (L32). Keep a facts-only list of how the offer differs from the leading alternatives (M4), each point checkable, weave those facts into copy and calls, and show them in the offer doc as a short, dated, like-for-like table of what the buyer could buy instead, with real prices and the cheapest included. Compete with the thing the buyer would actually use instead (a free webinar, a short course, a junior staff member), not the brand behind it. Keep rivals unnamed in public copy and every comparison true (see X9). Pick 2 or 3 true associations for the owner's name and repeat them everywhere, never implying official or professional-body endorsement (see X9). A brand is a promise kept over and over: keep the same core message for years and vary only the wrapper (O13); repositioning every few months makes buyers forget and distrust the owner. The name, the look of every asset and the price carry the same personality and stay consistent year to year. In one-to-many assets, put the owner's name and the offer's name right next to the most memorable line or image (never in the headline, L73), and ask the O3 reviewers who it was from. The look and production of public content is a positioning choice too: raw, lo-fi content gains reach and relatability but can cut against a clearly premium price (M2), while polished content can read as distant to a mass avatar; decide on purpose which the price position needs, judge it by the avatar results in L60, never by views, and test it (L63) rather than assume it. A collaboration with another brand is checked against the three associations above and disclosed where X14 applies, never used just to borrow relevance from an audience outside the avatar (F14). [stage: after the first target] If the business needs both a premium position and lo-fi reach, run the lo-fi side under a separate channel or brand rather than diluting the main one; before the first target, keep one channel (F3). A brand is the set of associations buyers hold about the name, built by pairing it again and again with things the avatar already knows and likes (a solved problem, a result, a respected person, place or idea) until the liking transfers; unplanned pairings happen every day, so choose them on purpose and prune the rest, since a name linked with many unrelated things means nothing to anyone. Advertising is making the name known; branding is what is made known while doing it, so a piece can be good advertising and bad branding: judge every pairing by what the avatar likes, never by reach alone. Why it pays: strong associations with the avatar lower the cost to win a client, raise conversion and the price buyers accept, and make them buy again and only from the owner, so judge brand work on the M23 inputs over time, never on likes. Every new association wins some people and loses others: before adding one, name who it gains and who it loses, and go ahead only if the gains are more of the avatar and the losses are people outside it; turn down appearances and pairings with large reach whose associations a big part of the avatar dislikes. Keep one central pillar, the topic every member of the avatar wants: the owner's other interests and personality appear only around it, never in its place, and a run of wider topics that drifts off the pillar is cut as soon as the avatar numbers fall (L60). Choosing content that appeals to one side of a political or social question is a positioning bet with the same gain-and-loss test; refusing or treating any buyer worse because of who they are is a legal question, see X23. For a personal brand, attention comes from both the message and the messenger: a messenger stands out by combining several true, uncommon traits or skills that rarely go together, especially ones that seem to contradict, so list the owner's real ones (skills, background, interests, habits) and show them around the central pillar rather than hiding them to fit in; it only works on top of being genuinely excellent at the core, and every trait shown must be real (X9). Every position has an advantage: when buyers compare a small seller with the market leader, the true comparison is often the owner's own attention against the staff member the leader would assign, so a small business may say so where it is true and checkable (direct contact, who does the work), never by running the leader down (F27, X9). A differentiator only counts if the buyer can see it; one only the owner can detect (better ingredients, better workouts) doesn't set the offer apart, so put the effort where buyers judge. A personal brand needs something worth hearing first: real results, or a documented volume of work (outreach attempts, jobs, tests) whose lessons save the audience time. Associations work only through what the avatar likes (its topics, free and paid products, and the people it admires), so an association that delights one audience can cost a business with another; check every pairing against the avatar, not the team's taste. The brand becomes what buyers say after using the product, so the product must keep the promise. [stage: after the first target] Stay relevant with regular brand moments: a public goal with a date and real stakes that the avatar finds aspirational, which also aligns the team; a brand that stops having them gets nibbled away by newer rivals. [shape: e-commerce] Selling several unbranded products on paid media alone hits a ceiling: acquisition cost rises, margins shrink and copies undercut every winner. Keep the product that carries the rest, cut the others, and build one brand around a product the owner believes in, promoted by creators who genuinely use it; lead with brand before legal protection, since patents protect only those with the size and budget to enforce them.Sell from the position the business actually holds: a new or small business sells full attention, flexibility, direct access to the owner and being one of very few clients; an established one sells track record, specialists and proven systems that won't break under demand. Claim only the advantages of the true position (X9).Judge a brand on three measures: reach (how many of the avatar recognise it), influence (the share whose behaviour changes when they see it) and direction (towards the business or away); a strong brand need not be divisive, and seeking controversy buys recognition at the cost of direction. Random, unrelated pairings weaken it; after a bad pairing, don't try to erase it: outweigh it with many more pairings the avatar likes, and remove the cause if it is still there.
- O3 [writing the offer, bio, pitch, posts or messages] Positioning line: "I help [exact person] solve [specific problem] by [unusual method] without [deepest fear]." The current version is in the instructions box; use it everywhere. Short script form for messages: "I help [avatar] get [dream outcome] by [deadline] without [effort], [plus proof or guarantee]". The more specific the avatar, the higher the price the same product supports. Where results support it, also frame the value as a trade: the small thing the buyer gives (minutes, one step) for one specific gain, both stated as numbers in the buyer's own unit, then scaled to a year with "what would you do with...?" (O4, X9). Before changing the line because it feels confusing, ask 3 people outside the business (ideally one from the avatar and one extreme outsider with no knowledge of the field) to say back what the business does in one sentence, and rewrite only if they can't. Keep the chain simple: where the pain comes from, what the client's existing tools already handle, what the offer fixes. One big idea per piece of copy: one benefit, and every other line a reason for it. The whole offer should fit in one short text; test that on outsiders, not on prospects. Before a new email or page headline goes out at volume, write 20 or more one-line versions and show the best few to people before picking. Before any one-to-many asset, write one line on the single avatar member it is for and write to that person. One main idea per piece, researched, kept simple, with a true story where one fits. Each one-to-many asset carries three true lines: who the owner is and why (F21), the result the buyer wants (O4), and why the offer is a new route rather than more of the same (O25); the buyer is the one who acts, the owner is the guide and the offer is the route, never the star. Write 20 short future lines of 3 to 6 words, test them as above with no figures until proven (O15), and use the winner as the sign-off line of follow-ups, the pre-call video and recaps, never the first cold email (L10), checked at the D15 review. Before launch, test the explanation of an offer, page or programme on the least informed person who could still buy (someone who has never heard of it), asking them to say what it is; when they can't, rewrite and test again, often over many versions in a week, until a stranger gets it at once.
- O4 [writing copy, pitches or scripts] Sell the destination, not the journey: lead with their pain now, then life after. Mention the process only as a short named mechanism that explains why it works, because a new mechanism raises likelihood (O25); never a list of steps or tools, and mention technology only where it explains how. For each feature climb the ladder: feature, then benefit, then the benefit of that benefit. Then ask "so what?" aloud on each feature, with a partner if possible, until the answer is something the buyer already wants (an O24 desire in their words), however many steps it takes; for technical features ask "what's so great about that?" instead. Show, don't tell: describe the moment they realise it has worked and the better problems they'll have afterwards, framed as an illustration, not a promise, until results prove it (O15). Naming problems they haven't hit yet makes them believe the owner has been there. Each piece pairs one modest, evidenced claim with one surprising but true mechanism; say "easily" only once results show it (O15; see X9). Give each piece one high-contrast picture: a specific before-and-after moment in their own numbers, or one true number made visible, built from the claims file (O47), never staged and never melodrama (L50). Frame the offer as a new way of doing things, not an upgrade to what they have, while saying plainly what they keep (O2, O28): a new opportunity sells where an improvement makes them admit past choices failed. Credit hard facts to their source and put the fix in the same breath, so the owner's name sits next to the relief, not the problem.
- O5 [writing copy, messages or call scripts] The pain is the pitch. Describe the avatar's pain in their own words, better than they can. Collect exact phrases from replies and calls into a phrase bank, and ask prospects directly what they want. Describe specific moments of pain rather than vague words, ideally one from the last 7 days, timed to when the pain peaks. Read public reviews of the tools and services they already use, and of direct alternatives to the offer (M4), and speak to what buyers complain about or say is missing. Be very specific about the problem and modest about the promised result; the aim is for them to feel understood. The bigger the pain, the more the owner can charge. What-Who-When, for any copy, paid or not: What covers the four value drivers and their opposites (the nightmare of staying as they are, the risk of not acting, how slow their current path is, the effort it takes without the offer); Who covers the people whose view of them changes (partners, staff, clients, rivals, family), both those who give status and those who take it away; When walks them through past, present and future, showing the cost of deciding and of not deciding now. Longer copy just covers more angles; keep the opening and the call to action the same. Any line naming a risk carries one specific, doable step in the same message, or the risk line goes: fear without a step breeds denial. Where the buyer has a team, test an owner-benefit line against a team-benefit line (L63). The shared enemy is always a thing, never a person, firm, regulator or vendor (O2, F27). Tag each logged phrase as win, retrieve, escape or stop, lead problem-aware hooks with escape or stop (L76), and test a destination-led opener against the pain-led one (L63). Match length to the medium: cold messages stay short with one benefit (L10); long-form assets can stack many benefits under one big idea (O3), each a true, checkable claim from the claims file (O47), every line earning its place, never many ideas without structure. In replies and call notes, log the hidden feeling as well as the problem, in their exact words with any inference marked (C4), and answer the strongest one in the pre-call video and on the lead magnet page. Once a week, spend about 30 minutes reading the trade press and forums the avatar reads, read only and never posting (L4), and log their phrases and the headlines and threads with the most replies into the phrase bank and the L73 swipe file; under F2 it counts only if it feeds hooks or the phrase bank.
- O6 [designing the offer or writing copy] Win on at least one of speed, risk or ease: deliver in a third of the time, reverse their biggest fear, or do the prep for them. Speed sells more than size: lead with the time the offer gives back before the next moment that matters to them. The same result faster can carry a higher price (no figures in copy until proven, O15). Never drop a speed, risk or ease idea because it seems unaffordable: cost it in the owner's hours or money, build that into the price (M2), and test it on 5 to 10 calls; keep it only if prospects say it changes their decision. Run the first version of any costly promise in a capped form (one group, a limited window, the owner only), with its worst-case cost in hours and money written against the M26 runway first, and widen it only after an L43-sized sample. Rule of thumb: look at what everyone else does, then do it in half the time for twice the price. Levers for each: speed comes from templates for repeated work (ads, emails, pages, presentations, steps), since decisions are usually the slowest part; from preparing ahead for predictable demand; and from availability, since more open slots and longer staffed hours let buyers start sooner and often raise conversion and the price they will pay (check the cost of the extra hours against the extra sales). Risk falls through consistency (list every variable that affects the client's result, control as many as possible and add to the list as new ones are found), through reputation over time, and through guarantees while there is no reputation (O8). Ease comes from removing what is hard rather than adding features: ask clients what is hard at each step from first contact to delivery, remove the items one at a time, never make them repeat information between calls or staff (pass notes forward and open with them, C4), and record each client's history so whoever serves them next knows it (D5). Being cheapest is a fourth way to win, but only as a whole-business design chosen at the start (automation, AI, lower-cost labour and a model built so clients keep paying even in a bad month), never as a price cut to an existing offer (M2, M3); internal use of AI or offshore staff need not be advertised, but never deny or misstate it, and client data sent abroad follows X8. In marketing, lead with the one way that matters most to the avatar, not all of them, since buyers don't take in several benefits at once; fast often beats free (O27). Name internal skill levels the staff earn by measured experience (a stated number of completed jobs and a quality check), and sell the higher tier on them; say they are the business's own levels, never implying an outside accreditation (X9).
- O7 [designing delivery or onboarding] Remove effort: walk the client's journey, list every action they must take, and cut them one by one.
- O8 [writing, stating or choosing any guarantee] Risk is the biggest objection to any purchase, so reversing it is the fastest way to lift conversions; spend as much time on the guarantee as on the deliverables. State it as "if you don't get X by Y, I will Z": the consequence gives it teeth. Four types: unconditional (pay, try, refund for any reason), conditional (terms attached, ideally better than money back), anti-guarantee (all sales final, with a strong, true reason), and implied (performance pay: no performance, no pay). Guarantees can be stacked, for example an unconditional short-term guarantee plus a conditional longer one, or milestones in sequence. Broad unconditional guarantees suit low-ticket consumer sales; for high-ticket business-to-business services, use specific guarantees, not broad unconditional ones. With high delivery costs, prefer conditional or anti-guarantees, because a refund loses both the fee and the cost of delivery. Every condition costs the guarantee some teeth, so use only conditions that predict success: make them the actions that predict success (attend sessions, do the weekly tasks, share before-and-after numbers), so buyers who only want a way out are filtered out and everyone who qualifies gets the result. Match their investment with an equal or bigger commitment from the owner. Most people would rather have the result than a refund. Milestone guarantees with dates in sequence pace them into the future, make the result feel likely, and show the owner is serious. Frame it as letting them decide from the inside, as you'd look inside a house before buying, and quote real support response times. To build one: list their biggest fear, pain and obstacle across time, emotion and outside costs, and reverse each; the more specific and creative, the better. Give it a vivid, memorable name, never "satisfaction guarantee", and state it boldly with the reason the owner can offer it. Even with no guarantee, address risk head-on. Do the maths, not the emotions: compare the extra sales against the extra refunds before choosing. A guarantee enhances a good offer but can't rescue a bad product or weak sales. Start with a service-based guarantee or performance pay, then either keep it and scale or move to less restrictive guarantees to increase volume. Consider giving the strongest guarantee only to clients who pay upfront ("Would you rather pay less today or get all your money back?"): upfront payers are more committed and it brings cash forward (M20). The current guarantee stack is in the instructions box; options are in O42. Judge the guarantee at the first point after delivery when results can show, and log refund and claim requests by delivery week. Give the full terms before they commit and honour every valid claim promptly (see X11).A guarantee can also be sold rather than given: offer the standard service, and a stronger guarantee as a paid option, stating plainly what it adds beyond the buyer's legal rights, which are never sold as an extra (X3). Track how many buy it and how many claim, and drop or reprice it if claims cost more than it earns.
- O9 [a prospect raises a non-price objection] Solve it once, turn the fix into a templated bonus, and keep a stack of bonuses matched to objections. Keep a written, rehearsed bank of spoken answers too (C14): any objection that surprises the owner means they were underprepared.
- O10 [designing the offer or its messaging] List what the buyer must believe to buy and cut it to one belief. Use free content to settle the rest beforehand, each with the belief-breaking formula in C14. Build that list from the real objections and stalls logged on calls and in replies (C14, O9), not from guesses, refresh it whenever the log changes, and give each recurring objection at least one free piece aimed at it; a piece first takes the old belief apart, then offers the new one. Write that belief as one internal sentence in the instructions box, never as public copy: "if [the avatar] believes [the new opportunity] is the key to [their desire], and [the offer] is the fastest way to get it, the rest falls away"; never "the only way" in public (O2; see X9). If an alternative the buyer could choose instead would meet the sentence just as well, it is an improvement offer: rework it round the new mechanism until it can't.
- O11 [writing copy or answering doubts] Admit relevant weaknesses first, then "but" and the strength that matters, never the other way round: what follows "but" is what sticks. Keep a written list of the owner's and the business's true flaws (new, small, no case studies yet, and so on), each with a ready "flaw, but strength" line written as the upside of that same flaw, including one that recognises the buyer's own expertise (X4). Add the limits a buyer wouldn't spot alone, true only, each with its "but". A major flaw (a poor result, a method that fails for part of the avatar) is never a copy line: fix the product first and disclose it wherever it affects their decision (M27). A damaging admission lands better with a light touch of humour. If a line feels fake to say, something is overstated: state exactly what has been done. Never lie. Never imply a bigger business than it is: a one-person business says "I", not "our team", with no implied staff, offices or head count. When an offer sounds too good to be true, open the call by listing what is bad about it and who it won't work for, before any benefit: buyers then believe the good part, and those who can't live with the trade-offs rule themselves out.
- O12 [writing posts, emails or pages] Say who it is for and who it isn't, using the avatar's real limits (too small, too big, missing the basics the offer needs, or wanting the work taken off their hands when the offer is done with them). The "not for" callout is stronger than the "for" one; pair it with a free alternative (the do-it-yourself version). State the real commitment plainly in copy and on calls (who on their side, the hours a week, the tasks): buyers who flinch at it would miss the guarantee conditions anyway (O8). Polarise on method and fit, never on people (L75). After the case study, one line names the true, evidenced variety inside the avatar, always with the not-for line and never wider than L2.
- O13 [writing promotions, hooks or angles; or responses are dropping; or an offer is tiring] Keep the core offer the same and change only the wrapper. Offers fatigue over years, not months, and faster in a small market; most prospects miss the first mention, so reaching a list once isn't fatigue. Vary the creative and copy and come back. When responses drop, change things in this order, moving down only once the earlier step has stopped working: (1) the creative (images, video, examples); (2) the body copy; (3) the headline or offer name (O15), including the reason why or season; (4) the duration; (5) the enhancer (bonus, free element, promotion); (6) only as a last resort, the whole money model (prices and sequence of offers). The lower the step, the heavier it is to run. Once an offer makes money, rarely change it: rinse and repeat. To start, pick the offer and headline most likely to work, stick with it, and keep whichever version gives the highest return; rotate between proven versions only if it adds no operational drag. Produce many variations from real angles (true dates and seasons in the market), not one polished version. Test a loss-framed line against the gain-framed version, true figures only (O15; see X9), and test a revenue angle against a time angle. Before building a new offer, check whether the current one keeps and refers clients (M9, L65): needing new offers again and again means the first one wasn't good enough. Keep a "dos" list of every concrete thing the offer does for a client, mark which ones copy has used, add a column naming the value each item serves, and lead the next hook with an unused one (steps 2 and 3). When a hook tires, twist the angle on the mechanism, never escalate the claim (O15). For a challenger, write from scratch without looking at the control (a peer, or a fresh assistant chat working only from the O5 phrase bank and the claims file, O47, so hooks still come from buyers' words, L73), and test it against the control (L63). Before committing to a big idea, ask: did it make the owner gasp at first sight, does the owner wish they'd thought of it, is it unique, does it fit the strategy, could it run for years? Then place it on the line between mainstream (what free guidance and vendors already say) and crazy (anything buyers would dismiss or that breaks a [Fixed] rule); only the sweet spot between goes ahead. Once one works, keep it: a working message doesn't wear out after one showing. Never call the offer tired or failed while the dos list has unused items: first test a from-scratch challenger built on an unused one, then move down the refresh order or to L3(b). Peak buying days (a sale weekend, a season, the business's own anniversary): give the days the preparation of a normal month of sales, and build the page or offer for those days only: navigation cut to the sale items, the few best sellers first, one clear "start here" offer for first-time buyers (often the subscription or continuity entry, M40), and a banner naming the day with a real end date (O37 [Fixed]). Fewer options usually convert better, but the evidence is mixed, so test the number shown (O18) rather than assume.
- O14 [designing a lead magnet or free offer] Solve one narrow problem that reveals the next one the core offer solves, ideally one that gets worse while they wait. Never solve the core problem free. Free services only for qualified prospects (need, budget, authority, timing). To pick a lead magnet, list what the avatar already pays others for, then make a better narrow version free.
- O15 [naming the offer, a promotion, a bonus or a lead magnet] People are drawn to what resembles them, so the name must call out the avatar, attracting the right buyers and repelling the wrong ones. Use the MAGIC formula, picking 3 to 5 parts in whatever order sounds punchiest: Magnet (a reason why: a season, event, deadline, launch, or a free or limited element); Avatar (as specific as possible but no more, and the more local the better: the town or city at minimum and a sub-area where possible, rather than the whole country); Goal (their dream outcome, specific and tangible); Interval (how long it takes); Container (a word that signals a bundle, such as Blueprint, Intensive, Fast Track, Reset, System or Accelerator). Shorter and punchier wins. Rhyme or alliteration helps but is never forced, and rhyme may carry a tagline or P.S. only when its claim is marked proven in the claims file (O47) and tested against plain wording (L63). Say-it test: before using any offer, bonus or business name, say it once on the phone to someone outside the business and keep it only if they can say it back and spell it well enough to find it; also check a buyer could say "we're a [name] client". For bonus and template names only, test one unexpected but still descriptive name against a plain one (L63). Name every bonus and sub-item with the same formula. Other formats: number plus outcome plus time frame; how to X without Y, even if Z (their biggest fear); X mistakes that stop Y; the [adjective] [container] that [good result]; the unwritten [X] that make or break [Y], never implying official rules or requirements (see X9). True calendar reasons can carry a promotion, but where the market has real deadlines, those beat jokey ones. Test 2 or 3 names at once, keep the winner as the control, test new names against it, and don't get emotional about which wins. A name that pairs a measurable result with a time frame is an objective claim: until results prove it, use goals that aren't figures (see X9). An offer gets called a scam because of what it promises, not what it teaches: only make promises that are justifiable for nearly every buyer who does the work. Before any new name, template, page, script or letter goes out, run the O47 claims check.
- O16 [writing an offer doc or pitch] Five parts: outcome, weekly deliverables, investment, risk reversal, and a true reason to act now (a real date in the market, real capacity). The reason must be true (see X10).
- O17 [writing any call to action] Make it clear: the exact next step, a reason ("because..."), and what happens next, then make sure it happens. Each line sells only the next step: the subject sells the open, the first line the read, the email a reply, the reply the call, the confirmation the show-up. Ask often, with value between asks. End emails with a P.S., the second most-read line, given the same care as the subject line: in cold emails use it for the "not for" line or a one-line recap for skimmers; on the owner's own list use it for small rewards so people learn to read it, and add a P.P.S. only in longer emails to that list. In long assets, end each part with a reason to go on: each video clip names what the next one answers, each section of the offer doc or case study ends with a one-line lead into the next, and page 1 of a letter ends with a reason to turn over. In the offer doc, the lead magnet results page and the pre-call video, put a booking route at the end of each section with the guarantee's name next to it, as an escape route for skimmers (never in cold emails, L10; full guarantee terms before they commit, O8). In letters, the offer doc, the case study and video scripts, read every paragraph's first line as if it were a headline and rewrite or cut the weak ones; the punch comes from specific facts and moments, never hype or exclamation marks (L11, L50). When the outer piece (subject line, envelope, page headline) is strong, open the inner piece more quietly and let the specifics carry it.
- O18 [testing marketing] Test in this order: offer, headline, image, then the tools in the path (forms, booking, payment). For each prospect-facing tool (booking page, lead magnet form, e-signature, payment sign-up), try 2 or 3 alternatives in turn and keep the one with the highest completion rate once volume gives readable samples (L43); keep the loser as the L36 backup.
- O19 [planning content, the website or emails] Give away the how for free, more than others charge for: list every deliverable, write specific problems for each, and publish the solutions in scrambled order. Clients pay for the full sequence and the implementation. Value in advance earns trust. In content and pitches that sell, teach mostly how and why things work (what is possible, what changes the result, the numbers behind it), which the viewer gets value from just by listening; sell the step-by-step doing, since a list of tasks given before the sale leaves people feeling they have enough to do already.
- O20 [making content or videos] Put the effort in before recording: idea, hook and notes; editing only makes it clearer. For one-to-many assets (the lead magnet page, the pre-call video script, a letter, the case study), split the time roughly 40% research (O47), 20% body, 20% headlines and 20% checking; cold messages stay send before tweaking (L16, L50).
