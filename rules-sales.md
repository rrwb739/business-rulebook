## Sales (C)

- C1 [a prospect shows buying interest] [Fixed] Close on a call. Send the offer document and payment link straight after the yes, never before the call for the prospect to "review with the others". When they're ready, stop selling and take payment: go straight to the agreement and the payment set-up, and read the key details back before hanging up (legal name, account holder, invoice email) so the first collection doesn't fail. In person, a payment method may be taken before the decision only with the true reason said plainly (so paying is quick if they go ahead), stored by a compliant payment provider, never charged without a separate yes to the amount, and deleted if they don't buy; never under a pretext, and no copy of an ID document unless the work truly needs it (see X8, X9). Put the agreement in writing every time, with the same line: so there are no unspoken expectations. When scope or terms are agreed in writing after a call, include the owner's photo, a one-line true bio (F21) and one short true personal line. Have the decision maker type their own details into the agreement (legal name, the named owner of the work on their side, invoice email), never in a way that discourages any exit right (O36, X3). If the owner quotes a price, bonus or term in error and the buyer says yes, honour it for that buyer and fix the template: never correct a quoted term upwards after a yes, and check the offer document against the call sheet before sending. If asked to "send over a proposal", agree, then book a time to walk through it with every decision maker, never leaving it to be read cold, and log that walkthrough as the next step (C11). Before the first call that could close, run a live end-to-end payment test (agreement, payment form and link, a small test payment refunded) and see it land in the business account; after each sale, check the agreement and amounts are entered correctly in billing. Once the buyer says yes, stop selling and move straight to payment and the next step: everything said after the yes only adds chances to reverse it. Card details are entered by the buyer into the payment provider's own page or terminal, never written down or keyed by the seller from a card handed over (X8).
- C2 [setting up the sales process] [Fixed] Before calls, send a short video answering common questions in the prospect's words, and ask qualifying questions: the size of their problem in the avatar's own unit, who handles it now, whether the problem is big enough that they could reasonably pay (without quoting the price yet), who decides, timing (a date they name for having it fixed), how much of their team's time they could give (confirm size and time as a pair when both are needed for the result), and who on their side will own the work afterwards (no owner is a red flag). Screen every prospect against the F12 red flags. Add questions that put a cost on the problem and project it forward. Invite every decision maker to the call; if one can't come, move the call. Before the price step, confirm by name who signs (L8) and when the owner last spoke to them directly, and give the senior buyer the business-level value and the day-to-day user the daily-work value, with the same facts and price (M2). When the enquirer isn't the decision maker, treat it as a buying signal: give the help they asked for first, ask what made the decision makers want it looked at now and what they'd need to see, reach a decision maker only with the enquirer's agreement or a three-way introduction, and tag pre-call clips by role. Pre-call video, so the call can go on their concerns rather than a pitch: (1) promise, pain, proof and plan in under 30 seconds, with the owner's name and one-line role as a caption so the spoken intro stays under 5 seconds; (2) the mechanism as old way against new way, or the hidden problem behind the visible one (O4), then the three pillars in 60 to 90 seconds; (3) the top 5 objections, each looped through the C14 belief-breaking formula, including "why hasn't this been solved yet?" and "why this beats the alternatives" (O2); (4) what happens on the call, who will ring, and one small, easy question to answer before the call; then proof, stacked, true only (O11, O47; until client results exist, the owner's own true results, F21). Put a new hit (a proof point, a specific pain moment, a surprising true fact, a short true story) every 20 to 30 seconds, timed on the draft and checked against viewer drop-off. Open every clip with the same 10 to 15-second line (name, who it's for, the one promise, who it isn't for), checked at D15; title clips as real buyer questions or true curiosity lines with no figures (O15); end each teaching segment by naming the next problem the core offer solves (O14); make the last clip a "you're probably wondering" list from the L59 log, each answer a checked claim (O47). Send a one-page written summary with the video. Keep it visibly current and re-record when its time reference passes; never imply a recording is live or present a clip as anything but a sales asset (see X9, X14). Pre-call form: honest, useful work, always presented as the first step of the diagnostic for a sales call, never as a survey (F25); ask two or three questions first (also for the D8 count questions), then send the fuller form once they've answered, with a personal named line and a thank you, never a bare link; ask about the problem from several angles (size, who handles it, how long, what the next 5 years look like if nothing changes). Before any one-to-many asset, fill in a one-page brief: the category of one (O2), the one belief (O10), the stack (O32), the true origin story (F21) and the three beliefs (vehicle, internal, external), each with a true story; each quarter, sort the C14 false-belief list into those three columns and check the clips answer the top one in each. In the O20 checking pass, read each clip against: does this make sense, could the buyer do this, do they need it; does it back their goal, credit past attempts truly, answer fears with evidence, confirm only true suspicions and name a problem, never a person, as the enemy. Build the first full video from the pitch as delivered live on about 20 calls. Read viewer drop-off: fix the biggest drop first and expand the parts people rewatch; if a recorded asset isn't converting, deliver that part live for a while, then re-record. Tests (L63, L15): the pre-call form against the short form on show and close rate, watching bookings; versions differing only in their examples; 3 or 4 short clips, one per key belief, the last openly asking them to book, against the single video on views to bookings; two opener sets for the buyer camps; the order hook, one belief, origin story, three belief stories, booking. Prioritise those ready now. Where a first sale opens an upsell or menu conversation (M32), send a second, separate short video before that conversation, about what buyers usually don't know about the rest of their situation, with true proof from similar clients, so the conversation starts framed. Pre-framing: the questions on the booking form or application shape what buyers do next, so ask about the outcome and the option the buyer wants (for example whether they want the fuller version) before they see it, using only real options; the pre-call video explains plainly what the offer is and how it works, so buyers arrive knowing what they're booking. Add good friction (qualifying questions, the price range, real requirements) that turns poor fits away and keeps good fits, never bad friction (slow pages, broken steps, walls nobody can pass); good friction usually raises the cost per booked call and raises the return and the sales team's efficiency more, so judge it on gross profit per pound and calls closed per seller, not on cost per call (M23, L15). In the pre-call video, say early that price ranges and timings come at the end, so viewers stay; give the ranges and expectations there, then ask them to come knowing their budget, with everyone who decides, and their timeline; about five to seven minutes is enough. [shape: e-commerce] Where a store sells both standard and custom work, put a custom-order route (form plus calendar in one step) on every product page and in a fixed banner, so buyers with bigger needs reach a call. Count the calls or conversations it takes to close on average and cut them by moving the information buyers need to decide into the pre-call video, refreshed as new objections come up on calls; fewer and shorter calls per sale raise sales per seller. When booking from outreach, offer the video as a choice (watch it before the call or together); if a prospect arrives without having watched a short video, play it at the start of the call so every call runs the same script, rather than keeping two versions. Build the video's FAQ section from the questions and objections actually heard on calls, in the prospect's own phrasings: the same objection said several ways ("I'm really busy", "I've got a lot on") gets each phrasing named and answered. Put the price in the video when sales capacity is short and friction is wanted; when sellers have spare capacity, the price may wait for the call, but say on the video that prices come at the end. A short taste of the lead magnet can sit inside the video to keep viewers watching. When one-to-one enquiries swamp the owner, invite every enquirer to one regular live group presentation (weekly, say) and answer the most common questions there, in order of how often they come up, keeping one-to-one time for the paid assisted route (M7).
- C3 [a lead books a call] [Fixed] Contact them straight away; offer an earlier slot if possible; send a small personal message before the call to raise the show rate. If they're free when the owner rings and every decision maker can join, run the call there and then; otherwise qualify briefly (C2) and book the earliest slot that works for all of them. On the booking or qualifying ring, clarify, label and size the problem only, with no solution, so they arrive wanting it. Show-up often moves sales more than call skill, so fix it first: below about 70% show, show rate is the problem, and a low rate can mean the wrong people are booking (L15). Default to calls the same or next working day from fixed call windows on set days (F23), never more than 3 working days out except to reach the next set call day; when there's a gap, ring those booked days ahead and offer an earlier slot, saying a slot has opened only when one truly has (O11). Offer times the buyer can take within the owner's set hours (F1), avoid the buyer's known busy periods, use 15-minute start times, offer "ring me now" alongside the link, open as many slots as the hours allow and favour slots with nothing straight after them. Never block out fake slots or signal idle capacity: offer two specific times, and say the week is filling only when logged as true (O36). Put the owner's name and photo on the booking confirmation with who will call and from which number, use the same clear, recent, unedited photo and sender name on every channel, and host the C2 pre-call video and one true proof line on the confirmation page. Dress as the buyer would for a client meeting, never props implying a role not held: looking credible never stands in for proof (O25). If anyone else calls leads, show that person, chosen on skill (see X18). End every booking with "if anything changes, will you let me know?" and wait for the yes; on a phone booking, ask them to put it in their diary on the line and read the time back. A free preference question before the call lifts show-up. Reminders: a personal message 24 hours before that shows prep (one observation about their business), then the morning of, then an hour before; each sells one reason to turn up (rotating pain, proof, speed, ease, with the dream outcome in their words) and never mentions how many people miss calls; voice notes or video only on a channel they've already used; anything automated must look automated and never pretend to be the owner; reminder texts are service messages with no pitch (see X2). The booking form asks for a mobile and a preferred reminder channel. Leave voicemails by hand (X2). If booked more than 2 days out, at most one touch a day until the call, and extra assets only when they reply or ask. No gifts to get them to turn up. Ring a no-show the same day: assume something came up, one light line at most, a specific new slot, no guilt; one who doesn't rebook gets the pre-call clips one at a time, at most one a day, then falls under L12. Move unconfirmed bookings with honest wording, never a made-up excuse. Log show rate before and after each change (L30) and test (L63): a short video on the confirmation against photo only; a last service text 10 to 15 minutes before; one teaser line in a reminder; a shorter stated call length, always the length the call really takes (O11); and, only where the owner will truly do it, saying at booking what will be prepared for them before the call (a short audit or plan for their situation) and that it is walked through on the call, not sent ahead. Show rate rises from many small fixes rather than one trick: keep the list of what has been tried with its before and after numbers. When a follow-up call is booked on a call, send the invite while they are still on the line, ask them to accept it, and ask them to text if anything comes up so the slot can go to someone else (only if that is true). Availability is the biggest lever on how many leads book: offer as many call slots as the owner's set hours genuinely allow, across as many days as possible, in short start increments (15 minutes), and aim for same-day or next-day calls, since show rates fall the further out a call is booked. When a slot genuinely frees up, offer it to someone booked later. When someone misses a call, send one light, warm message the same day with no guilt (assume something came up), restate what is set aside for them only if true, and offer the same time the next day; if they miss again, move on. No-show ladder, lightest first, stepping up only while no-shows stay high: (1) an honest commitment check after the time is agreed (the slot costs real money if missed; can they keep it?); (2) a card held with a stated no-show or late-cancellation fee that reflects the real loss, told plainly before booking (X3, X11 for consumers: the fee must be fair and clear); (3) a paid consultation credited against anything they buy; (4) a paid consultation, not credited. Work out what the missed share of slots costs in profit before deciding it would scare people away. For in-person services, a small paid first appointment (a heavily discounted assessment) usually raises the show rate well above free bookings; take the payment at booking with the card details kept only with the client's consent and under the payment provider's rules (C1, X8), sell at the end of the assessment, when need is highest, and never charge any no-show fee not stated before booking (X3).For in-person appointments, a small paid booking or a card held at booking raises show rates; state the true reason plainly (the slot is held for them; whether the fee is credited or refunded), never a vague "it's just our policy" in place of the reason (X3, X9). Collect before the visit whatever the owner needs to price the plan (eligibility, cover, last year's figures) so the plan and price are ready on the day.
- C4 [preparing for a sales call] Spend about 10% of the call's length on research, about 90% of it on the prospect (their pains, situation, past attempts) rather than the product, and reread the notes so they never repeat themselves. Use one research fact in the first minute and bring one pre-worked observation about their business; for a new, unknown supplier, prep stands in for reputation, so bring one worked idea inside the offer's scope, never advice the owner isn't qualified to give (X4). Research stays on the prospect's own business sources (website, directories, public registers, reviews, public professional roles), never profiles of named people built from social media (X2). At the start of any second call, read the notes back, re-run the short agenda and briefly re-clarify the problem in their words. For an unusual name, note a phonetic spelling, rehearse it, and if unsure ask once. For a rare, high-stakes meeting, prep for longer. Before a call with a dismissive or upset person, write down one true thing to respect about them or their business, and say it only if honest and relevant. Notes stay on the business and the decision: record exact words, actions and dates, never a guess at what they meant, mark any inference as an inference, and record personal details only if offered and relevant (see X8). Where the person who pays and the person who must do the work or approve it day to day are different people, find out who really decides, and make the pitch about that person's standing and reduced workload, not only the payer's saving.Prep ratio: while a kind of meeting is new, prepare for about a quarter of its length (15 minutes for an hour), shrinking towards a tenth as the same kind of meeting is repeated and prep itself gets faster; for a one-off, high-stakes or one-to-many event (a talk, a large pitch, a presentation to a whole team), prep can run to many times the event's length, scaled to the audience and what is at stake, with diminishing returns. Prep as close to the event as possible. Before recommending anything on a client's or prospect's selling, look at it from outside first, as a buyer would (their website, pages, ads and follow-up), rather than spending the call fact-finding what can be seen. Let the prep show in the first minute: it signals effort that talk can't.
- C5 [on a sales call] [Fixed] Follow CLOSER: clarify why they're here, label the problem, overview past attempts, sell the destination, explain away concerns, reinforce the decision. Run the diagnostic inside it: they score where they are and their goal, and the price is tied to that gap. Listen twice as much as you talk. Run the full sequence on every call, warm and referred included, so a lost call shows which step failed (F24, C13). (1) Open: if they watched the video or used the lead magnet, start with what stood out; if not, offer to let them watch it now or hear the short version, never a condition of the call (M27). Ask one genuine, specific question from the C4 research, then give a 15 to 20-second agenda (proof, promise, plan, call length, "if it doesn't make sense, no problem, and you'll leave with something useful either way"), framed as a diagnosis: if the owner can't help, say so as soon as it's clear. The agenda may include whether they qualify for the guarantee or a bonus, on real criteria applied to everyone (O8, L2). End with "does that sound fair?" and wait (L47); at the stated time, ask whether to carry on or rebook. (2) Clarify: 2 or 3 true yes questions from their own reply, never a trick; one change they already pulled off; "what made you reply?", why now, why that goal; play their words back and get the problem confirmed. Ask one future question, then where they are today against it. Dig with "tell me more" and "can you give me an example?" until there's a specific moment with a number, then a ladder of why questions, stopping at business standing, never personal ground. Hard gate: no ask until they've said in their own words why they need a fix. By about minute 8 know their size, who handles it and the biggest pain, with nothing about the owner before that. Treat easy early agreement as a reason to dig. Offer no fix in discovery. Ask for counts, not adjectives (D8), and use corrected numbers without comment. Ask whether the goal is a must-hit and what a miss costs. The L34 magic question may come once the problem is labelled. (3) Label: "you're at X, you want Y, Z is holding you back", in their words plus the true cause or cost they hadn't put into words (O5). (4) Overview past attempts (what, how long, how it went, "what else?" until nothing's left), then what they already have and don't fully use (O2, O28). Credit what each attempt gave and name the missing piece, never running down named tools; free them from a past choice without loss of face, true only. Put a figure on the cost. Collect budget, authority and timing inside these questions. Ask "if we didn't speak again, what's the plan?" and judge it honestly: if it would work, say so (M27, C14). Write each roadblock in their exact words: the pitch uses only these. Note what they liked and disliked about each past attempt: link the offer to what they liked, and never lead with anything that resembles what they disliked. Bring an overconfident buyer back with one true question and steady an unsure one by normalising the doubt, logged as an inference (C4). Then ask permission to explain. (5) Sell the destination in 60 to 90 seconds, never over 3 minutes: the three pillars set in the instructions box, each with one statement, one analogy from their world, one commitment matching the guarantee conditions, mapped by name to a roadblock they gave, with one true proof example (O15; until client results exist, the owner's own, F21). Mention only features they care about; never hold back anything that would change their decision (M27). (6) Before the price, raise likely obstacles yourself, name and probe any hesitation, re-check budget, decision makers and timing, check priority ("if not, we'll stop here"), recap the pillars in their words and ask how things would go with all three running. Any big numbers before the price are true and relevant, never anchors (M27, O11). (7) Ask using their goal in their words: current state, goal, obstacle, path, price, then the saving. Let a neutral measure (their diagnostic or own log) show the gap; after a live demo on one case, point to how many more they have, then price (C7). Say what paying means, inside the guarantee terms (O8, O15). Make the last thing shown before each ask a true reminder of their gap or win. (8) Reinforce: after payment, restate proof, promise and plan in their words, book the kickoff and pass notes to onboarding (D7). With no decision or a missing decision maker, email a short recap in their words within a working day, asking them to correct it, with no ask (L30). Throughout: speak in questions, restatements and short stories; answer price, terms, exclusions and data straight. Never disagree; ask permission before a hard truth; use only true third-party foils where the owner lacks authority. Never tell them they're a good fit: ask until they say it, and decide privately against L2 and F12. Ask "what would you want to be sure of before deciding?", never "any questions?". Never say "ignore the price for now"; correct any misstatement in writing. Meet dismissal with one calm true line, and experience with one true respect line and the owner's narrow lane. Bring in the quietest person by name. Correct only facts that affect the decision, via the published source (X4, D16). With several decision makers, build more than one relationship and test a stated 60 to 90-minute working session with all of them (never the full offer sequence, O14; L63). Never use real client data in demos (X5). Record only with consent (see X8). Pre-handle the usual concerns inside the discovery questions rather than meeting them at the end: have they set money aside for solving this, has anyone who shares the decision seen it, have they bought something like this before and how did it go (C14). Never give away the full diagnosis and plan as free consulting before the offer; the call diagnoses, the offer prescribes. Once they say yes, ask one plain question to surface any hidden doubt, such as "apart from something unexpected, is there anything that would stop you starting?", and answer it there and then; this is a check, never pressure, and a real doubt is resolved or the sale is not taken (M27). If they ask for a proposal, ask what they'd need to see in it, answer that on the call, ask again, and if it still needs a document, book the next call before hanging up (C11).Sell at the point of greatest need: where the service relieves pain or a problem on the first visit, do the assessment, then present the plan and price, then deliver the first help, not the other way round, so the buyer decides while the need is felt; still deliver what was promised for the visit whatever they decide (X3).
- C6 [presenting bonuses on a one-to-one call] Ask for the sale first, on the core offer. If they say yes, reveal the bonuses after they've signed, to confirm the decision. If they say no, agree, add the one bonus that removes their stated obstacle and ask again whether that's fair; repeat with the next bonus if needed. Each bonus carries its genuine stated value (O39, X3) and removes a stated obstacle, never a shiny extra to blur a gap in the numbers, with the total shown in writing (M32). Never discount to close: adding value keeps a position of strength, while discounting teaches buyers that prices are negotiable. This order is for calls only: one-to-many assets stack before the ask (O32).
- C7 [presenting the price] [Fixed] Use the return close with their own numbers: "if this saved you X, worth Y a year, would [price] be worth it?" Get the cost from them first, as their own rough estimate, and use their number, never the owner's; work the sum live with deliberately cautious inputs, labelled as an illustration until client results prove it (O15). Before the price, close off "that was quick" once with a yes-framed line, only if true and never naming a time not yet measured, and keep the price on the value, never the owner's hours (M2). Give one true reason for the price, never implying a guaranteed result (O8, O15). M2 sets the price where the gap to the value is clear, so buyers feel it's a steal and stay and refer (L65). Just before the number, say plainly that it is expensive (or a serious investment), so the buyer hears it from the owner first and the owner never sounds apologetic; then state the price, stop talking; if the owner chokes on it, show it in writing on screen (M32). "That's expensive" is usually a reaction: agree and carry on. A money objection is usually a value gap: go back to the return close before any payment plan (M37). A run of calls with no flinch means the price is too low (M33). If the owner can't say the price with full belief, fix the offer, not the pitch (O1): reread client results (until they exist, the owner's own, F21) before every call block and after a run of noes, rewatch the owner's best calls, and book calls soon after a client win. Self-check: would the owner pay this in the buyer's place? When calls stall, ask whether conviction, expertise or rapport is low before changing the script. Never blame a client who didn't get the result: ask what would have made the next one succeed and fix that gap. Label any multi-year value illustration as such until sourced (see X9). Tests on 10 calls each (L63): a true pre-frame ("it isn't cheap") then a pause against the plain statement, watching the flinch rate; a loss-framed return close against the gain version, with their own cautious numbers (O13, O15).
