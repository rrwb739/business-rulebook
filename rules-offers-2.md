## Offers (O)

### Building the Grand Slam offer

- O30 [building or rebuilding the offer] Build the Grand Slam offer in five steps. (1) Dream outcome: sell where they arrive, not the vehicle. (2) Problems: list every problem in the order the client meets it, before, during and after using the offer, in insane detail; for each task write the four objections (not worth it, won't work for us, too hard, takes too long). More problems means more value to create. (3) Solutions: turn each problem into a "how to..." solution. (4) Delivery: list every way to deliver each solution (O31). (5) Trim and stack (O32); in this step, ask of each element whether it makes the buyer look capable to the people around them or risks looking foolish, and rename or cut it. The working version lives in the instructions box; update it as you learn. Before building content for a new version, write its one-page offer doc, headline and session names and test them on calls. For a new offer or asset, also write a half-page internal launch note as if it had worked (who, the one problem, the one benefit, what the buyer would say, and the 5 questions a buyer would ask first, with answers) and build only if 1 or 2 avatar members find it compelling; the note is never published or presented as fact (O47).
- O31 [step 4 of O30, or stuck for ideas] Think divergently: many solutions to one problem before choosing. Use the delivery cube: level of attention (one to one, small group, one to many); their effort (do it yourself, done with you, done for you); live medium (in person, phone, video call, email, chat); recorded format (video, audio, written); reply speed (within minutes, within the hour, same day). Then run the 10x and one-tenth test: what would you give if they paid 10 times the price, and how would you still make them succeed at a tenth of it (O43)? While listing, judge nothing on cost (the price isn't set yet) and build on each idea rather than rejecting it: good ones often come on the third or fourth version. In trim (O32), don't default to zero-cost items only: high value at a modest cost is less crowded than free. Add service hours (all day or set hours) and days a week to reply speed: staffing so clients never wait costs efficiency, full staff use makes clients wait, and either can work if chosen on purpose. Live and in person is usually valued most and scales least; recorded and digital scales most and is valued least.
- O32 [step 5 of O30, or adding anything to the offer] Trim, then stack. Trim: remove high-cost, low-value items first, then low-cost, low-value items; keep low-cost, high-value and high-cost, high-value items, judging value with the value equation (O21). Stack: bundle what's left by problem, give each bundle a benefit-led name and a stated value, and show the total value against the price, so they think "I get all this for only that?" The bundle must solve every listed problem, make the offer impossible to compare, and give the owner conviction it is one of a kind. If a high-value item costs too much to deliver, step back to a lesser version deliverable at scale, one step at a time, until the cost is acceptable, or raise the price enough to make it worth it. Start building now: every piece of value created stacks up over time. Once a quarter, count every choice a buyer meets from first contact to signature (tiers, payment options, start dates, bonus names, lead magnet routes) and cut any that doesn't help them decide, keeping the M32 menu to a handful. In one-to-many assets, the stack comes before the ask: present it item by item on a running list in the order the buyer meets each problem (O30 step 2), so it reads as their route to the result, then the full list with values before the price; after the price, give the true comparison (C14) and the guarantee. On calls, C6 holds. The stated total should clearly exceed the price but stay well below the premium tier's price so the M33 anchor stays clear, and every value is a price genuinely charged or an honest replacement cost (X3), never "priceless" or a figure for what the result is worth. After the first round of clients, cut any bonus fewer than half of them actually used.
- O33 [a prospect gives a reason not to buy] Solve every perceived problem: a single unsolved one is enough to lose the sale. Don't get romantic about how to solve it: solve it the way they need, then turn the fix into a template or training so it never blocks a sale again (O9).
- O34 [choosing delivery methods] Favour high-value, one-to-many assets (calculators, templates, checklists, recorded walkthroughs): costly to build once, near-zero cost to deliver again, and they keep paying. Save one-to-one time for the biggest value adds. Judge every asset on value per minute (L46).

### Enhancers: scarcity, urgency and bonuses

- O35 [presenting, launching or promoting the offer] Enhancers change demand without changing the offer, and are often more powerful than the core offer: scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees and naming. Marketing's job is to raise demand and lower supply, or perceived supply. Keep supply below demand: sell fewer places at a higher price, and leave some desire unmet so demand builds for next time. Demand is uneven: a minority of prospects will pay far more, and selling to everyone at a low price kills future demand. The longer the build-up before the ask, the bigger the ask can be.
- O36 [selling places, groups or the lead magnet] [Fixed] Scarcity: every limit must be true (see X10). People want what they can't have and what others want; fear of loss beats desire for gain. Three types: limited places, limited bonuses, never available again. For services, use one or more caps: a total client cap, a weekly cap on new clients, or a group cap. Honest scarcity is easiest: set the real capacity, state it with the real count, and always say when it's full. Offer fewer places than you think you can sell, so you sell out and people remember it. A capped free offer works too, if the cap is real. If places go in order of sign-up, say so (first come, first served), only when true. If a place opens mid-way, tell the pipeline truthfully with the real condition, never identifying who left; the terms state upfront an exit for non-participation with a pro-rata refund (X3). Replied prospects and early clients hear real capacity changes and price rises (M5) before any wider send and get first refusal when places are short, and the owner says so only when true and logged. Even when true, never use live rivalry on a call ("another client is deciding on the last place today"): give the count and close date in writing. Scarcity decides when, never whether: every yes must stand on the return close (C7) and M27, and if a buyer would only buy because places are going, don't close.
- O37 [any sales conversation, campaign or page] [Fixed] Urgency: every deadline must be true (see X10). Deadlines drive decisions. Use (a) group start dates ("sign up today and you start Monday; otherwise it's the next group"); (b) dated, named promotions with real end dates, changed regularly; (c) urgency on a bonus or price rather than the service; (d) exploding opportunities: real dates in the market itself. Most sales come in the final hours before a deadline: don't extend it. Launch anything (groups, new offers) with Whisper, Tease, Shout: Whisper builds curiosity with short behind-the-scenes hints, every 4 to 6 weeks until 60 days out, then every 2 to 3 weeks until 30 days out (start as early as possible: the longer something seems to take, the more it's valued); Tease reveals the offer, the date and the value, once a week until 14 days out, then twice a week until 3 days out; Shout gives specific actions with bonuses, scarcity, urgency and guarantees for early buyers, at least twice a day from 3 days out, every few hours on the day, then every 30 minutes in the last 2 hours (adapt the frequency to the channel, never spam). Send true place-count updates as they change, with last week's count, never rounded or staged: newly scarce moves people more than always scarce. Buyers who join late but before the stated close get fast-track onboarding as a bonus; after the close the answer is no, with first refusal on the next group (more prep time, a longer payment plan). Never let anyone in after a stated deadline, or nobody believes the next one. Before each date-driven campaign, batch-write and bank the opener and follow-up variants weeks ahead, so the sending window is spent sending. After the first target, line up local and trade press ahead of any launch, never implying coverage the business doesn't have. Frame a true market date as permission, not pressure ("the deadline is a good reason to stop putting this off"), never as a threat (M27). During a launch, keep publishing the usual value at the usual volume and add the launch messages on top, never in place of it, since new people arrive during the launch with no goodwill yet; make the launch messages worth watching in themselves, like a good trailer.
- O38 [after the first target, F3] [stage: S3+] Extreme scarcity: a tiny, very high-priced tier of direct access to the owner. Cap it hard. In small groups, "once you leave, you can't come back" makes people think hard before leaving, but only as a real, written policy applied every time and stated before any sales call (O36, O37 [Fixed]), and only where the group is already valued; otherwise it isn't used. The evidence is weak, so treat it as a test, and never use it on the continuity offer (M42).
- O39 [building or presenting bonuses] An offer broken into parts and stacked as bonuses is worth more than the same offer presented as one thing: anchor the price on the core offer, then widen the gap between price and value with each bonus until saying no feels stupid. Rules for every bonus: (1) always offer them; (2) give each a name with the benefit in it; (3) say how it relates to their problem, what it is, how it was made or what it took, and how it makes things faster, easier or more certain; (4) give proof (a statistic, a past client, the owner's own experience); (5) paint a picture of life after using it; (6) give each a price tag and justify it; (7) prefer tools, checklists and templates over extra training, because they cost the client less time and effort; (8) each bonus kills a specific reason they think they can't succeed; (9) or solves the next problem before they hit it; (10) the bonuses' total value should exceed the core offer, and each bonus should on its own be the deciding reason for at least one type of buyer (stated values within X3); a bonus that needs explaining gets simplified or cut; (11) add scarcity or urgency to the bonuses themselves (only via this offer, limited in number, or a true, stated window after the call, the same for everyone, never a sign-today offer), always true and never to stop a buyer taking time to think (O36, O37). A big bonus stack also signals the core offer must be valuable. People buy because one thing is worth it, not because of the total: each bonus must on its own be worth more than the price (test: if only this were offered, would the right buyer still buy?), which forces fewer, sharper bonuses. Match bonuses to each type of buyer in the avatar and drop bonuses that mainly appeal to weaker-fit buyers: the bonuses promoted decide who is attracted. At most one named free asset per cold email (L47), called free only within X3. Bonus to test: a completion certificate for each person who finishes, labelled as the owner's own certificate, never an accreditation or endorsement (O25; see X9). Log every change to the bonus stack in the L63 test log and watch replies and bookings weekly, because response can collapse suddenly rather than fade. After the first target, test one bonus next to the core topic but not about it against a strictly on-topic bonus. A strong bonus also helps sell the next thing: log each group's bonus stack in the L63 test log next to its continuity take-up (M9), and keep the bonuses linked to higher take-up. Build at least one bonus per C2 belief: vehicle (a demonstration, then the case study), internal (checklists and recorded examples, D5) and external (client-facing templates and calendars). In one-to-many assets only, after the core offer ask once "if this were all you got, would it be worth it?", true only; calls keep C6.
- O40 [deciding what's core and what's a bonus, or building assets] Pull out the most distinct, high-value items that could almost stand alone (especially short, high-value tools like checklists and maps) and present them as bonuses so they don't get lost. Record every workshop, webinar, session and interview, with consent and no client personal data (see X5), and keep them with every tool and template in a bonus vault to use when an obstacle comes up. A month of a higher service level makes a good bonus and doubles as an upsell. List what the owner already uses to run the business (follow-up templates, trackers, research checklists) and offer the ones that fit the core offer as bonuses. Never offer legal documents as a bonus (see X4). Look for by-products the business normally throws away (offcuts, spare capacity, templates, recordings) and turn them into bonuses that cost almost nothing, ideally ones that bring the client back for a paid service.
- O41 [adding bonuses from other businesses] Other people's products: ask adjacent, non-competing businesses to give clients an exclusive discount or free offer in exchange for exposure to them. It must be a deal clients can't get by themselves: perks they already get free don't count. Aim for partner perks worth more than the price on their own. Line up several partners per need to multiply the perk, help partners shape their perk into a strong offer, and treat each partner as a goodwill and referral relationship. Turn down any partner whose reputation with the avatar would cheapen a premium, careful offer, even if they'd bring quick sales (the same check applies to affiliates, L69). Negotiate a group discount using the buying power of all clients, and a referral commission for the owner. Ranking, best to worst: pre-negotiated rates plus exclusive features plus commission, discounts plus commission, discounts only, the product alone (M18). Disclose any commission received to the client (X3). Keep clients out of commission chains for referring their own customers (see X15). From the first paid group, review each partner perk at the end of each group against client feedback; a partner that still misses the written standard after one written request is dropped under the agreement's terms, and affected clients are told plainly (L75).

### Guarantee menu and tests

- O42 [choosing or adding a guarantee] Guarantee menu (rate these against the current stack before changing it). Ways to pay out: a full refund, a partial refund, a refund of their extra costs, paying for a rival's programme, or a refund plus extra. Unconditional satisfaction: guarantee the level of service, not the result they control; best for lower-ticket offers. Service: keep working free until X is reached; it removes the time risk and is rarely claimed, because committed clients get close to the goal (for the owner's confidence only, never said to prospects). Modified service: an extra period of support, at least double the original, free; a finite exposure and an easier place to start. Delayed second payment: no second instalment until their first result is live; best with a systemised first result, and it focuses delivery on the activation step that predicts retention. First outcome: the owner covers an extra cost until they get their first result. Wage payment: if a session isn't worth their time, the owner pays their charge-out rate for the hours their staff spent in it; untested, so track it on the first clients and honour every valid claim promptly without demanding proof of their rate. Ancillary costs: refund costs around the purchase (for example travel to an in-person session) if it isn't worth it. Credit: a refund as credit towards another service; best for upsells. Personal service: the owner works with them one to one until X, with strict conditions that make failure unlikely. Release of contract: they can leave a continuity commitment free; costs nothing if the contract wouldn't be enforced anyway. Outsized refund: more back than they paid; only with conditions and high margins, best when the client has a lot to do and success is near certain if they do it. Anti-guarantee: all sales final, with a strong, true reason that shows real exposure (a damaging admission), such as handing over proprietary methods that can't be unseen; having no guarantee stands out, so lean into it rather than hedging; for high-ticket, customised work: "if you need a guarantee before starting, we're not the right fit; we want motivated self-starters"; it never removes the client's remedies if the work isn't done properly (see X11). Implied (performance pay): a share of the measurable gain, so no performance means no pay and good work is paid well; it aligns both sides, weeds out poor fits and builds long relationships; pair it with a minimum (the greater of a fixed sum or a share), or a fixed fee for the first months then pure performance; no payment without performance is the stronger version; it only works with measurable outcomes and trust or control over getting paid. Insurance: hold the relevant professional indemnity and cyber cover and state the real cover and limits accurately as an answer to the data-security fear (O28); the client stays responsible for its own regulated work (X4), and never offer to cover its penalties or errors (see X11). For estimated or time-based work, a price cap (the total won't exceed a stated figure unless the job differs from what the buyer described) removes the fear of an open-ended bill and sets the offer apart from hourly rivals.
- O43 [building or improving the offer] The 10x and one-tenth test: ask what you'd deliver if the offer cost 10 times its price, and what you'd deliver if it cost a tenth and still had to give more value than it does now. Both answers surface better components. Start with the less scalable, high-touch version (more of the owner's own time) to get the first yeses and cash, then move the same results into low-cost assets (M22, O34).
- O44: merged into O39 and O41.
- O45 [a prospect hesitates over an unconditional guarantee, or choosing guarantee types] Scripts: "I'm not asking you to say yes or no today, I'm asking you to make an informed decision." "Both decisions carry little risk, because the guarantee covers you as long as you [state the conditions]; only one helps you reach your goal: which one is more likely to get you there?" With a conditional guarantee, never call either choice risk-free: state the conditions in the same breath (O8; see X11). Extra guarantee types to consider: outsized refund (2 to 3x), credit towards other programmes, personal one-to-one until they hit it, release from the contract, revenue or profit share (paid only from the result). Stacking options: conditional plus unconditional; two conditional guarantees for a small and a big outcome; the same terms over a short and a long timeline.
- O46: merged into O13.

### Claims

- O47 [writing or checking any claim in copy, calls or pages] Keep one claims file for the offer: every claim you might use, one line each, with its primary source (document and page or link, and the date checked) and its evidence status (proven, illustration only, needs client data) (see X9). Build it by reading each source end to end yourself (the instructions box, the D5 checklists, official guidance, research reports, the L59 question log, reply and call notes, later client data), never from summaries, AI ones included, and pick the angle only once it's done. Keep the owner's own ideas in a separate section marked as such, so they're never mistaken for sourced claims. Group the claims into blocks by the avatar's top problems (O5) and the parts of the offer's mechanism, and build each piece of copy from the blocks that match its one big idea (O3); only proven claims go in as statements of fact. Before any new template, page, video script, call script or letter goes out, check every claim against the file and every figure and date against its primary source that day, mark each line wrong, missing or weak, and fix or cut it before it goes. Mark any figure that presents inaction as normal and reframe it to the majority doing the right thing, or keep it out of prospect copy; pain figures stay as "you're not alone with this problem", never "most of your peers haven't started". Then read the piece once as the buyer receiving it and cut any line that would make them feel played, even if true (M27). Claims about official rules, penalties and dates follow the dated facts in the instructions box, and never imply a rule or penalty applies before it does; environmental claims need evidence in the claims file (see X9). If you bold or italicise words in a quote, say "my emphasis" or drop it. Look in process notes and client data for one specific, surprising, checkable fact that proves the point better than any adjective. The power is in the product, not the prose: when copy is weak, go back to the claims file and the source for a stronger true claim before reworking the words. Never follow a source's own order or tone: order the claims by selling power for the one big idea (O3). When anyone points out an error, thank them, fix it the same day everywhere the claims file shows it appears, and invite them to flag the next one; if a wrong date or figure went out in a batch, send those recipients one plain correction with no pitch (see X2). In the claims check, separate platform risk (an account ban on a messaging or ad platform) from regulatory risk (fines or regulator action), and clear both before anything goes out.
