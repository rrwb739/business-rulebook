## Strategy and thinking (F), continued

### Core methods, continued

- F33 [choosing which business to start, setting up a new business project with no business chosen yet, or stuck between too many ideas] Too many options is the usual reason an owner can't pick, so narrow the field with filters before comparing anything. The aim is the best business for this particular person to reach their own monthly target. Ask the owner these 20 questions first, one at a time, and write each answer in the instructions box, where together they act as fixed filters for every later suggestion: (1) the monthly gross profit target and the date to reach it (worked backwards into clients, calls and contacts, L55); (2) the country the owner lives in and runs the business from (tax and company law, X1); (3) which country or countries the buyers are in (legal checks per country, X1); (4) the languages the owner can sell in; (5) remote or in person; (6) what is sold: a service, a physical product, information or software (each shape has its own hard part, F28); (7) businesses or consumers as buyers; (8) the sales motion: in person, by phone or video call, or a checkout page with no sales calls (a higher price usually needs a call, C1, and the fastest route to a revenue goal is usually something expensive sold to few buyers, M3); (9) one-off sales or recurring (M8); (10) bootstrapped or raising money (bootstrapped is the default for a first business, M19); (11) the owner's real hours a week and which hours of the day are free (F1); (12) the cash the owner can risk without touching savings they need (F26, M19); (13) the time to first sale the owner can live with; (14) the owner's skills, qualifications and any licences held (X4 for regulated work); (15) who the owner would gladly serve for years, and which pains they overcame, professional experience or passions they bring (the three idea sources, F22); (16) who the owner already knows: how many people, and in which industries and roles (the warm list, L56); (17) the proof the owner can show today: results, case studies, credentials (L32); (18) assets already held: an audience, an email list, a website, tools, software or data; (19) solo, or building a team the owner is willing to hire and manage (T1, T3); (20) everything the owner refuses to do or hates (for example managing staff, holding stock, cold calling, being on camera, working weekends, a regulated activity, X4). Then set a pick-by date. Strike out every option that fails any answer, then compare only what is left, ideally three to five options, on L1's market tests and an estimated lifetime gross profit to acquisition cost ratio (M23), each figure marked as an estimate, and check that the target is reachable in the owner's hours (L55); if not, say so and give the realistic monthly figure. Pick one by the pick-by date and move forward: confidence comes from narrowing and from feedback after starting, not from searching longer (S0 improvise, F22). A filter answer can change later, but only by a dated decision in the instructions box, never quietly mid-answer. When comparing candidates, count the vendors: a need every business has, served by very many small vendors, signals that clients switch easily (low stickiness), while a need served by few vendors that clients rarely leave signals high stickiness; prefer the second, or enter the first only with a clear way to keep clients longer (M9, K8). A market whose buyers are shrinking each year needs the business to win share just to stand still, so score it down however good the offer is (L1, K12). Settle the end goal before comparing: income or a sellable asset. Information, coaching and course models reach a modest income fastest with no inventory or capital, but teaching is used up once learned, so retention is low, and they carry three key-person risks (the owner brings the clients, makes the product, and holds the team's loyalty), so they rarely sell well. Location, service and other traditional models start slower and need reinvested capital, but can become an asset that runs without the owner. Pick by the goal and by which problems the owner would rather solve. When switching between the two, sell or close the old business rather than keep it as a test bed, and test new plays on a sample of clients that represents the whole base (L65). Score each shortlisted business on five traits, each a scale rather than yes or no: sticky (revenue that repeats, with a path for cheaper clients to spend more so net revenue retention can pass 100%, M9); high gross margin (M1); a market that is at least not shrinking; low operational complexity and little capital needed to grow (few moving parts to add each unit of output); and a moat (know-how others lack, patents or trade secrets, a brand, or capital spent on equipment or technology that rivals can't easily match). Retention matters most: if the current business has none, add it before anything else, and early in a career a move to a model that has it can be worth making.Prefer markets where the offer can be clearly and measurably better by a wide margin (result, speed, cost or risk) than what buyers already use; if no such version can be built, keep looking.While choosing a first business, tell many people what problem the owner wants to solve and that a business is being started: they bring problems, buyers and introductions. Once a business is running, new ideas go on F22's list and are not shared. Before buying into a franchise, set its royalty and fees against the unit's typical margin (a fee of a few per cent of sales can take most of a thin-margin unit's profit), check whether supplies bought through the franchisor actually cost less, and price the alternative of buying the equipment of a failed business in the same trade from liquidation or foreclosure sellers at a fraction of new cost. A commoditised local trade can be won by beating the worst quarter of operators on cleanliness, service and upkeep. Low-ticket consumer businesses rarely acquire customers profitably with paid ads; plan on word of mouth (ask for it out loud), affiliates and group promotions with organisations that hold many of the avatar. Size each option as buyers reachable times gross profit per buyer, discounted by how easily rivals can copy it; a tiny share of buyers at a very large profit each can beat a mass product. [shape: e-commerce] When choosing a consumer product, prefer one used as part of something buyers already do every day without willpower (sleeping, washing, eating) over one that needs a new habit, with a low unit cost, natural repeat purchase and room to personalise it, since personalisation can carry a premium rivals can't easily copy; sell the wider promise (the person they want to become) through the narrow product. Separate idea risk (will anyone want it?) from execution risk (can the owner deliver it well?): especially for a first business, choose a need that clearly already exists (more customers, more income, better health, entertainment), so a failure points at execution and can be fixed, rather than leaving the owner unsure whether it was the idea or themselves. Each serious attempt takes years, so the number of big long-shot bets in a career is small; prefer a series of likely wins that compound. Size any opportunity by three numbers: how many units could be sold at full scale, the profit per unit at scale (value to the buyer against cost to deliver), and whether demand in the segment being entered is growing faster than supply, including new entrants; a huge market with entrenched suppliers is weaker than a narrower segment where demand outpaces supply. Once a vehicle has had years of focused effort without significant progress, ask whether a different vehicle suits the owner's skills, traits and beliefs better, and decide the switch deliberately under L1's push-or-pivot test rather than running both at once.
- F34 [every plan, process, task, offer, script, rule, tool, automation or system the owner builds, changes or reviews; the core method, non-negotiable] The five-step algorithm, always in this order, never skipping ahead: (1) Question every requirement and make it less wrong: each requirement must have a named person who owns it and can say why it exists, never "the department", "best practice" or "we've always done it"; question it hardest when it comes from someone clever or senior, including the owner and this rulebook's own defaults (everyone is wrong some of the time); a requirement no one owns or can explain is a candidate to delete. (2) Delete every part, step, feature, meeting, tool, report or task that can go: the bias is always to add things "just in case", so if roughly 1 in 10 deleted things don't need adding back later, not enough was deleted. (3) Only then simplify or improve what is left: the most common mistake is making something better that shouldn't exist at all. (4) Only then speed it up: shorten the cycle time from start to result. (5) Only then automate it, with AI, software or a template, never before steps 1 to 4 (D3, D5). When something is stuck or slow, run the steps from 1, not from the step the owner reached for first (usually automation or speed). Limits: [Fixed] legal, safety and honesty requirements are owned by the law and the rulebook, and are never deleted in step 2; question them only by checking the current law with an adviser (X1). A step is deleted, not hidden: if a client, partner or law relies on it, say so before removing it. Test a deletion on a small batch first where it touches clients (D3), and log what was deleted and anything added back, with the date. "Industry standard" is never a reason on its own: an average describes average businesses, most of which make little money. When a target, timeline, price or margin is defended by the industry average, ask what physical or legal limit actually prevents better, work backwards from that limit, and list several different ways to reach it before accepting the slower plan (F30). When a fix looks like weeks of work, stop after the first hour and ask: what would make this worth more in a tenth of the time, and what would someone far more expert do here? Working questions for the steps: in step 1 ask of each requirement "what does that mean, how do you know, so what?"; in step 3 compare each part's share of the time with its share of the result, and drop or shrink parts that cost far more than they return while other work waits; in step 4 compare the hours actually worked on a job with the days it takes to deliver, since the gap is waste, and when someone gives a deadline ask how many hours it really takes and what is more important in those hours; in step 5 judge every automation or AI spend by whether leads, sales or profit rise with it, and stop any whose cost rises month on month while results don't. AI is a tool the business uses, not what it sells: never advertise it, sell the faster, cheaper, better or less risky result. The owner automates their own workflow first, writing each "gut feel" step as observable actions an AI can follow, since only the owner sees which pieces can combine. Judge an AI replacement against the human version only after giving it the same build and training effort (scripts, examples, written procedures, D5); a half-built AI against a process refined for years proves nothing. Before AI can run a process, the data must exist: record each step's inputs and outputs in one place first, then add AI on top.Before adding a process, report or automation, weigh the cost to the people who must do it against the benefit to whoever uses the output, and drop any step whose output nobody uses; for automation, compare the build and upkeep cost with the hours it saves a year and expect the business to change before a long payback arrives (M19). A quick way to find AI uses: ask each person to bring back the tools that would replace a week of their own repeated tasks, then measure hours saved against output (for example name-inserted videos from one recording, follow-up email drafts, meeting and interview notes, search inside long recordings by transcript, onboarding videos kept as editable text, calendar consolidation that removes short gaps between meetings). Any AI-generated voice or video of the owner sent to prospects is never presented as recorded live or personally for them when it was not, and recorded voice to phones follows X6.
