## Strategy and thinking (F), continued

### Core methods, continued

- F27 [someone criticises the owner in public, or a competitor talks the business down] [Fixed] Never argue point by point. When a former client or employee copies the business's material or courts its clients, don't change the model in reaction or name them: give clients more, and use contracts and IP protection for material actually taken (X20, X18). Two options: grow so big no one hears them, or answer with kindness ("You're right, I'm far from perfect. Congrats on what you've built."). Claim the owner's flaws first (new, small, in business to make money; O11). Turn vague criticism into something usable before weighing it: ask what exactly fell short of what was expected, how the person knows (the evidence or number), and why it matters to the avatar or the result; a remark that cannot answer these is an insult rather than criticism and gets no change. Most criticism is a different preference, not a fact; build the tallest building instead of knocking others down. One client's criticism is a preference; the same point from 3 or more independent clients is a fact: log it (C14 objection log, M9 cancellation log) and fix it (L75). Never complain to prospects, clients or in public about regulators, suppliers, competitors, clients or workload: say the fact and the next step. When a message or slip annoys the owner, write the reply but do not send it: a neutral acknowledgement the same day if needed, then the full reply only after a night's sleep, reread from their side and sent calm and factual, or not at all. Before sending, answer: could they be right, even partly; will this fix the problem or only vent; will it bring them closer or push them away; what would winning cost? Count complaints settled without escalation (L75). Never pay or pressure anyone to remove, bury or suppress genuine reviews or posts, and never post or commission counter-reviews; ask a platform to remove a post only if it is fake or defamatory (see X12). If a rival uses fake reviews, actor testimonials or false scarcity, report it privately with evidence to the regulator or the platform (see X12), never call it out in public or to prospects (O2). For a public hit piece, do not acknowledge it to the owner's audience: put the time into client results and proof.Default to ignoring: an attack that gets no reply fades, while a reply hands the attacker the owner's attention and audience. Where a reply helps, agree with what is true, or go one further with true, light self-mockery, so there is nothing left to push against; never claim a fault that isn't true (X9). Reply in public only to someone with a bigger audience, and then with humour, never anger. Never repeat a denial again and again: saying "I am not X" keeps pairing the owner with X (O2); answer by putting out more of the work buyers value. A post that is false and damaging is handled through the platform or legal advice (X12, X1), never a public fight. Competitors: decide from customers' data, never from what a competitor did; a decision made to beat or punish a rival is the costliest kind. Being copied means the business is ahead; copiers watch the business instead of their customers. Most markets are far larger than any two rivals' share, so compete on being better for the avatar rather than on the rival.
- F33 [choosing which business to start, setting up a new business project with no business chosen yet, or stuck between too many ideas] Too many options is the usual reason an owner can't pick, so narrow the field with filters before comparing anything. The aim is the best business for this particular person to reach their own monthly target. Ask the owner these 20 questions first, one at a time, and write each answer in the instructions box, where together they act as fixed filters for every later suggestion: (1) the monthly gross profit target and the date to reach it (worked backwards into clients, calls and contacts, L55); (2) the country the owner lives in and runs the business from (tax and company law, X1); (3) which country or countries the buyers are in (legal checks per country, X1); (4) the languages the owner can sell in; (5) remote or in person; (6) what is sold: a service, a physical product, information or software (each shape has its own hard part, F28); (7) businesses or consumers as buyers; (8) the sales motion: in person, by phone or video call, or a checkout page with no sales calls (a higher price usually needs a call, C1, and the fastest route to a revenue goal is usually something expensive sold to few buyers, M3); (9) one-off sales or recurring (M8); (10) bootstrapped or raising money (bootstrapped is the default for a first business, M19); (11) the owner's real hours a week and which hours of the day are free (F1); (12) the cash the owner can risk without touching savings they need (F26, M19); (13) the time to first sale the owner can live with; (14) the owner's skills, qualifications and any licences held (X4 for regulated work); (15) who the owner would gladly serve for years, and which pains they overcame, professional experience or passions they bring (the three idea sources, F22); (16) who the owner already knows: how many people, and in which industries and roles (the warm list, L56); (17) the proof the owner can show today: results, case studies, credentials (L32); (18) assets already held: an audience, an email list, a website, tools, software or data; (19) solo, or building a team the owner is willing to hire and manage (T1, T3); (20) everything the owner refuses to do or hates (for example managing staff, holding stock, cold calling, being on camera, working weekends, a regulated activity, X4). Then set a pick-by date. Strike out every option that fails any answer, then compare only what is left, ideally three to five options, on L1's market tests and an estimated lifetime gross profit to acquisition cost ratio (M23), each figure marked as an estimate, and check that the target is reachable in the owner's hours (L55); if not, say so and give the realistic monthly figure. Pick one by the pick-by date and move forward: confidence comes from narrowing and from feedback after starting, not from searching longer (S0 improvise, F22). A filter answer can change later, but only by a dated decision in the instructions box, never quietly mid-answer. When comparing candidates, count the vendors: a need every business has, served by very many small vendors, signals that clients switch easily (low stickiness), while a need served by few vendors that clients rarely leave signals high stickiness; prefer the second, or enter the first only with a clear way to keep clients longer (M9, K8). A market whose buyers are shrinking each year needs the business to win share just to stand still, so score it down however good the offer is (L1, K12). Settle the end goal before comparing: income or a sellable asset. Information, coaching and course models reach a modest income fastest with no inventory or capital, but teaching is used up once learned, so retention is low, and they carry three key-person risks (the owner brings the clients, makes the product, and holds the team's loyalty), so they rarely sell well. Location, service and other traditional models start slower and need reinvested capital, but can become an asset that runs without the owner. Pick by the goal and by which problems the owner would rather solve. When switching between the two, sell or close the old business rather than keep it as a test bed, and test new plays on a sample of clients that represents the whole base (L65). Score each shortlisted business on five traits, each a scale rather than yes or no: sticky (revenue that repeats, with a path for cheaper clients to spend more so net revenue retention can pass 100%, M9); high gross margin (M1); a market that is at least not shrinking; low operational complexity and little capital needed to grow (few moving parts to add each unit of output); and a moat (know-how others lack, patents or trade secrets, a brand, or capital spent on equipment or technology that rivals can't easily match). Retention matters most: if the current business has none, add it before anything else, and early in a career a move to a model that has it can be worth making.Prefer markets where the offer can be clearly and measurably better by a wide margin (result, speed, cost or risk) than what buyers already use; if no such version can be built, keep looking.While choosing a first business, tell many people what problem the owner wants to solve and that a business is being started: they bring problems, buyers and introductions. Once a business is running, new ideas go on F22's list and are not shared. Before buying into a franchise, set its royalty and fees against the unit's typical margin (a fee of a few per cent of sales can take most of a thin-margin unit's profit), check whether supplies bought through the franchisor actually cost less, and price the alternative of buying the equipment of a failed business in the same trade from liquidation or foreclosure sellers at a fraction of new cost. A commoditised local trade can be won by beating the worst quarter of operators on cleanliness, service and upkeep. Low-ticket consumer businesses rarely acquire customers profitably with paid ads; plan on word of mouth (ask for it out loud), affiliates and group promotions with organisations that hold many of the avatar. Size each option as buyers reachable times gross profit per buyer, discounted by how easily rivals can copy it; a tiny share of buyers at a very large profit each can beat a mass product. [shape: e-commerce] When choosing a consumer product, prefer one used as part of something buyers already do every day without willpower (sleeping, washing, eating) over one that needs a new habit, with a low unit cost, natural repeat purchase and room to personalise it, since personalisation can carry a premium rivals can't easily copy; sell the wider promise (the person they want to become) through the narrow product. Separate idea risk (will anyone want it?) from execution risk (can the owner deliver it well?): especially for a first business, choose a need that clearly already exists (more customers, more income, better health, entertainment), so a failure points at execution and can be fixed, rather than leaving the owner unsure whether it was the idea or themselves. A third kind is technical risk: a need everyone knows about that is hard to meet, where buyers doubt it can be done rather than whether they want it; it and new-category ideas can pay most but need proof the thing works before anyone buys, while execution risk is the surest money. Each serious attempt takes years, so the number of big long-shot bets in a career is small; prefer a series of likely wins that compound. Size any opportunity by three numbers: how many units could be sold at full scale, the profit per unit at scale (value to the buyer against cost to deliver), and whether demand in the segment being entered is growing faster than supply, including new entrants; a huge market with entrenched suppliers is weaker than a narrower segment where demand outpaces supply. Once a vehicle has had years of focused effort without significant progress, ask whether a different vehicle suits the owner's skills, traits and beliefs better, and decide the switch deliberately under L1's push-or-pivot test rather than running both at once. Most service businesses get harder as they grow, so build in at least one advantage that strengthens with size, and do one fully rather than several halfway: a network where each new client makes it better for the rest (a buying group), exclusive supply or data, a licence or permit others struggle to get, lower unit costs from scale, owning more of the supply chain, a trusted brand, tiers priced to each segment with a real difference in what each gets, exclusive supply, customer or distribution contracts, a high capital barrier, protected IP, buying up rivals in a fragmented local market (within competition law), a free core offer paid for by a second product, or owned distribution to one area or segment.
- F34 [every answer (instructions.md step 2b), and every plan, process, task, offer, script, rule, tool, automation or system the owner builds, changes or reviews; the core method, non-negotiable] The five-step algorithm, always in this order, never skipping ahead: (1) Question every requirement and make it less wrong: each requirement must have a named person who owns it and can say why it exists, never "the department", "best practice" or "we've always done it"; question it hardest when it comes from someone clever or senior, including the owner and this rulebook's own defaults (everyone is wrong some of the time); a requirement no one owns or can explain is a candidate to delete. (2) Delete every part, step, feature, meeting, tool, report or task that can go: the bias is always to add things "just in case", so if roughly 1 in 10 deleted things don't need adding back later, not enough was deleted. (3) Only then simplify or improve what is left: the most common mistake is making something better that shouldn't exist at all. (4) Only then speed it up: shorten the cycle time from start to result. (5) Only then automate it, with AI, software or a template, never before steps 1 to 4 (D3, D5). When something is stuck or slow, run the steps from 1, not from the step the owner reached for first (usually automation or speed). Limits: [Fixed] legal, safety and honesty requirements are owned by the law and the rulebook, and are never deleted in step 2; question them only by checking the current law with an adviser (X1). A step is deleted, not hidden: if a client, partner or law relies on it, say so before removing it. Test a deletion on a small batch first where it touches clients (D3), and log what was deleted and anything added back, with the date. "Industry standard" is never a reason on its own: an average describes average businesses, most of which make little money. When a target, timeline, price or margin is defended by the industry average, ask what physical or legal limit actually prevents better, work backwards from that limit, and list several different ways to reach it before accepting the slower plan (F30). When a fix looks like weeks of work, stop after the first hour and ask: what would make this worth more in a tenth of the time, and what would someone far more expert do here? Working questions for the steps: in step 1 ask of each requirement "what does that mean, how do you know, so what?"; in step 3 compare each part's share of the time with its share of the result, and drop or shrink parts that cost far more than they return while other work waits; in step 4 compare the hours actually worked on a job with the days it takes to deliver, since the gap is waste, and when someone gives a deadline ask how many hours it really takes and what is more important in those hours; in step 5 judge every automation or AI spend by whether leads, sales or profit rise with it, and stop any whose cost rises month on month while results don't. AI is a tool the business uses, not what it sells: never advertise it, sell the faster, cheaper, better or less risky result. The owner automates their own workflow first, writing each "gut feel" step as observable actions an AI can follow, since only the owner sees which pieces can combine. Judge an AI replacement against the human version only after giving it the same build and training effort (scripts, examples, written procedures, D5); a half-built AI against a process refined for years proves nothing. Before AI can run a process, the data must exist: record each step's inputs and outputs in one place first, then add AI on top. When using AI to analyse the business's numbers, give it the specific business questions first (what a client costs to acquire and how that has changed, which funnel step is leaking, what changed after a channel or supplier change) rather than asking for a summary or a dashboard, and check its figures against the source before acting; keep confidential data within D17's limits. Before adding a process, report or automation, weigh the cost to the people who must do it against the benefit to whoever uses the output, and drop any step whose output nobody uses; for automation, compare the build and upkeep cost with the hours it saves a year and expect the business to change before a long payback arrives (M19). A quick way to find AI uses: ask each person to bring back the tools that would replace a week of their own repeated tasks, then measure hours saved against output (for example name-inserted videos from one recording, follow-up email drafts, meeting and interview notes, search inside long recordings by transcript, onboarding videos kept as editable text, calendar consolidation that removes short gaps between meetings). Any AI-generated voice or video of the owner sent to prospects is never presented as recorded live or personally for them when it was not, and recorded voice to phones follows X6. To find waste, compare the hours of actual work on a job with the time from order to delivery: every day beyond the working hours is waiting to question and cut. To speed a team or supplier up, shorten the update cycle (end of day instead of end of week) and, when someone gives a date, ask how many hours of work it really takes and what is more important in the meantime. For AI tools, check monthly that spend on them is matched by more leads, sales or saved paid hours; rising tool costs with flat results means something was automated that should have been deleted.
- F35 [a setback, bad news or something upsetting knocks the owner off the plan] Treat steadiness under setbacks as four skills that can be practised, each judged by behaviour rather than feelings: how much has to go wrong before the owner acts differently, how far their actions change once it does, how long they take to return to normal, and whether the new normal is better or worse than before. Feelings are allowed; acting them out is the problem. When the owner notices they have started acting differently (snapping, skipping the day's outreach, avoiding calls), they do the next thing they would have done if nothing had happened, and reverse course straight away rather than letting one bad decision lead to the next. Once steady again, ask what the setback can teach or change for the better, and turn the answer into one action. Sleep and food raise every one of the four, so protect them during hard weeks. This rule is for business setbacks; for grief, or anything that feels heavier, the owner leans on the people and professional support around them.
- F24 [starting any new skill: sales calls, emails, delivery] Write down the number of reps after which it would be unreasonable to still be bad, and commit to it. After every 10 to 20, review the best 10 to 20% and change one thing. When something fails, find the step that failed, never "it doesn't work". Doing beats reading. Drill a corrected line several times in a row, work on one skill for a week or two, and study runs of wins as closely as losses (C13). After every win, write one line on why it happened. After a big win, check the next 2 weeks of the L41 scoreboard: if inputs fell, the win is costing the owner. Only real outreach, real calls and role-plays in call conditions (C13) count as sales reps; employer work, reading and rulebook work do not. Log focused drilling hours alongside reps: aim for about 20 focused hours of script drilling and role-play before the first live pilot sales call, and give any new tool or skill about 20 focused hours over 2 to 3 weeks, starting within 48 hours of deciding, before calling it too hard. Keep the first recorded call and first email batch; at call 20 and 100, and email 300, compare them side by side and write what changed, and use that rather than comparing with others when discouraged (L71). When an input falls short, raise the bid on the same method (more volume, more follow-ups, a better asset) and write the next bid down before calling it failed, within F1 hours and L43's stop rules. Keep a running lessons log, written the day each lesson happens, losses as well as wins. In each review, a trait counts as a factor only if all the top reps share it and the rest mostly lack it; drop the rest as superstition. Knowing about a method (videos, books) is not knowing how to do it: count only real reps, and expect the first 100 real attempts to teach more than any amount of study. Learning is a new behaviour in the same situation, and speed of learning is iterations per day: pack the reps close together rather than spreading them over weeks. Break a skill into sub-skills until each can be described as observable actions (what to say, do with the body and voice), and write a measure of success for each. Copy what the best observably do, not their explanation of why it works (they often don't know); skip the "why" and change the inputs. Then compare the top tenth of the owner's own reps against the rest, adopt one or two differences, repeat, and add a layer each round; hire an expert to start further along, then out-iterate them.In the lessons log, write what the owner expected before acting and what actually happened, and for every win note the outside conditions that helped (cheap ads at the time, a quiet market, one big client) as well as what the owner did, so a win is not wrongly credited to skill; reread the matching entries before starting similar work, and reread the entries from a winning period when results slip.
- F25 [learning the market or a skill fast] Consultant method: ask one expert for the 5 best people they know, ask each of those the same, interview them all, sort notes by category, and distil a few core truths. Interviews with the avatar must be genuine: say up front that the owner is building an offer for them and that a sales conversation may follow, and never disguise a pitch as an advice or research request (see X9; X2, M27, O11); those who then show interest are engaged leads. In those interviews, ask how they built what they run and let them tell it; ask what they do all day, what is easy, what is hard, what causes stress, what their bosses or partners want and how they measure success; ask what they have struggled with, for how long, what it is like when it goes wrong and what the ideal would look like, and, about past solutions, what they had to start doing that they disliked and what they had to give up that they enjoyed (O28); log the answers in the O5 phrase bank, and record only with consent and no client personal data (X5). When an avatar member or peer has been cool, ask one small, genuine favour of their expertise, saying plainly if a sales conversation may follow, never an invented favour. Paying for access to people who have done it compresses years of mistakes: not knowing a skill is ignorance debt, a yearly bill equal to the gap between what the owner earns now and would earn with it (M19). Buying a skill means paying someone who already gets the result to watch the owner do the activity and give feedback until the owner gets it without them; buying only gives permission to start. Compare any spend on a skill with what an index fund would return. Skills compound: choose the next skill partly by how much it multiplies the ones the owner has. Investing in the owner's own skills is the lowest-risk spend: put as much as affordable, within M19's cash buffer, into the one skill most limiting growth now. Writing teaches a market: write the whole method up as the DIY version during the pilots. Run a course exactly as taught once, then keep what works; ask its success stories what they did beyond the teaching. Before paying for any course, coach or tool, write the result and the date, and commit to being its best success story; if it fails, first list what was not applied. Before paying a coach or a room, ask what they got wrong at the owner's stage and how they fixed it, and prefer one who can name specific failures (F28). Label each learning spend "what exists" (rooms, people far ahead) when the options are unknown, or "how to" (an expert watching the owner, T2) when known. Learn just enough of a skill to get it working, and deepen it only when the next stage needs it (L6). Pay down ignorance with the cheapest currency first (interviews, communities where the owner gives first, employer-funded training, favours), then money from a fixed monthly education pot within M19's buffer, spent only on the one skill most limiting the first target. In any chance conversation with a member of the avatar, ask about the problem the offer solves, thank them and log their phrases (L59); no pitch unless they ask, and say a sale may follow if it might. With no skill and no money, the fastest route is to work for or alongside the best operator the owner can reach, for long enough to copy how they work: who the owner learns from matters more than the industry. List up to 20 people the owner admires in the community, rank them, and approach the top ones offering one free, proven piece of value first (F15), never a bare request. Read by current constraint: on the topic that limits the owner now, read the best-reviewed books in turn rather than whatever is recommended, then move to the next topic. Skills build on each other: before buying a programme, check it starts where the owner is and ask what it assumes they already know; when one fails, look first for the missing earlier skill before judging the teacher. In any paid room or network, give the owner's strongest skill to others in full, as carefully as for a paying client (a whole script rewrite, not one tip), with no ask attached; the goodwill later buys help in the skills the owner lacks, often by barter.Go into every programme aiming to be its best student, and run it exactly as taught before judging it (F24): the result depends more on how it is used than on who teaches it. Don't credit the latest teacher alone, since earlier ones laid the base it built on; and even a bad programme pays if what went wrong is logged as what not to do (F24). Weigh advice by the adviser's evidence, highest first: someone who has taken several people like the owner to exactly where the owner wants to go; someone who has taken one such person there; someone who has taken others there; someone who has been there themselves (a strong performer is not always a good teacher); someone who knows a person who got there; people who know the owner but don't have what the owner wants; strangers' comments. Act on the higher levels and treat the lower ones as data at most. Match paid learning to the problem: a course or coaching programme for a specific tactical skill (a channel, a script, a method), since it buys speed and accountability; a peer group or one-to-one mentor someone well ahead when stuck for a long time, since that is usually a strategic or belief problem. Before paying, try free routes first: searching the exact question, and asking someone slightly ahead in exchange for something useful. For someone with no business yet and no income to fall back on, a paid job inside a small business (about ten staff or fewer) in the trade they want to enter is a fast way to learn every function while being paid: take the pay the market offers for a learner, commit to a set period (for example two years), ask colleagues to show how their part works, and learn how that business wins customers first, since that is the skill that carries into any later business of their own (F26).
