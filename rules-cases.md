## Case patterns (K)

How to use this section. Each K entry is a strategy that worked for one kind of business, written in general words so any business can borrow it. A K entry is an analogy, never proof: it says what was done, why it worked and when it fits, and it points to the rules that carry the method. Before using one, check the business matches the entry's "Fits when" line, mark the idea "Proposed, not decided", run it through F34 (the five steps) and the stage gate (F32), and name the number that will show whether it works here. If a K entry and a numbered rule in another section disagree, the other section's rule wins, and [Fixed] rules always win.

Format of every entry: [trigger] Situation. The move. Why it worked. Fits when. Does not fit when. Linked rules.

- K1 [a local service business with walk-in or inbound traffic but low conversion of enquiries into paying members or clients] Situation: enquiries arrive but the front desk or first contact treats them as admin, so most leave without buying. The move: turn the first contact into a sales step with a script, run short fixed-length challenge programmes (a few weeks, a clear before and after) as the entry offer, and sell the ongoing membership at the end of the challenge while the result is visible. Why it worked: the short programme lowers the risk of saying yes, gives a fast visible result, and creates a natural moment to offer the next step. Fits when: the service can show a result within weeks and clients come back repeatedly. Does not fit when: results take months to show, or the service is bought once. Linked rules: O1, M28, M8, M31, C3, C5, O26.

- K2 [an owner who has made one business work and is asked by peers how they did it] Situation: other owners in the same trade want the results but cannot copy them alone. The move: stop only doing the work and start licensing or teaching the system that produced the results (scripts, playbooks, training, software), sold to owners of the same kind of business. Why it worked: the system was already proven in one business, the buyers share the same problem, and selling the system scales without the owner's delivery hours. Fits when: the owner's own results are measured and repeatable, and the buyers can run the system without the owner. Does not fit when: the results depended on the owner's personal skill or a local advantage, or there is no measured proof yet (X1: every claim must be provable). Linked rules: F30, F13, D11, M2, X1.

- K3 [a fragmented local market of many small operators with no clear leader, and an owner with capital or financing] Situation: many small firms do the same work, each with weak systems and no brand. The move: buy or merge several operators in one area, put them on one set of systems, one brand and one back office, then add more. Why it worked: shared systems, buying power and marketing cost spread across more revenue raise margins, and a larger firm is valued at a higher multiple than the small ones were bought at. Fits when: the owner can run systems and finance purchases, and the operators are cheap relative to their profit. Does not fit when: the owner is at an early stage with no cash or systems (F32), or buying rivals would breach competition law (X20). Linked rules: F30, M19, X20.

- K4 [many small buyers in the same trade each paying full price for the same supplies or services] Situation: no single small buyer has the volume to negotiate. The move: form or run a buying group that pools the members' purchasing, negotiates volume terms, and charges the members a fee or keeps a share of the saving. Why it worked: the group's combined volume wins terms no member could alone, so joining saves members money at once, which makes the offer easy to say yes to. Fits when: the members buy the same things regularly and the saving can be shown in their own figures. Does not fit when: the purchases are too varied to pool, or the arrangement would fix prices or share markets between competitors (X20). Linked rules: O1, M23, M8, X20.

- K5 [a business whose core product can be given away cheaply or free, with a paid service that pairs naturally with it] Situation: buyers resist paying for the core product, and competitors charge for it. The move: give the core product free or at cost, and earn on a paired paid service that most users need once they use the core (setup, support, a done-for-you version, an add-on). Why it worked: free removes the first objection and widens the top of the funnel; the paired service is bought by users already getting value. Fits when: the cost of serving a free user is low and a large enough share of users need the paid service. Does not fit when: free users cost real money to serve, or only a few would ever buy the paired service, so cash runs out (M23). Linked rules: M29, L46, O14, M31, M23, M26, X3.

- K6 [a low-price job sold door to door or in person to consumers, with the same customers needing it again] Situation: each job is cheap, so the cost of winning each customer eats the profit. The move: sell the first job at the door, then bundle repeat jobs into a membership paid monthly, so one win becomes recurring revenue. Why it worked: the membership turns a one-off low-ticket sale into lifetime value high enough to pay for door-to-door selling. Fits when: the job recurs on a schedule and customers are happy to be billed regularly. Does not fit when: the job is genuinely one-off, or the business cannot meet off-premises consumer contract rules (X22). Linked rules: M8, M9, M23, M42, X3, X22.

- K7 [a business selling a low-price product automatically online, with a share of buyers able to afford a much bigger offer] Situation: the automated funnel makes small sales but leaves the bigger buyers unserved. The move: add a call team that contacts recent automated buyers, qualifies them and offers the higher-priced next step (ascension). Why it worked: buyers who have already paid once are warm, trust is partly built, and the call team only spends time on people who already said yes once. Fits when: the bigger offer is worth enough to pay for the calls, and calls are lawful on the channel used. Does not fit when: there is no higher offer worth calling about, or the calls would breach calling and marketing rules in the buyers' country (X1). Linked rules: M31, M16, M33, C3, C5, C14, X1, X18.

- K8 [an agency or service business in a market where every business needs the service, but clients churn, prices are low and delivery is labour-heavy] Situation: demand needs no testing (every business wants what the service gives, for example more customers), yet the market is crowded with small vendors. A need that every business has, served by very many vendors, signals low stickiness; a need served by only a few vendors that clients rarely leave signals high stickiness. The causes of low stickiness here are clients who cannot carry out their side, prices set too low for the work, and delivery that is hard to run consistently. The move: rebuild delivery so AI does most of the repeatable work under a named human check, price consistently for the result, and use the lower delivery cost to make the low and middle price points profitable and the results steady enough to keep clients longer. Why it worked: the demand was already proven, so the only open problems were pricing and operations, and AI reduces both the cost and the variation of delivery. While good AI delivery is still hard to set up, that difficulty keeps most rivals out; the advantage shrinks as it becomes easy for everyone (M10). Fits when: the service's output can be checked against a clear standard, AI matches that standard after setup and testing, and retention can be measured by batch. Does not fit when: AI is used out of the box without testing against the human standard, results cannot be made consistent, or the service is regulated so that a human must do the work. Linked rules: F34, O28, D3, D4, D5, M1, M9, M10, L1, X1.

- K9 [a professional service (accounting, legal, IT, consulting) sold as a generalist, competing on price with many similar firms] Situation: the firm can serve anyone, so nobody sees why to pay it more. The move: pick one client group that passes the market tests (growing, can afford it, easy to find, in pain), list what those business owners want and struggle with beyond the firm's own service (more customers, profit, staff, a sellable business), and publish free, practical content on those topics drawn from what the firm sees across its clients, so the firm's name becomes paired with the clients' own goals; the core service is then sold as the way to get them. Why it worked: content that solves the avatar's wider business problems keeps small promises the buyer can check, which makes them trust the big promise, and one client group means the same work repeats, so margins rise and the price can go up. Fits when: the firm has real, checkable insight across several clients of the same kind, shared without client names or data. Does not fit when: the insight would break client confidentiality or professional rules (X4, X5, X8), or the chosen group fails the market tests. Linked rules: L1, L2, O2, O3, O19, L60, L65, M2, M13, X4.

- K10 [a business with an audience that has seen it keep its promises many times, launching a new product] Situation: the owner has delivered more than buyers paid for, again and again, to one audience. The move: launch the new product first to that existing audience (a live session or recording), take orders in the order they arrive, and let those who missed the session join the queue. Why it worked: buyers who have only had kept promises from the owner trust the next one before they see the details, so the launch sells without new proof or persuasion. Fits when: the record of kept promises is real and the new product truly helps the same audience. Does not fit when: the audience is new or has had promises broken, or the queue or scarcity is invented (X10), or buyers would pay without knowing what they buy (C1 read-back: every buyer still gets the full terms before paying, X3). Linked rules: L65, O37, M31, X3, X10, C1.

- K11 [a well-known brand pairing itself with a person, cause or campaign its core buyers dislike] Situation: a mass consumer brand ran a campaign with a figure whose associations a large part of its core buyers disliked; the campaign was seen widely, so as advertising it worked, but as branding it failed with its own buyers, the brand backed away quickly, and sales fell. The move to learn from (by avoiding it): test every pairing against what the core avatar likes, and count who it gains and who it loses before running it. Why it failed: the people it gained were fewer than the core buyers it lost, and backing away then lost the new people too. Fits when: any business choosing partners, endorsers, sponsorships or public positions. Does not fit when: the business's own avatar likes the association, in which case the same pairing can be good branding for it. Linked rules: O2, X14, X23.

- K12 [selling a good new product to an industry whose buyers are shrinking every year] Situation: a supplier offered a shrinking industry a sensible add-on that let its businesses earn from the newer, growing channel with no extra work. The offer was good, but the industry lost a large share of its businesses each year, so the supplier had to win share every year just to stay the same size, its buyers had less and less money, and good customers kept closing. The lesson (to avoid it): a good offer cannot outrun a shrinking market; serve the growing side of the change directly, or pick a market with more demand than supply. Fits when: choosing or re-scoring a market (L1), or when sales stall while the offer tests well. Does not fit when: the shrinking market still has a large, stable core of buyers with money and the business only needs a small share of it (check the clients needed against the base, L1). Linked rules: L1, F33, M10, L3.

- K13 [a service whose result is a percentage gain for the client (conversion, cost saving, recovered revenue, speed), sold to small clients for small fees] Situation: the same work lifts a small client's revenue a little and a large client's revenue a lot, but the owner prices for the small client. The move: sell the same work to clients many times larger, where the same percentage gain is worth many times more, and price on that value. Before deciding the business is bad at selling, check in order: is the work good (measured results, D8), then who is it sold to; a buyer too small to feel the gain will not pay for it. Why it worked: the value comes from the client's size, not from extra effort, so the price can rise with no more work. Fits when: the result is measurable in the client's own numbers and the owner can reach and serve larger clients with proof (L32). Does not fit when: there is no proof yet at any size, or larger clients need security, compliance or capacity the business cannot meet (D11, X5). Linked rules: M2, L1, L2, O3, M3, D8, L32.

- K14 [a creative or expert service where buyers value the idea far more than the making of it] Situation: a creative firm sold finished design work at good but capped fees; when it once gave away an idea, the buyer said they would have paid a lot for it. The move: sell the concept, strategy or diagnosis as the main paid product, and price the execution separately or hand it to cheaper producers with a clear brief. Why it worked: the buyer's pain sits in not knowing what to do, while execution is easy to buy elsewhere, so the idea carries the price. Fits when: the owner's ideas produce results buyers can see and execution can be specified in a brief. Does not fit when: the buyer's real problem is execution capacity, or the idea cannot be judged before the work is done (then sell a paid diagnosis first, M2, C5). Linked rules: M2, O2, O34, D5, M31.
