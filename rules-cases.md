## Case patterns (K)

How to use this section. A pattern that recurs across businesses lives in the general sections (F to X), never only here; a K entry keeps the specific situation, the story of how one kind of business used the pattern, and its Linked rules name where the general pattern is held. Each K entry is a strategy that worked for one kind of business, written in general words so any business can borrow it. A K entry is an analogy, never proof: it says what was done, why it worked and when it fits, and it points to the rules that carry the method. Before using one, check the business matches the entry's "Fits when" line, mark the idea "Proposed, not decided", run it through F34 (the five steps) and the stage gate (F32), and name the number that will show whether it works here. If a K entry and a numbered rule in another section disagree, the other section's rule wins, and [Fixed] rules always win.

Format of every entry: [trigger] Situation. The move. Why it worked. Fits when. Does not fit when. Linked rules.

- K1 [a local service business with walk-in or inbound traffic but low conversion of enquiries into paying members or clients] Situation: enquiries arrive but the front desk or first contact treats them as admin, so most leave without buying. The move: turn the first contact into a sales step with a script, run short fixed-length challenge programmes (a few weeks, a clear before and after) as the entry offer, and sell the ongoing membership at the end of the challenge while the result is visible. Why it worked: the short programme lowers the risk of saying yes, gives a fast visible result, and creates a natural moment to offer the next step. Fits when: the service can show a result within weeks and clients come back repeatedly. Does not fit when: results take months to show, or the service is bought once. Linked rules: O1, M28, M8, M31, C3, C5, O26.

- K2 [an owner who has made one business work and is asked by peers how they did it] Situation: other owners in the same trade want the results but cannot copy them alone. The move: stop only doing the work and start licensing or teaching the system that produced the results (scripts, playbooks, training, software), sold to owners of the same kind of business. Why it worked: the system was already proven in one business, the buyers share the same problem, and selling the system scales without the owner's delivery hours. Fits when: the owner's own results are measured and repeatable, and the buyers can run the system without the owner. Does not fit when: the results depended on the owner's personal skill or a local advantage, or there is no measured proof yet (X1: every claim must be provable). Linked rules: F30, F13, D11, M2, X1.

- K3 [a fragmented local market of many small operators with no clear leader, and an owner with capital or financing] Situation: many small firms do the same work, each with weak systems and no brand. The move: buy or merge several operators in one area, put them on one set of systems, one brand and one back office, then add more. Why it worked: shared systems, buying power and marketing cost spread across more revenue raise margins, and a larger firm is valued at a higher multiple than the small ones were bought at. Fits when: the owner can run systems and finance purchases, and the operators are cheap relative to their profit. Does not fit when: the owner is at an early stage with no cash or systems (F32), or buying rivals would breach competition law (X20). Linked rules: F30, M19, X20.

- K4 [many small buyers in the same trade each paying full price for the same supplies or services] Situation: no single small buyer has the volume to negotiate. The move: form or run a buying group that pools the members' purchasing, negotiates volume terms, and charges the members a fee or keeps a share of the saving. Why it worked: the group's combined volume wins terms no member could alone, so joining saves members money at once, which makes the offer easy to say yes to. Fits when: the members buy the same things regularly and the saving can be shown in their own figures. Does not fit when: the purchases are too varied to pool, or the arrangement would fix prices or share markets between competitors (X20). Linked rules: O1, M23, M8, X20.

- K5 [a business whose core product can be given away cheaply or free, with a paid service that pairs naturally with it] Situation: buyers resist paying for the core product, and competitors charge for it. The move: give the core product free or at cost, and earn on a paired paid service that most users need once they use the core (setup, support, a done-for-you version, an add-on). Why it worked: free removes the first objection and widens the top of the funnel; the paired service is bought by users already getting value. Fits when: the cost of serving a free user is low and a large enough share of users need the paid service. Does not fit when: free users cost real money to serve, or only a few would ever buy the paired service, so cash runs out (M23). Linked rules: M29, L46, O14, M31, M23, M26, X3.

- K6 [a low-price job sold door to door or in person to consumers, with the same customers needing it again] Situation: each job is cheap, so the cost of winning each customer eats the profit. The move: sell the first job at the door, then bundle repeat jobs into a membership paid monthly, so one win becomes recurring revenue. Why it worked: the membership turns a one-off low-ticket sale into lifetime value high enough to pay for door-to-door selling. Fits when: the job recurs on a schedule and customers are happy to be billed regularly. Does not fit when: the job is genuinely one-off, or the business cannot meet off-premises consumer contract rules (X22). Linked rules: M8, M9, M23, M42, X3, X22.

- K7 [a business selling a low-price product automatically online, with a share of buyers able to afford a much bigger offer] Situation: the automated funnel makes small sales but leaves the bigger buyers unserved. The move: add a call team that contacts recent automated buyers, qualifies them and offers the higher-priced next step (ascension). Why it worked: buyers who have already paid once are warm, trust is partly built, and the call team only spends time on people who already said yes once. Fits when: the bigger offer is worth enough to pay for the calls, and calls are lawful on the channel used. Does not fit when: there is no higher offer worth calling about, or the calls would breach calling and marketing rules in the buyers' country (X1). Linked rules: M8, M31, M16, M33, C3, C5, C14, X1, X18.

- K8 [an agency or service business in a market where every business needs the service, but clients churn, prices are low and delivery is labour-heavy] Situation: demand needs no testing (every business wants what the service gives, for example more customers), yet the market is crowded with small vendors. A need that every business has, served by very many vendors, signals low stickiness; a need served by only a few vendors that clients rarely leave signals high stickiness. The causes of low stickiness here are clients who cannot carry out their side, prices set too low for the work, and delivery that is hard to run consistently. The move: rebuild delivery so AI does most of the repeatable work under a named human check, price consistently for the result, and use the lower delivery cost to make the low and middle price points profitable and the results steady enough to keep clients longer. Why it worked: the demand was already proven, so the only open problems were pricing and operations, and AI reduces both the cost and the variation of delivery. While good AI delivery is still hard to set up, that difficulty keeps most rivals out; the advantage shrinks as it becomes easy for everyone (M10). Fits when: the service's output can be checked against a clear standard, AI matches that standard after setup and testing, and retention can be measured by batch. Does not fit when: AI is used out of the box without testing against the human standard, results cannot be made consistent, or the service is regulated so that a human must do the work. Linked rules: F33, F34, O28, D3, D4, D5, M1, M9, M10, L1, X1.

- K9 [a professional service (accounting, legal, IT, consulting) sold as a generalist, competing on price with many similar firms] Situation: the firm can serve anyone, so nobody sees why to pay it more. The move: pick one client group that passes the market tests (growing, can afford it, easy to find, in pain), list what those business owners want and struggle with beyond the firm's own service (more customers, profit, staff, a sellable business), and publish free, practical content on those topics drawn from what the firm sees across its clients, so the firm's name becomes paired with the clients' own goals; the core service is then sold as the way to get them. Why it worked: content that solves the avatar's wider business problems keeps small promises the buyer can check, which makes them trust the big promise, and one client group means the same work repeats, so margins rise and the price can go up. Fits when: the firm has real, checkable insight across several clients of the same kind, shared without client names or data. Does not fit when: the insight would break client confidentiality or professional rules (X4, X5, X8), or the chosen group fails the market tests. Linked rules: L1, L2, O2, O3, O19, L60, L65, M2, M13, X4.

- K10 [a business with an audience that has seen it keep its promises many times, launching a new product] Situation: the owner has delivered more than buyers paid for, again and again, to one audience. The move: launch the new product first to that existing audience (a live session or recording), take orders in the order they arrive, and let those who missed the session join the queue. Why it worked: buyers who have only had kept promises from the owner trust the next one before they see the details, so the launch sells without new proof or persuasion. Fits when: the record of kept promises is real and the new product truly helps the same audience. Does not fit when: the audience is new or has had promises broken, or the queue or scarcity is invented (X10), or buyers would pay without knowing what they buy (C1 read-back: every buyer still gets the full terms before paying, X3). Linked rules: L65, O37, M31, X3, X10, C1.

- K11 [a well-known brand pairing itself with a person, cause or campaign its core buyers dislike] Situation: a mass consumer brand ran a campaign with a figure whose associations a large part of its core buyers disliked; the campaign was seen widely, so as advertising it worked, but as branding it failed with its own buyers, the brand backed away quickly, and sales fell. The move to learn from (by avoiding it): test every pairing against what the core avatar likes, and count who it gains and who it loses before running it. Why it failed: the people it gained were fewer than the core buyers it lost, and backing away then lost the new people too. Fits when: any business choosing partners, endorsers, sponsorships or public positions. Does not fit when: the business's own avatar likes the association, in which case the same pairing can be good branding for it. Linked rules: O2, X14, X23.

- K12 [selling a good new product to an industry whose buyers are shrinking every year] Situation: a supplier offered a shrinking industry a sensible add-on that let its businesses earn from the newer, growing channel with no extra work. The offer was good, but the industry lost a large share of its businesses each year, so the supplier had to win share every year just to stay the same size, its buyers had less and less money, and good customers kept closing. The lesson (to avoid it): a good offer cannot outrun a shrinking market; serve the growing side of the change directly, or pick a market with more demand than supply. Fits when: choosing or re-scoring a market (L1), or when sales stall while the offer tests well. Does not fit when: the shrinking market still has a large, stable core of buyers with money and the business only needs a small share of it (check the clients needed against the base, L1). Linked rules: L1, F33, M10, L3.

- K13 [a service whose result is a percentage gain for the client (conversion, cost saving, recovered revenue, speed), sold to small clients for small fees] Situation: the same work lifts a small client's revenue a little and a large client's revenue a lot, but the owner prices for the small client. The move: sell the same work to clients many times larger, where the same percentage gain is worth many times more, and price on that value. Before deciding the business is bad at selling, check in order: is the work good (measured results, D8), then who is it sold to; a buyer too small to feel the gain will not pay for it. Why it worked: the value comes from the client's size, not from extra effort, so the price can rise with no more work. Fits when: the result is measurable in the client's own numbers and the owner can reach and serve larger clients with proof (L32). Does not fit when: there is no proof yet at any size, or larger clients need security, compliance or capacity the business cannot meet (D11, X5). Linked rules: M2, L1, L2, O3, M3, D8, L32.

- K14 [a creative or expert service where buyers value the idea far more than the making of it] Situation: a creative firm sold finished design work at good but capped fees; when it once gave away an idea, the buyer said they would have paid a lot for it. The move: sell the concept, strategy or diagnosis as the main paid product, and price the execution separately or hand it to cheaper producers with a clear brief. Why it worked: the buyer's pain sits in not knowing what to do, while execution is easy to buy elsewhere, so the idea carries the price. Fits when: the owner's ideas produce results buyers can see and execution can be specified in a brief. Does not fit when: the buyer's real problem is execution capacity, or the idea cannot be judged before the work is done (then sell a paid diagnosis first, M2, C5). Linked rules: M2, O2, O34, D5, M31.

- K15 [a seasonal service business that makes its money in one season and loses money or lays staff off in the other] Situation: a field-service firm earns its profit in a short peak season (for example a weather-driven service) and runs several other lines in the rest of the year at a loss, with crews hired and let go each season and the owner feeling the business is four businesses at once. The move: accept the seasonal swing as the business's built-in hard part (F28), not a fault to escape; run one main service for each season with the same crews, never calling either the "off season" to the team, so effort holds all year; give crews a real reward for bringing in friends during the peak (lawful, X18); fix the losing season's sales process first and then raise its price, since in a business limited by workers rather than buyers price is the easiest lever (M5, D11); use the better margin to keep more staff full-time, which steadies delivery. When choosing which of two other-season lines to keep, pick by acquisition cost against lifetime gross profit and how long clients stay (M23, M24), since the owner's day at scale will look the same either way. Why it works: the peak season already proves demand and margin; the loss sits in the other season's pricing and selling, and steadier crews cut rehiring and training costs. Fits when: demand is reliable in each season and the same crews can do both services. Does not fit when: one season has no demand that pays above cost even at a fair price, in which case cut it and plan cash and staffing around the peak (M26, F2). Linked rules: F28, F3, M5, M23, M24, D11, T1, C2, X18.

- K16 [a high-end local practice whose clients are wealthy and live or socialise in the same clubs and communities] Situation: the practice relied on paid marketing and public events, which were seasonal and thin. The move: run an event at a club where one satisfied client was a member, at that client's invitation; other members saw it promoted and asked for the same event at their own clubs, so the channel spread through clients at almost no acquisition cost. Every visit was also designed to produce a referral, and something valuable was given away instead of discounting. Why it worked: wealthy buyers trust what their peers bring into their own spaces, and one host opens a room full of people like them. Fits when: clients cluster in member communities and have had real results. Does not fit when: the profession's rules limit promotion or inducements (X4), or the host is rewarded without disclosure (X14, X16). Linked rules: L79, L66, M28, M2, X4. Follow-up from a later source: the practice's referral offer became guest places for friends at each treatment and a quarterly giveaway to the list; seasonal population changes made outside events scarce, so the owner set up their own talk series at venues where ideal clients live (L79).

- K17 [a service business selling large one-off projects with prices made up deal by deal, thin margins and reps who close worse than the owner] Situation: each quote was improvised, some deals lost money, and the sales team converted far below the owner. The move: build a quote formula from cost basis so every deal is profitable by design, add a price-lock guarantee and prepaid and multi-project terms that turn one-off buyers into multi-year contracts, move reps to setting appointments while the owner closes, give same-day quotes, and pick one channel where hundreds of business buyers gather (sponsored booths and speaking at industry events, with a short talk and an on-the-spot opt-in). Why it worked: the formula stopped loss-making deals, multi-year terms raised the value per client and made revenue predictable, and the event channel put the owner in front of many buyers at once. Fits when: buyers book repeat projects and gather at industry events. Does not fit when: margins are thin because the market is a commodity with no room to raise value, which an investor may still see as a weak opportunity (F30). Linked rules: M1, M40, M37, C13, C3, L79, F3.

- K18 [a family-run clinical or expert practice where one senior practitioner is the only person who can do the core work] Situation: the business across several sites was capped by one person's calendar, with a very high close rate and prices that had not kept up, and the senior practitioner believed buyers would never pay up front. The move: hire practitioners (where possible with qualifications buyers find reassuring) to take routine work, shift the senior practitioner's role from best practitioner to best teacher of practitioners, raise prices because the close rate showed they were too low, and collect more cash up front for long treatments. Why it worked: capacity was the constraint, so added practitioners and higher prices raised revenue at once, and proof from the first large up-front sale broke the old belief. Fits when: demand already exceeds the senior practitioner's capacity. Does not fit when: professional rules limit who may do the work or how fees are taken (X4), or new practitioners would be presented as more than their real qualifications (X9). Linked rules: D5, F30, M5, M37, C2, D11, X4, X9.

- K19 [a cash-strapped venue or service running a big giveaway to bring in money fast] Situation: running at half capacity and short of cash, the business picked one offer and ran a giveaway that produced thousands of leads, far more than its team had ever handled, and most were never converted; revenue fell while it cleaned up its numbers. The lesson (to avoid it): one offer and a giveaway can work, but set follow-up capacity, scripts and the sales process before the push and stagger it to match (L72), and know the numbers first so the constraint is visible (M46). Fits when: planning any lead surge. Does not fit when: the team already handles that lead volume. Linked rules: L72, M28, M46, F3, M26. The full play, from a later source on the same kind of business: work out the monthly gap to break even and debt payments, pick the one seasonal offer that brings cash forward before the slow season (a full-season pass framed as "buy a month, get the season" with the reason for the deal stated), clean the page (one message, the buyer's biggest fear answered with a true safety record next to the button, relevant reviews, a short video at the top, a stated money-back promise), then run a 7-day giveaway with a big first prize and a second prize that is the core offer at a discount, given to more than one winner, a countdown, a 72-hour window to claim, and extra entries for referring other families (prize-promotion rules checked locally, X10, X1); call every entrant, then switch to the standing offer. Result reported: a large jump in bookings, capped by too few people to call the leads, so hire callers before the next run.

- K20 [an expert coaching business where the owner is the bottleneck, selling through long pitches and cheap one-to-one time] Situation: the owner answered every repeat question, sold in pitches of several hours and underpriced one-to-one time. The move: build a system so the team answers repeat questions without the owner, price one-to-one time properly, cut the pitch to a short weekly webinar that is clear about when it teaches and when it sells, drop the cheapest tier, shorten the core programme and offer the next level part-way through it instead of at the end. Why it worked: the owner's hours went to selling one-to-many, and the upsell moved to the moment buyers felt the next need (M31, M35). Fits when: the owner's expertise is proven and the questions repeat. Does not fit when: results depend on the owner's personal one-to-one time, which then becomes the premium tier rather than being removed (M7). Linked rules: D5, M2, M31, M35, M7, C5.

- K21 [an owner of local service outlets who also coaches other owners in the same trade, where coaching clients keep asking to have the work done for them] Situation: the owner taught other owners leadership and marketing, including an acquisition method from their own outlets: sell a popular, well-known product line online with shopping ads, put a voucher for the local service in each parcel, and retarget buyers until they visit as service clients. Coaching clients didn't want to build the online store themselves and asked the owner to do it, so the business drifted into an agency model, while the owner's own outlets split their attention. The move: sell the outlets to focus, since the coaching and done-for-you business could replace their net income within months; lead with a fixed-term done-for-you build followed by continuity for running the store and its search work; keep the coaching as the front end; tell the story of building and exiting the outlets as proof; sell the growth, with the mechanism as a short part of the pitch. Why it worked: the coaching sold easily, the done-for-you service and physical products kept clients, and the owner's attention went to the one business that had both. Fits when: the owner's method is proven in their own business and clients repeatedly ask for it done for them. Does not fit when: done-for-you clients churn fast, or the acquisition method breaks platform, brand or marketing rules (retargeting needs lawful cookie and marketing consent, X2, X8; reselling a brand's products needs genuine stock and claims, X9). Linked rules: M8, F22, M9, M7, C5, O4, X2, X8.

- K22 [a consulting service sold to small-business owners with plenty of organic attention, where booked buyers don't understand what is being sold] Situation: content brought shares, saves and booked calls, but buyers arrived not knowing what the service was or how it worked, so every call had to bridge that gap, and clients who bought often didn't take the actions that produced results. The move: a pre-call video and booking questions that explain the offer and pre-frame the purchase; good friction to turn away poor fits; a fast-cash campaign from the client's own past customers and lists as the first onboarding step, so the fee is covered by money the client wouldn't otherwise have made; bonuses and a conditional guarantee tied to the client's actions, stated before purchase and enforced. Why it works: buyers who understand the offer close faster, and a client who earns the fee back in the first month stays and acts. Fits when: demand already exceeds what the sales process converts. Does not fit when: the constraint is attention, not conversion (L1, L41). Linked rules: C2, D7, O8, F28, M20, X2, X11.

- K23 [an experienced owner or investor about to put money into a company after working with it closely] Situation: an investor did months of paid consulting with a company, met its team, visited its offices and saw it grow, then rolled the fee and a large investment into it; days later the founder vanished, and it emerged that the team included hired actors and the company kept two sets of books, so the investor could not even serve legal papers. The lesson (to avoid it): closeness and expertise are not verification. Run the four goods before any money moves (T3): independent background checks offered both ways, the bad-case question with past evidence, a spreadsheet the investor understands, and contracts that can be enforced against someone who can be found; and tell the people who share the money (a spouse, a partner) about every large decision. Fits when: any investment, partnership or acquisition. Linked rules: T3, M19, F2, X20.

- K24 [a specialist with a very small, very niche following who sells a low-priced product or books calls] Situation: one specialist with a few thousand followers taught a narrow professional skill to others in the same profession, with posts that barely got likes, and still made steady daily sales of a low-priced product; she then personally emailed every buyer offering her higher-priced programme, which most of the revenue came from. Another, an agency serving only large online stores, posted detailed results threads to a few thousand followers with a calendar link in the profile and booked several qualified calls a day. The lesson: reach is not the measure; a narrow audience that is exactly the avatar converts, and a personal next-step message to every low-price buyer turns small sales into large ones. Fits when: the niche is narrow, valuable and reachable. Does not fit when: the content goes to people outside the avatar, or the next-step messages are sent without a lawful basis (X2). Linked rules: L60, L2, M8, M31, F14.

- K25 [a buyer of an existing business or franchise location who discovers the seller defrauded customers before the sale] Situation: after the purchase, it emerged the previous owner had invoiced customers, taken payment, shipped nothing and voided the invoices to hide it; dozens of customers were suing and the location's reputation was badly damaged. The lessons: one-time, high-stakes, irreversible deals (buying a business or property) carry the highest risk of hidden wrongdoing, so due diligence comes before signing (T3 four goods, independent checks of sales records against bank receipts and customer complaints, warranties and indemnities in the contract, X20); after the fact, suing recovers money only if the other side has it and it covers every claimant, it takes years and legal fees, so weigh the likely recovery against the cost and time with a solicitor, and often put the energy into outgrowing the loss. Fits when: buying any business, location or asset. Linked rules: T3, M19, F2, X20.
