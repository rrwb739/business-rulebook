## Leads and marketing (L)

### Market and avatar
- L1 [choosing, reviewing or questioning the market; every quarter] Market test: score the market on massive pain, purchasing power, easy to target and growing, each with its evidence, and write the scores and a verdict in the instructions box. Market beats offer, and offer beats persuasion. Channel existing demand; never try to create it. To find demand to channel, keep a standing scan (by hand or by an AI tool) of problems people in the market report publicly in their own words (communities, reviews, forums), logging the problem and wording without names or handles (X8) into the O5 phrase bank and any new idea onto the F22 idea list; score each candidate on these market tests plus one feasibility test: can the business solve it with the owner's skills and tools that exist today, before any build (F16). Vehicle check at each re-score: name the kind of vehicle the business is (for example expert-dependent, F30) and any time-limited window driving demand (a regulatory or ageing shift); log how much of the window is left and what replaces it (M10). Score any candidate market under L3(d) on four more tests: what it looks like maxed out (a large, sellable business in the project's country, or only good incomes?), how easily anyone can enter (more barriers, fewer rivals), whether the demand comes from a regulatory or ageing shift and when that ends, and whether it sounds dull (dull usually means fewer rivals); pick the vehicle that fits the goal written in F22, never the highest ceiling alone. Market ladder, re-checked each quarter: one of the three markets that always exist (wealth, health, relationships), then the sub-market, then the avatar's niche, then the new opportunity (O2). At each re-score, also log monthly search volume for 5 to 10 of the avatar's own problem terms as a growth signal only, never a market test (purchasing power still decides); mark yes or no for active communities, the market's own vocabulary, its own events and paid trainers or consultants already selling to it; and ask 5 avatar buyers in genuine F25 interviews whether the new opportunity interests them, logging their words in the O5 phrase bank. Re-score every quarter, first rereading the positioning line and the L3 need-to-believes for drift in copy or delivery, and tell the owner if any score changes. Rivals entering are evidence of demand, not a reason to leave: answer them with a category of one (O2). Check the customers needed against the avatar's size in the current region; if the plan needs above about 5% of it, widen sooner. Most "saturated market" claims are really a lack of skill at reaching the rest of it. At each re-score, also check for tighter buyer budgets (more money objections, more "not now", failed payments); if they rise, lean on the return close (C7) and M37 plans before touching the price. Where the avatar can be counted from public registers, count new entrants each year as a later, replenishing lead pool and an input to the plan for after the window (M10); very young businesses usually fail L2's steady-base preference, so they aren't the main pool.
- L2 [defining or checking the avatar, or any idea outside the niche] Avatar: define it in the instructions box by objective, checkable criteria (who the buyer is, size, any legal form that decides whether they can be contacted lawfully, X2, and the minimum need that makes the offer and the guarantee work), and write why each floor exists (for example, below a set volume the buyer can't get the promised result, so the guarantee and price don't work). Cover only the area the offer works in. Use a local callout only where it is true for that prospect. Turn away anyone outside the avatar, even if the business could help them: name the objective criterion and why it matters, one true, specific thing liked about their business, wish them well, offer the free self-serve version, name one provider genuinely known to fit them where there is one (no commission, logged; F15, O41), and, if the exchange gave them something useful, ask once whether they know someone who does fit and take up any introduction they offer (C11, L66), and don't reverse under pressure or through anyone with influence (L4). That includes people who could resell the method (consultants, trainers, outsourcers): materials are licensed for the client's internal use only, stated in writing before they commit (X3), without limiting what its own staff do with its own customers. Within the avatar, prefer buyers with a steady base (several years trading, more than one fee earner): a client's volatility becomes the business's, and the smallest buyers are likelier to drop a membership in a bad quarter. Push back if the owner drifts. Never narrow L2 further until the list counts (L9) show the narrower group still gives room for the daily outreach volume (L41). After the first target, a disclosed referral swap with a rival for buyers outside the avatar is fine (any commission disclosed, O41); never agree prices, fees or which buyers each targets (see X20). When expansion is on the table (after the first target), there are five directions: up market, down market, adjacent (a similar buyer with a different model), narrower, broader. Up market raises acquisition cost but usually improves payment reliability and retention: score both before choosing. Default to narrower: most businesses grow more by serving one tighter avatar better, and a narrow niche can still be large if the business is the best in it. Use a local callout and local proof per region where true (O15). To choose between directions, score each as expected lifetime gross profit to acquisition cost (M23) times reachable buyers in the region, and pick the highest. Before any direction, write the single worst thing buyers there put up with now and how the offer removes it; with no answer, don't go yet.
- L3 [results are poor, or the owner wants to change niche, market or business] Decide in this order. (a) Inputs: were daily messages consistent? If not, fix that first (L17). (a2) List: if replies are near zero, sample about 20 leads against L2's criteria before touching the offer; near-zero response usually means the wrong people, not a bad offer. (a3) Talk: before changing the offer, script or delivery, speak to 3 to 5 prospects (replied prospects, pilots, no-shows) and ask what stopped them or what's missing. (b) Offer: while L1 scores the market normal or better, change the offer, not the market; test at least 3 genuinely different offers (different promise, guarantee or price model), each with at least 100 messages at consistent volume. (b2) Avatar: are the buyers who buy the ones who can get and pay for the result? Check the F12 traits and red flags before changing the offer again or the market. (c) Market: consider a new market only if two or more L1 tests score bad, or all 3 offers fail and the replies point to the market. (d) When moving, keep the skill and method, and score at least 3 candidate markets first, each on L1 plus three tests: results countable for that segment (D8), proof that will be believed there (L32) and a true category of one (O2). (e) Never automatic: run the council and bring the owner the decision. When a need-to-believe or a test is disproved by its number, the council and anyone consulted get the numbers first; agreement from friends, peers or the council is never evidence the belief still holds, and nor is gut feel, which only breaks a tie where no number exists (F20). A failed offer means the offer is bad, not the owner. Expect most offers to fail and act boldly anyway: one winner pays for the misses. No niche-hopping on feeling: every market has downsides and switching resets to zero. Push or pivot: write the need-to-believes in the instructions box (the demand driver holds, the avatar exists in numbers, it can be reached lawfully, it pays for this kind of help, and no vendor or tool makes the offer redundant; check the last one at every quarterly L1 re-score). At that re-score, also name the one deliverable a vendor release or tool is most likely to replace in the next 12 months, and template, fold in or retire it before clients ask why they pay for it (O29, M40). While the need-to-believes hold, hard is the good kind: push and iterate. Pivot only when data proves one of them false.

### Channel
- L4 [any outreach or lead generation] One channel until the first target (F3). "Channel" means one of the Core Four (L40); write the chosen one and its sub-rules (who it goes to, how each medium is sent, daily volumes per medium) in the instructions box, and use no other Core Four method until the first target unless the instructions box names it. Each message is personalised and sent from the owner's own name. Messaging apps, where used: only to numbers the prospect itself publishes as a business contact, never numbers found elsewhere; sent one by one, never broadcast lists, and never by an automation tool where the platform's terms bar it (a ban loses the number); ideally from a business account on a separate number; identify the owner and offer an opt-out in the first message; follow-ups may go by email inside the email budget (L41) while replies are answered on the app. [Fixed] Messaging apps and live calls follow the same lawful rules as email (X2; see X2, X6). A live follow-up call to the prospect's published business number is allowed ("I'm calling to follow up on my email"), screened first (X2). Letters by post are allowed as a later test. Before that test, cost print and postage per letter against cost per engaged lead from email (M19). The pack: a plain addressed envelope (tested against one short true teaser line), a one-page letter whose first lines do the envelope's job (post is often opened by someone other than the buyer), one proof insert and one reply device (a short web address or QR code to the lead magnet, plus a phone number), one job per insert, each part judged only on moving them to reply; the first side carries the hook, the one benefit, the response route, who the owner is and an opt-out. Test one genuinely useful printed insert the buyer keeps in view (with a one-line contact on it) against none, and a short handwritten line with initials and a thank you against print only. [Fixed] Never send anything with a demand for payment; keep any insert, gift or hospitality low value, the same for everyone and never conditional; never make post look like official mail; and have the data protection notices ready before mailing named people (see X7, X16, X8). Later test, once email and follow-up calls have readable samples (L43): one trade event or show as a medium within the channel, costed per engaged lead against email (M19), within the owner's set hours and the host's selling rules, run from one written event checklist so it can be handed off (D5). Before it, put the published exhibitor or attendee firms through L8 and pick the top 5 (published details only, a bought delegate list only after the L9 and L49 checks); on the day, open with one research fact, ask whether a follow-up is welcome and pitch only if asked; get recorded consent on the day where X2 needs it before any marketing email; follow up within 48 hours. Judge it on engaged leads at 4 weeks and cash at 6 months, set in writing beforehand as placeholders (L62). When choosing the one channel, any of the Core Four can work if done to volume: pick the one with the most overlap with the owner's and team's existing skills, and if one is already bringing clients, do far more of it before learning another.
- L5: moved to X2.
- L6 [setting up or scaling email] Send from separate sending domains, never the main one. Set up SPF, DKIM and DMARC, and include a one-line unsubscribe in every email. Keep each sending inbox at 30 to 40 a day, and reach the daily target (L41) by adding inboxes as they are set up, never by pushing more through one inbox. Follow-ups and calls come on top of the first-touch target. If the owner chooses to send from one personal inbox before sending inboxes exist, write it in the instructions box as a departure from this rule, set a daily cap well under the provider's sending limit (shared across every project using that inbox, with headroom left for replies), and count what it has sent today before every batch; if the provider shows any sending-limit warning, or bounces pass 3% that day, halve the budget from the next send and tell the owner. On any setup, if bounces pass 3% or spam complaints pass 0.3%, halve the volume for a week. Every cold email asks for a short reply, because replies help the sending domain's deliverability; service messages to clients (a schedule change, a reminder) may say no reply is needed.
- L7 [deciding whether to automate outreach] Every message is personalised to the prospect with one genuine, checked detail (L10, L50), but AI tools may research, draft and send it if the owner chooses. The owner approves every template and the sending rules, reads a random sample of at least 20 drafts before each day's batch goes out, and reads every reply. A draft without a checked, prospect-specific detail is not sent: no identical blasts. Every message still meets X2 [Fixed] (lawful recipients only, identifies the owner, opt-out, suppression list).

### Lead lists
- L8 [the owner asks for leads, a lead list or a lead sheet, or mentions leads in any way] Maintain one master lead sheet (create it the first time, then always add to the same one). When asked for leads: find prospects that fit L2 using L9's sources; keep only those the business may lawfully contact on the channel (X2); skip any already in the sheet; research each one (website, services page, team page, reviews, recent news); find the decision maker by role and a work contact the business itself publishes; check the name's spelling against an official register or the team page (including names an AI draft or researcher supplies), never a raw merge field; write one first message following L10 and one follow-up. Where a qualifying count can't be seen from outside, estimate it from size and the services shown, mark it "estimate", and confirm it on the call (C2). Columns: business, registration number, town, years trading, staff estimate, need signal, contact role, names spoken to (role, spelling, phonetic note), contact and where it was published, fit score 1 to 5, business type, trigger (a recent event on its own business sources), recent change (a job advert, a senior person leaving or joining, a merger, a system switch; never personal details, C4), last action, next touch due (from the L12 cadence; weekends move to Monday), first message, follow-up, status, date sent, replied, call booked. Write what a 1, 3 and 5 fit score mean from L2's criteria only, and never add engagement points (opens, clicks, downloads). After a 4 or 5 score or a reply, add 3 to 5 lookalikes (same directory category, town or size) to the next batch, each still checked and researched by hand, and log minutes per lead sourced (T2). Lead with triggered and changed prospects, using the event as the genuine detail (L10); for pilots, prefer avatar buyers where the main objections bite least. Run in the background in batches of 10 to 20 and report: added, skipped and why, average fit score. Never send anything. Keep a short list of top prospects at the head of the sheet, and once there are sales, recalibrate the fit score from the traits of closed deals (F12). Log the role of each person who replies or books, and after 100 contacts re-point the contact role column if a different role replies most.
- L9 [building a cold list] Build from the avatar's own professional or trade directories, official business registers and the prospects' own websites; name the sources in the instructions box. Manual lists are less saturated. Bought lists still need a lawful basis (X2): test a sample first. Prefer a public record that proves both that the buyer can pay and that they have the specific problem the offer solves (an asset, licence, filing or listing on a public register): follow it from the record to the owning business and its decision maker, and use it as the genuine detail and the reason for writing (L10). Where the record leads to a private individual rather than a business, the data is still personal data (see X8) and electronic marketing to them needs consent (see X2); never approach anyone at home.

### Messages
- L10 [writing a cold email or first message] Three steps: how the owner found them, with one specific, genuine detail about their business; one line on how the business helps buyers like them (one benefit); then "reply and I'll send you [named asset]" if the asset exists, otherwise an open question about how they handle the problem now. Under 100 words, a subject line under 6 words, the owner's name, an opt-out, and a P.S. Never open with a generic pitch and never sell in the first message: the only aim is a reply. Script frame: the short form in O3, plus a true reason for any limit on free places. Name the asset prospects most often ask for, forward or reuse, and lead with that. The subject and first line never name the business or the offer and never pitch; with the preview text, they carry the whole reason to reply. Each first message goes to one named person, never several decision makers copied in; in a three-way introduction or group thread, say by name who should reply. Where the only address is generic, address it to the named decision maker if the team page names one ("For [name]", never "Hi there") and ask for one role and one action ("could you pass this to whoever runs [the task]?"), never "let me know if anyone is interested". Name the one cue each message leans on (a local anchor, true proof, a genuine similarity) and check it is true and visible in the first two lines: rushed readers decide on one cue. Put the ask straight after the genuine detail or true proof line, and never put penalty or threat wording in the same sentence as the owner's name or the ask. Tests (L63), judged on bookings and cash per 100 contacted (L18), never on reply count alone: a follow-up that sends the named asset unasked, with no condition, against "reply and I'll send you"; a P.S. legitimising the smallest reply ("even a one-word reply is fine: yes or not now"), never used on price or the real commitment (O12); a message asking "what's your biggest question about [the problem]?", saying who the owner is and what the business sells (F25), with every answer logged in L59; a subject made of two true facts about them; a low-pressure ask with an easy out ("is [the problem] on your list this quarter, or already sorted?") against the open question; and, where the team page names an office or operations manager, a short message to that manager asking who runs the task against messaging the decision maker.
- L11 [writing any outreach or copy, or explaining anything to clients or staff] Clear, not clever. Aim for below US grade 3 (reading age about 8), and always stay below US grade 5 (reading age about 10): short sentences, small words. Deletion beats explanation: cut it as short as possible first (cut half the words; if it still makes sense, cut half again), then check every template and every web and opt-in page with a free reading-level tool before sending, editing until it reads below US grade 3 without changing the meaning, then test the simpler version against the old one. Plain words help experts read faster and beginners understand at all, so it is never talking down. Use analogies from the reader's own working world; the narrower the audience, the narrower the analogy. Pick one frame for the offer and keep it in the first message, pre-call video, call and kickoff (D15); test one alternative frame (L63). At every technical term the reader may not know, add one "it's kind of like..." line from their working life, checked by someone from the avatar: an unknown word loses the rest of the message. Swap fancy words for plain ones. No marketing words ("streamline", "unlock", "seamless"); fewer adverbs, plain strong verbs. With staff and clients, the aim is to change what they do, not to impress them. Simple beats short. Keep please and thank you when cutting words: courtesy isn't padding. If a phrase sounds like other ads ("save time", "peace of mind"), rewrite it as a concrete picture. If a sentence needs more than one comma, split it. Use full stops and line breaks, not trailing dots. If the owner can't explain something simply, learn it better before writing; complexity makes the value equation worse (O21). Start each writing session by tightening one line of the claims file (O47) or the last draft before writing anything new. In the edit pass on every template, page and script, turn "the business", "the clients" and "the team" into "your...", and check each cold message has more "you" and "your" than "I" and "we". Keep the profession's own technical terms where they are the plain word for the thing, even when the tool flags them, simplify everything around them, and have someone from the avatar check the usage (terms only, never regulated advice, X4). Shared reference points from the avatar's age group and working history work as analogies, each fact-checked first. Test: could a 10-year-old get the point in 5 seconds? If the owner can't say what the offer does in one sentence, they don't understand it well enough yet. Copy should never draw attention to itself: ask reviewers "what did you picture?", and rewrite any line praised for its wording rather than for the buyer's problem or result. Test a correct-terms first message against a fully simplified one (L63) and keep whichever gets more replies.
- L12 [running outreach] Track every conversation: first message, follow-ups, ask, close. Front-load follow-ups (2 touches on day 1, 2 on day 2, 1 on day 7), adapted to the channel, and write the cadence in the instructions box (for example a day 1 email plus a follow-up call that afternoon, a day 2 follow-up email, a day 7 final email). Day 2 and day 7 follow-ups open by asking whether the asset helped with one named case, then the question or ask, never mentioning owing. Recontact non-responders after 3 to 6 months. Anyone who said no or opted out is never contacted again, apart from one reply within a working day: a line of thanks and confirmation they won't hear again, no pitch, then the suppression list (X2; see X2). A "not now" is recontacted once, about 3 to 4 weeks before the next date that matters to them, or after 3 to 6 months, whichever comes first, unless they asked not to be contacted. No first touches, final emails or follow-up calls in the avatar's busiest weeks (their deadlines and seasonal peaks): queue them for the days just after, so the owner's name isn't linked with their most stressful days. Every follow-up carries one new, prospect-specific item, never a copy of the first, and is timed to and opens on their latest action where there is one, using only signals they knowingly gave. Log which touch number brings each reply and booking; after about 300 leads, keep a touch only while it earns bookings for its time, never past a no or opt-out, and within the calling rules (L50, X2; see X6). Tests (L63): a true, warm close-off on day 7 ("I'll assume this isn't a priority this quarter; if that changes, just reply"), never false finality; and the full sequence with the call for fit 4 to 5 leads against email only for fit 2 to 3, on bookings and cash per 100 contacted (L18).
- L13 [a lead replies or books] Reply as fast as possible: within the hour during the owner's set outreach windows. Speed of follow-up is a bigger lever than close rate. Reply using ACA (L56): acknowledge what they said in the owner's own words, echoing their exact word for any feeling or key term, compliment them, then ask a question that steers towards booking a call. If they ask something answerable in a minute, answer it fully first (within the business's lane, X4), then ACA: never send them elsewhere or swap their question for a pitch. Fast replies are for interested leads: a rude or hostile reply waits until the next outreach block, then gets one calm line or just the opt-out confirmation (X2, L12, F27). When a reply names the right person instead, mark the lead "redirected" in the sheet (L8), contact that person within the same window, say truthfully who pointed the owner to them and where their details came from (see X8), and count redirects in the funnel (L30).
- L14 [checking where leads come from] Record every lead's source. Tag each lead as a Seed (referral or introduction, L65), a Net (lead magnet or free guide opt-in) or a Spear (cold outreach, L4), and keep each type's conversion numbers separate, never pooled; a referral gets a faster, warmer path and a cold lead the long runway (L76); the Core Four stay the rulebook's terms (L40). Put a clear call to action wherever attention lands (email signature, website). Once there are clients, compare their quality (attendance, tasks done, D8 counts) by entry route, and add friction (L15) if the low-effort route brings weaker clients.
- L15 [leads are poor or too few] Adjust friction: add qualifying steps if too many poor leads, remove steps if too few. If friction is low and leads are still poor, the targeting is wrong.
- L16 [after every 100 messages or 10 calls] Don't change an email template before about 300 sends, or a call script before 100 conversations. Then compare the best 10% with the rest, and what the worst 10% have in common (to cut), find what differs, and test one difference at a time. Until the owner beats a proven script's numbers, copy it exactly with more volume. A proven script means the owner's own winner or a proven structure: widely published templates wear out once recipients have seen them, so borrow the structure and write the words in the owner's voice (W1). Learning means changed behaviour. Stick to the script word for word up to the ask, then branch into the objection answers (C14), so results show whether a problem is the script or the delivery. Keep scripts tight: no extra lines or rituals that lengthen calls. A call script is about 12 questions, each with one or two follow-ups, written as questions rather than paragraphs. Keep separate scripts for cold calls and for referral or inbound calls. For each script question, map the likely answers and the next move; if the owner doesn't know the answer to their question, ask about their question, and on price, terms or data say it will be checked and answered in writing by a set time, never guess (C5, O11). Write scripts someone without the owner's background could run from the page. Before writing or changing any script, email or video, write its single point in one line (first message: get a reply; pre-call video: they turn up believing it could work for them); if it can't be written, fix that first. Under it, write one line on why the buyer would want to do it, in their terms; if that line is about the owner, rewrite. Keep a one-page call sheet in CLOSER order with tick boxes and a names box (each name as given, spelling asked if unclear, used at the opening and the ask, about twice a call); the price step stays blank until the qualifying boxes (lawful to contact, the qualifying count, who decides and is on the call, the named owner and staff hours, the problem in their words, the goal with its number and what missing it costs, their plan for the next key date, and their date) are ticked. Keep a one-page written sales method (the buyer's journey, L76, the owner's steps mapped to it, and the qualifying boxes): the base for the owner now and any later hire's training. Before rewriting a script, check against the recording that it was delivered as written: a mediocre script delivered well beats a great one delivered badly, and in copy, care and accuracy beat flair. Aim for small repeated gains at each step (redo, get about 5% better, repeat).
- L17 [results are low or volatile] First ask: what did the owner stop or add in the months before the drop (a new supplier or assistant, T2, a lead source, template or tool)? Find the week the line first flattened in the funnel tracker (L30), then list every change in the weeks before it. Results lag inputs; when results rise, ask what was done 3 to 6 months earlier and do more of it. Check daily inputs were consistent before changing the offer. Never blame lead quality: poor leads are training.
- L18 [judging a channel or how much effort to put in] Judge by total return (calls booked, cash collected), not by ratio alone. Judge lists and targeting by cost per sale, not cost per reply or booking. A falling close rate is fine when more prospects reach calls and total sales rise: don't over-qualify to protect the rate. Credit each step, not the last touch: the follow-up or review call will look as if it booked the sale, but the first message or the lead magnet did the earlier work; referrals will look best but can't be scaled on demand. Once there are 10 or more sales, value each engaged-lead type (L30) as its conversion to sale times average first-year gross profit (M23) and put effort behind the highest; before that, use step rates only (L43).
