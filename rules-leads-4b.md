## Leads and marketing (L), continued

### Execution
- L70 [planning the owner's day or week for leads] One-page advertising plan: (1) pick the type of engaged lead (clients now; later employees or affiliates); (2) pick rule of 100 or open to goal; (3) fill in the daily checklist (who, what, where, to whom, when, why, how, how much, how many follow-ups, how long); (4) do it daily until someone else can be paid to; (5) then recruit help and repeat. The current plan lives in the instructions box. Guard one long uninterrupted outreach block every working day, before anything else, at the same time and place each week, protected even in busy call weeks; use quiet call weeks to work the pipeline (within L12). Don't change the routine (tools, location, schedule) in the middle of an outreach run. In the outreach block the only allowed activities are outreach or doing nothing: no lead-sheet tidying, tool testing, rulebook work or admin; research batches and reminders get their own blocks (L50); if stuck, sit with the list open until something is sent. Work leads that acted since the last block first (replied, used the asset, booked, rang back), then due follow-ups, then new touches. Once there is a team (stage S3+), plan advertising in four directions, not one: prospects into clients, clients into repeat buyers, candidates into employees (T1), and employees into engaged, better-trained staff; growth on the client side needs the same growth on the staff side.
- L71 [the owner is discouraged, comparing themselves with others, or wants to quit] The many-sided die: every roll teaches something, and results compound. The owner can't see what roll others are on; successful people look lucky because they kept rolling, and the owner can't lose without quitting. Once something works, stick with it for years; don't swap methods after a few losses. Anyone earning more is better at some part of the game: learn from them instead of putting it down to luck, shortcuts or cheating. Growth comes in steps (flat stretches, then a jump once something is figured out), so a flat month isn't proof of failure: check inputs first (L17). If a comparison makes the owner act worse (rushing, skipping steps, switching), drop it or compare with someone at the same stage. Growing pains beat the stress of standing still. Asking why it isn't working yet is the cue for the next input (10 more touches or one more call), not to re-plan or switch. After a failed offer, template or pilot, write down what it now takes, then start the retry within a week. Keep a one-page list of why the owner is sticking with the core offer (the L3 need-to-believes still holding, the market's timing, their own reasons) by the desk, and reread it before quitting or switching anything and before any F22 idea review. When stalling, write what the next 4 quarters look like if inputs stay as they are. Nothing hard lasts forever: the owner quits, it gets easier, or the owner gets better. Patience is working out what to do in the meantime. Keep a tally of hard things already got through, and reread it when the next one hits.
- L72 [a lead replies, books or opts in] Speed to lead: contact an inbound lead as fast as possible, ideally within 60 seconds; a slow reply lets them shop around and compare prices. If the owner's set hours make that impossible, keep reply notifications on and a booking link ready to send at once, and target minutes rather than the hour in L13. Once there's budget, someone paid just to call new leads fast is one of the first hires. Before any push that will bring a surge of leads (a giveaway, an event, a launch, a big ad test), set the follow-up capacity first: who contacts each lead, within what time, with which script and booking slots, and how many leads that capacity can handle a day; cap or stagger the push to match, since a flood of leads nobody calls in time wastes the spend and the goodwill. In any campaign with a deadline (a giveaway, launch or offer ending), expect a large share of responses in the last day or two and a quiet middle that tempts the owner to stop: keep spending and following up through the middle, and staff calls for the final surge. When lead flow is steady and the callers can't keep up, hiring another caller is low-risk growth spend, since each one is paid from sales they would otherwise miss (C13, T1). Score leads at opt-in on the one or two facts that best predict a sale (asked on the form, C2) and call the highest scores first. Size the calling team to the daily lead flow; any automatic or multi-line dialler is used only on leads who gave consent to be called, within the country's calling rules (abandoned and silent call limits, autodialler consent; X2), confirm with a qualified adviser.
- L73 [making any video, writing a subject line or first line, or opening a call or session] [Fixed] The hook decides whether anything after it gets seen, so spend the most effort on the shortest part: the subject line, the first line, the first 5 seconds; lifting the front end multiplies everything behind it. Every email, message and call has a headline, so write it on purpose: curiosity first, then being different; a headline's only job is to get the next line read, so it doesn't sell or name the offer. In a batch, write many hooks (about 50 a session, about 80% reworked from proven winners and 20% new), a few bodies and one or two calls to action. Call out the reader as specifically as still reaches enough of them, in their words; if they wouldn't call themselves the label, move it up or down a level. Lead with proof, promise and plan (for videos, also picture and pain in the first 30 seconds), in any order, leading with whichever the audience most needs (proof first where its first question is why it should listen); in a teaching piece the intro proves the owner can deliver what the title promised rather than repeating the title, and cut every warm-up before the hook. Framing changes how everything after it is judged, so the pre-call video and each session's introduction shape the client's whole view. On video the hook is visual as well as verbal. Once a hook works, keep reusing it (L63). Source hooks from the avatar's own words in replies and calls (O5) and from proven winners, never from an AI brainstorm or gut feel alone (AI may vary a proven hook, not invent one); keep a swipe file of subject lines and openers the owner actually opened, study the first 3 seconds or first line of what already works, borrow structures from unrelated industries, and daily pull apart any message that made the owner click or reply. Hook types, true statements only and one type per campaign when testing (L63): quotes, questions, yes questions, if-then lines, "what [X] doesn't tell you" aimed at the prospect's own hidden cost, a myth reversal with the true answer in the asset, "can't be done" or "only" lines when literally true and evidenced, the reader's own frustration with the usual route, well known but little understood, little known with a big difference, "this changes everything" only for a real change, crystal ball only with a sourced figure (L76, O15), back to basics, and confirming a suspicion only when true and in the claims file; and the outcome-and-character hook: name a specific outcome the avatar wants (in their words, O5) and tie it to a real person who got it (the owner, a consented client like the viewer, L32 and X13, or a sourced public example, never implying endorsement, O25), with the true numbers and time frame, and the result labelled as one result unless it is typical (see X9). Never run down rivals, authorities, the prospect's staff or clients (O2, F27, M27), never use a secrecy primer, and never invent proof: until pilots, use the owner's own true proof (F21, O11, O15). Test a pain-first opener (line 1 names a recognisable pain and its one cause, line 2 the true proof line) against the proof-first control, and an outcome-first hook against an information or tip hook on the same body, judged on avatar opt-ins per 1,000 views (L60), not views. "Free" and "new" are the strongest words: use them only when literally true (see X9, X3). For long assets, draft the body first from the claims file (O47), then write the hook from its strongest claim, which must be unusual (not what rivals already say, O2), powerful (tied to the biggest pain or outcome in the avatar's words, O5; relevant, never overwhelming) and marked proven. To make new candidates, pair one real avatar phrase from the claims file with one image from their world, or a proven claim with a hidden feeling logged under O5, true and never shaming (M27). Long copy works for a motivated buyer with a real pain (O5 sets where it's allowed). Once a quarter, take one proven long-form piece, write out its strategy (target, big idea, claim order, proof, ask), then copy it out by hand as practice (F24), never as a template to send. Findings from the owner's own logged calls or a newly published update, dated in the claims file (O47), may be framed as recent and first-hand; never claim insider contacts. For the body of an ad, pitch or page, work through four angles on the one action asked for: what more good they get by taking it, what bad they avoid, what bad grows if they don't, and what good they miss; then replace each general word ("feel better", "save time") with a specific moment the avatar would recognise, taken from real reviews, calls and interviews in the avatar's words, true and evidenced (X9). Show the call to action as well as saying it, and make the next page look exactly as the ad says, so expectations are met.
- L74 [when warm outreach becomes possible] For a beginner, the short-term order is outreach to people the owner knows with a compelling offer, then content for the long term. If the owner's personal network can't be used (L4), cold outreach is slower to start: lean harder on volume and patience (L41, L43).
- L75 [reply or booking rates fall while volume and targeting stay the same; launching anything new; any unhappy client] Bad word of mouth travels further and faster than good. A sign: cost per engaged lead rises while the effort to reach each prospect stays the same, so fewer of those reached respond; this matters more in a small market where the avatar talks. Keep early mistakes small and private: run every new offer free with a few clients first, ask "tell me how I can make this better", fix what they say, and only then sell to strangers (M5). Don't take money from a client the owner expects to be unhappy, and fix any problem fast (M27). With any upset client: invite the whole complaint ("tell me all of it; I'd rather hear everything"), never minimise it, call the concern reasonable, be more upset about the problem than they are, name the feeling with their own word, never a softer one, then ask what you can do together to fix it, opening with the goal they set at kickoff (D13). In a payment or complaint dispute (L36), hear them out fully before discussing money and thank them for raising it. On a disputed result or guarantee claim, go through their evidence with them by question first before citing terms, and never let questions stall a valid claim (O8). Tell a client about any problem with their work, data or deadline the same day, in plain words with the fix. When the owner slips, tell the client the same day, before they notice: what happened, that it's the owner's, and the fix or a redo in the same breath; never blame a supplier, a tool, an authority or their staff, and admit only what's true (O11); if an outside service fails, say so plainly as a fact, then say what the owner controls. Where a slip cost the client's team time, fix the cause in the checklist, say what changed and offer a proportionate make-good (a redo, an extra session), the same for every client in the same case and logged, never cash or gifts to staff (M27). Within M27's order (rollover first, then refund without a fight), offer up to three concrete fixes and let them choose, or name something else. Within a week of any error that reaches a client's own customers, or any data incident, write a one-page review (facts only, every contributing cause, which step was assumed safe, the checklist change, who was told and when), with no personal data in it, read by someone not involved (F15). Before admitting anything that could become a claim, tell the insurer and take advice first (O42); a personal data breach also goes to the client (see X8, X5). Before any launch or one-day push, run a pre-mortem: list every choke point in order (ad accounts able to spend, emails and texts sending, every link working, checkout taking payment, phones staffed), test each a day ahead, and write the backup for each (a second ad platform, a spare payment route); rehearse the live part in the real setting, and staff the phones with people trained by role-play on each offer.
- L76 [writing any message, choosing what to send, or deciding how much to give before asking] Buyers sit on a line from needing little information to needing a lot; those who need little are few and everyone fights over them. Match each message to the reader's awareness: unaware (broad curiosity; any loss figure needs evidence first, O15), problem aware (name the pain), solution aware (why this kind of solution beats the alternatives), product aware (the offer's terms and proof), most aware (clients and replied prospects: just make the offer). Find where most of the avatar sits and lead there; where most are problem aware, cold messages lead with the pain, not the offer. Frame uptake figures as a cost to the prospect and a reason to act now, never as "most are waiting", because publicising inaction can spread it; test a version without the peer figure (L63). To reach the larger group who need more information, give more than you ask: at least 3.5 gives to 1 ask, where the gives for a service are outcomes the owner has achieved, outcomes for clients like them, and free tools that get them a result themselves (L32). Education pays slowly, so it runs alongside direct asks, never instead of them; each lead should get several useful touches before the ask. The bigger the plane, the longer the runway: the higher the price and the colder the reader, the more selling it takes, so a stall often means a warm-style message went to a cold reader. Each step out in awareness reaches a bigger audience with fewer buyers per hundred: the ratio falls, the total rises. If deals routinely need 2 to 4 calls, there wasn't enough education before the first call: fix the pre-call material, not the number of calls. On each call, check which buyer beliefs (O10) the pre-call material has already settled, and spend the call only on the rest. If replies come only from prospects already shopping and dry up as the list grows, widen the hooks to the problem aware and unaware; when results plateau, the usual fix is a hook good enough to reach the next, less aware group, not a new market (L3). At each L1 re-score, also check market sophistication: if rivals all make the same claim (check by sampling about 20 rival pages, M4), lead with the mechanism (O4), never a bigger number (O15, O22). Direct response and brand are one job: every message both asks for a response now and keeps the same promise. For lead magnet takers the owner may lawfully email, test a plain-text sequence of about 4 emails over a week: the true origin story (F21), then one C2 belief per email, each ending with what the next answers, the last asking for the call, each with the opt-out (see X2). Map the avatar's buying journey stage by stage, with the questions asked at each from L59, and put each asset at the stage it answers. Where demand peaks once or twice a year, run one theme across every channel (ads, emails, search, content) for a set period before, during and after each peak, so the business is in front of the avatar year-round; each theme names a true pain of that season.
- L79 [choosing or running in-person events as a lead source: trade shows, sponsored booths, speaking slots, or events hosted by existing clients in their own communities] Go where many of the avatar gather at once rather than chasing them one at a time: shows, conferences and member events for business buyers, and the clubs, groups and venues where higher-income consumers already spend time. Score each event before paying: how many of the avatar attend, its cost, and the gross profit of the clients it must win to pass M23. Bring one reason to stop (a short talk on the most common mistakes, a live demonstration, a free check) with a way to opt in on the spot (a QR code to the lead magnet, O14), collecting contact details and marketing consent lawfully (X2, X8), and staff it to book meetings, not to close at the stand. Contact every lead within a day (L72) and book from the conversation. An existing client who invites the business to their own club or community is the cheapest version: offer it to clients who got results, with their consent, and run it as a real event, not a disguised pitch (X9, X14 if the host is rewarded). Judge each event on booked calls, cash collected and gross profit per pound spent, and repeat only the ones that pass; footage and proof from events feed outreach and content with consent (X13). An event counts as the business's one channel or waits until the first target (L4, F3). For a service with a quick, valuable test or demonstration, run it at the event (free or low-priced), let people watch a short explainer video while it runs, and close the same day or book the close for the next day; once events work, the owner's own half-day workshop with a small paid ticket filters for serious buyers and puts a card on file. Keep each event that pays back in a permanent yearly calendar. To get into community events (residents' groups, clubs, associations), approach the organiser outside event time with nearby proof (clients in the same area who agree to be named or called, X13), ask for their event calendar, and at every event book the next one. At a client event, plan the renewal or upgrade offer: open strongly, greet each person by name with two prepared facts about them (split attendees among staff), teach with stories and frameworks, then a short stack and close; on day two, a brief re-pitch that answers the objections heard, while still teaching; a real perk for those who commit there (such as dinner with the owner or travel to the next event), stated the same for everyone. Speaking invitations while the business can't serve the demand it already has add nothing: decline, or charge a fee at least equal to the sales the days away would cost (daily sales value times days, plus travel), remembering the lost focus as well.
- L80 [the target business's social media accounts are run by an agency or staff, or using a social platform such as Instagram to reach small-business owners] Treat the business's own account as the wrong door when someone else runs it: a message there reaches the agency or staff, so use the platform to find the owner, never as the first contact. (1) Find the owner's name from the business's tagged posts, the team or about page of its website, the owner's replies to public reviews and the public business register that lists company officers (L9). (2) Reach them first on a route the agency doesn't control: a call asking for them by name, an email to their own work address (found on the website, or the company's usual address format checked before sending), or the website's contact form opening with their name. (3) Use their personal account only as warm-up so the name feels familiar when the call or email lands: follow and leave a real, specific comment on a recent post; any message is one short, personalised, text-only note with no link, since most platforms let a non-follower send only one message request, often into a folder that isn't checked. (4) Platforms don't publish messaging limits and judge accounts by behaviour, so even an established account raises its daily volume gradually, never sends identical messages, puts the free guide in a pinned post rather than a first message, logs every warning and pauses a day or two when one appears; count comments, first messages and replies together towards the Rule of 100 (L41). (5) Law: where the owner is an individual, sole trader or partnership in the UK, a message to their personal account is electronic marketing needing consent or the soft opt-in (X2, L56); work email and personal-data handling follow X2 and X8; in other countries check the local rules first (X1). (6) The agency itself can be a lead getter rather than a gatekeeper when the offer makes its clients' results better: approach it as a partner under L64 and L69, with any referral reward disclosed (X15). Judge each route on replies and booked calls per 30 targets and move volume to the best (L63).
