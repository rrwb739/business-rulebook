## Team and leadership (T), continued

- T2 [considering an agency, or paying any outside supplier] Pay for the output, not the hours: a supplier billing by the hour has a reason to bill more. Outsource a task only if the owner's freed hours, spent on higher-value work, beat the fee plus the cost of any drop in quality (checked on a sample), and not without money to spare. Before any supplier starts, agree one written page: who is involved, who does what, where, by when, how much, for how long, who signs off the work as good, and a proportionate remedy agreed up front if it isn't good or is late (X3); it's for clarity, not suing, so decide what the owner wants first and say it plainly, even to a friend. Keep ownership of the work and the data, keep someone on the owner's side who can judge the work, and never share revenue figures a variable-fee supplier could price against. Never give an open-ended revenue share: cap it with a written review date (affiliate payouts sit under L69). Give each supplier their own login with the least access needed, never the owner's password, removed the day they stop (L36); a supplier handling personal data needs a written contract and never sees client data (X5; see X8). Check employment status before paying any contractor regularly (see X18). Choosing: test skill before interviewing (every candidate gets the same sample task, criteria and deadline, scored on accuracy); for a specialty the owner can't judge, first interview 3 to 5 people who do that job about what good work looks like (F25); pick agencies on referrals, known clients, a waiting list, an honest sales process, long-term strategy, clear requirements, regular meetings, simple reporting and a strong offer, talk to 5 to 10 first, and ask those not picked to refer a good one; ask referees to rank accuracy, speed, communication and reliability and say why; treat hand-picked testimonials as unverified until checked with a client the owner finds; before acting on advice, ask whether they are truly expert on this exact topic and what they gain. When someone cites business results to sell advice, a course or a partnership, ask which figure it is (contract value or cash collected, one month scaled up or a full year, lifetime or yearly, revenue or profit, a sale multiple on what) and weigh most the yearly cash the owner can take out after reinvesting; use the same plain measures in the business's own claims (O47). Check whether the fee rewards what the owner wants (a flat percentage on a sale rewards a fast close more than a higher price); where it doesn't, tier it: little or nothing below a floor the owner sets, and a larger share of each step above it. For lawyers, accountants and other professional advisers, judge intent and skill separately and prefer clearly good intent with strong skill over top skill with doubtful intent; before choosing, read two or three serious books on the topic so the owner can follow the advice and test it against what the other candidates said. When hiring an assistant, screen for reply speed and intelligence and pay above the going rate (T1). Give no exclusivity until a supplier has beaten an alternative on a live batch. For recurring work, agree a written standard, keep a scorecard from day one and give feedback within 2 working days; if a supplier won't meet it after one written request, switch, using the L36 backup and checking notice terms first. Agencies: use them to learn a method or platform, not forever, and name the senior person in the contract. The cheapest route is paying an expert by the hour to watch the owner do the work. Otherwise say up front the work will move in-house, pay for every decision to be explained, set a deadline (about 6 months), run the owner's team alongside until it wins, then drop to cheaper consulting, and end it once the owner is teaching them. Deciding: judge a reduced proposal fresh against the need, budget and payback written beforehand (M19), never against the first number; walk away from a last-minute take-it-or-leave-it demand rather than accept because of time spent; above a spend threshold the owner sets, read the proposal in a distraction-free block and get a view from someone who didn't see the earlier offers (F15); value extras at what they'd cost alone and compare totals in writing. Decline gifts or favours that would create a sense of owing and log any accepted; treat a free trial or "quick call" as the first step of a ladder and write down the bigger ask it leads to; repay favours in proportion, and never turn a small favour into a much bigger ask (M27). If a pitch makes the owner feel trapped by something said earlier, stop and decide later on the F2 test; if unusually charmed, decide only on the written offer, evidence and references (F25); anyone who keeps coming back for small concessions is a red flag. When work slips, put it in writing (thanks for one specific thing, then the specific extra item with its time and pay stated); if they fail outright, go to the decision maker, frame the fault as a risk to their reputation and ask for the fix, never shouting. Praise helpful support staff to their manager when sincere. After a bad supplier or course, write what was learned before judging it. Pay suppliers on time, every time. Before relying on a supplier for anything a launch or the core service depends on: get references from clients like the owner, at the owner's size and use case, and call them; a referral from a friend never skips the checks, so say plainly that the call will be treated as if the owner doesn't know them; ask for a test at the expected load before the date; and treat any doubt from the supplier's own staff as a reason to check again or switch, since bad news before a launch is cheaper than after. Never outsource the core of what the business sells (getting clients or delivering the result) to a supplier no one on the owner's side can judge (T3): where software is built outside, the business owns the code and accounts, the contract requires documentation and a handover, and fee rises are capped and agreed in writing. To learn a channel through agencies: start with a basic agency whose main job is to make the owner publish on a fixed cadence; once the owner knows what the audience responds to, hire an advanced agency on stated terms from the first conversation: a set period (about six months), paying extra for them to explain each decision and the steps behind it, co-creating while the owner documents their process (D5) and trains the team, then a lower-cost consulting agreement, ended once calls teach nothing new. Set the in-house date at the start; if the team can't match the agency by then, either the people or the teaching is the problem. Watch for the usual decline: results after the senior person, then a junior takes over and results slip; the named senior person in the contract is the guard. With no money, learn the channel by doing it. Use specialist partners for what they have done for decades rather than learning it, held to written specifications, regular audits (for safety-critical goods, before the first order) and quarterly business reviews that name problems so they are fixed rather than the relationship ended; own in house only the input whose loss would stop all revenue and that no partner can guarantee. Key supplier risk: where one supplier controls a function the business can't trade without (making the product, building the software, running ads, payment processing), keep a qualified backup, own the code, data, designs and accounts, keep getting leads, selling and delivery in-house where possible, and agree in writing a notice period before they stop (about 6 to 12 months for core work), a proportionate exit charge, and service levels with set remedies for missed dates. Treat repeated requests for earlier or larger prepayments as a warning: ask for the goods already paid for before paying more. The bigger a share of the supplier's business the owner becomes, the more leverage the owner has; where a key supplier is excellent and small, consider bringing them in-house by hire or acquisition (T3). Ask an agency for its best practices (funnel, offer, speed to call leads, call script, show-up steps); one that can't share them is a red flag. Agree a short results window before any longer commitment, and keep speaking to experts in the field until calls teach nothing new before choosing. For sporadic or seasonal work (a launch, one-off tasks the owner keeps doing), keep a bench of vetted contractors switched on and off as needed, with one person checking quality, rather than hiring full-time; trade the owner's hours for their few hours task by task. A software business needs in-house technical leadership: don't build the core product through an outside development shop, just as an agency shouldn't white-label all its delivery.
- T3 [considering a partner or a first hire] [stage: S3+] Consider a partner or first hire only when the roadmap stage allows it (after the first target, F32), never before. A partner must bring money, experience or time the owner lacks, written down and measurable before any equity moves; never partner out of fear of going alone. Before any equity moves, agree in writing, with a solicitor, what happens on exit, death, disagreements about money and a sale. Map the three roles every business needs (acquisition: marketing and sales; product: delivery; operations: finance, legal, HR, IT) and give each partner the role they will own; if two partners would own the same role, one of them isn't needed as a partner. List every activity in the business (if unsure, each partner logs what they do every 15 minutes for a week) and assign each to one named owner, since shared ownership means no one owns it. Write down working expectations too: hours, weekends, holidays and response times. Treat the negotiation of the agreement as a test of how the other person behaves under disagreement. To make a partner or senior operator act like an owner, first consider a written share of the profit they control (with an optional small, symbolic buy-in to show commitment) before any equity: it is faster, simpler to unwind and gets most of the same behaviour; move equity only where the measurable value justifies it, with advice first (see X17, X18, X19). If the product's core is technical work the owner cannot judge, the first partner or senior hire should be someone who can, with a real stake in the quality of who is hired; until then, have a trusted outside expert interview or test candidates. Split equity by each person's measured contribution of time, money and skill, never equally by headcount; equal skills and equal contributions mean one of the two isn't needed. Have the split conversation on day one, before any work: if a partner balks at a fair split then, that is the answer. Whoever brings in the clients and makes the money gets a share in line with that, because the business can't run without them. A function the business needs (accounts, legal, IT, admin) is hired or bought from a supplier (T2), never paid in equity; equity goes only to people core to getting clients or delivering the result (one making the product exceptional and one telling the world about it is a sound split). A partner who works in the business is paid a salary for the job, separately from what they own as a partner. Never sell a share for less than its value as a growing business (advice first, X17). Red flags in a partner mean stop, believed the first time: a fraud or insolvency history (check public records), asking the owner to personally guarantee their debts or leases, to front all the money, or to do all the work for half, and the pitch "you're leaving money on the table". Never personally guarantee a partner's obligations without advice (X17); keep shared money in an account that needs both signatures for any large withdrawal, with books both can see. Hiring follows T1. Before any partnership, investment, joint deal or equity arrangement, run four checks, the four goods: good people (trust but verify, a mutual background check offered both ways, references the owner finds themselves), good intentions (ask what happens if everything goes wrong, and for a past example of how they behaved when it did), good rationale (the deal in a spreadsheet the owner understands, with the downside case), and good contracts (written, advised, enforceable where the other side can actually be found and held to it). A partner who resists any check or rushes the deal is a red flag. Understand the structure before signing: in any equity, option, sale or investment, terms such as preferences, ratchets, vesting and dilution can change what the owner receives many times over, so read them with a qualified adviser (X20). Where the owner has skill or time but no capital, offering to do the work on someone else's deals for a written share of each one (sweat equity) is a lawful way in, agreed in writing before work starts (X18, X20). Trust means giving someone something they could use against you (information, money, your word or theirs relied on); before giving more, check two things: their record of protecting what they were given before, and whether betraying the owner would cost them more than protecting the owner. Grow trust in small steps, and know that one betrayal usually ends it whatever came before. Bring in a partner rather than hire only when the person is a "barrel" (someone who can create results from nothing at the owner's own level), their skill and experience would take years to build or can't be hired, and the owner would gladly spend time with them outside work; years of working together informally first is the best test. When values and expectations are aligned beforehand the terms are quick to agree, so a fight over final terms signals a deeper mismatch to resolve before signing. Equity carries four things: risk, control, profit and proceeds on sale; staff and junior operators usually want only the profit, so give a profit-share bonus rather than ownership. A partner who also works in the business is paid the market rate for that role before profits are split, so the unit's real profit is known before it is copied. When partners' goals diverge, raise it early, say plainly what was agreed and not done and ask what they think is fair, and choose a clean exit (with any shared debt split in a short signed note stating the amount, payment dates and what happens on default, X20): a shotgun offer (one names a price and terms at which either side buys the other), an equity swap where partners co-own several units (each takes full ownership of some), or a buyout tied to helping recruit a replacement; take advice on tax and contract terms (X19, X20). Never give equity for one-off work, especially at the very start when the business is valued at almost nothing; pay for it or give a capped fee. Where staff hold shares in a business that won't be sold or listed, plan how they can sell: a regular buyback from profit at an independent valuation, or an auction where holders say what price they would sell at, set up with an adviser (X19, X20). When an owner lists many problems at once, the real gap is usually two or three missing senior people. In a profitable but loose business, a strong first senior hire is often a finance lead asked to find savings worth their own salary within about 90 days, which then funds the next hire. When one partner pushes to expand and the other holds back, the cautious partner writes the conditions they would need to see met before the next step (a checklist of numbers and readiness), both agree it, and expansion goes ahead each time the list is met; the same list is reused for the next unit. When partners disagree on a decision, don't move forward until they agree, unless the matter is truly urgent; start by asking each other what information each is using, since one usually holds data the other lacks. A partner must bring knowledge, money or time the owner lacks; if both bring the same, one isn't needed, and a need that can be paid for (an accountant, a developer) is paid, not given equity. Check that both want the same destination, the same way of getting there and compatible personal lives and money habits, since a partner's life spills into the business; test it by giving both the same data and seeing whether you reach the same decision. Give the partner a fair share from the start rather than squeezing them, since a lopsided deal gets renegotiated once reality sets in. For a key employee whose contribution is hard to measure (a senior leader rather than a marketer or seller, who should get performance pay), consider phantom equity instead of real shares: a written right to a stated share of the proceeds if the business is sold (and, only if chosen, of profit), vesting over several years after a first-year cliff and lapsing if they leave before it vests, with no vote, no ownership and no liability; keep each grant proportional to the role (well under the owner's stake, typically a small single-digit percentage for senior leaders), say whether a sale buyer can require them to stay, and take tax and legal advice before offering it, since payouts are normally taxed as employment income (X19, X20).
