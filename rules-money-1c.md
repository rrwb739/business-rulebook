## Money models (M), continued (M9 to M18)

- M9 [designing or running continuity] Find the months when most members leave (check the early months first), make the first period excellent and plan a reason to stay past each of those points. Aim for net revenue retention above 100%. Don't scale outreach while members are leaving: fix the leak first. Log every cancellation by reason: structural churn (the client closed, merged, sold or retired) is fixed by avatar choice (L2), not delivery; tag the rest as didn't use it (fix onboarding and engagement, or offer a lighter tier, M39) or used it but not good enough (fix the product, L65). Tag "overwhelmed" as its own reason and answer it by removing or replacing material, never by adding sessions or templates; in the core offer too, if a client falls behind, cut its task list before adding support. Compare tenure by client segment and cut the segment that leaves first. If members average under two billing cycles, treat it as a fit problem first (F12 red flags), not a content problem. Track take-up and renewals by delivery batch, not in aggregate, and track both logo retention (share of members still paying after 12 months) and revenue retention (last year's members' revenue now divided by their revenue then); set the retention target from the business's own data. Build legitimate stickiness through integration (what is built lives in the client's own systems and is updated each cycle), but never hold back their work or data when they leave (X5, M27): they keep everything built. Results and experience are separate: after each session and at each cycle, ask one 1 to 10 question ("how easy was working with us this week?") alongside the D8 counts, log reply times, and act on low scores before renewal; a good experience never excuses missing a guaranteed result (O8, M27). At each cycle, tell each member one specific, true thing it did well, from its D8 counts. At each cycle check-in, ask the client's lead contact how the period went, what went well and what to improve, then show each D8 count against their own day-1 baseline and ask "what do you see?" before giving the owner's view. After the first paid batch, send early clients, clients and members a yearly one-page letter in the owner's voice: consented, labelled results in counts (L32), what the owner got wrong and fixed (L75), what changes next and the original promise restated, with no other client's details (X5, M12) and an opt-out for anyone not a current client (see X2). Offer a short call to everyone who cancels or asks for a refund, to learn what went wrong, never to block or delay the cancellation (it goes through as asked, X3): let them say it all, never minimise it, agree plainly where the business fell short, then ask "what would it take to make this right?"; log the reason and the fix, and tell them what changed (D9). Members who feel overwhelmed cancel: keep continuity to the fewest things that deliver the main reason they joined, each worth the price on its own, and cut or merge what few members use rather than adding more. When clients who start using the offer stay but many never start, the fix is mostly who is sold: compare those who stuck with those who didn't on three things, who they are, measurable facts about them (size, budget, current results) and what they did in the first weeks; sell only to the profile that sticks, even if acquisition cost rises, and build onboarding around the early actions the stickers took (O26). Each month count joins against cancels and label the month growing, flat or declining. Average lifetime in months is roughly 1 divided by monthly churn, so halving churn doubles lifetime value; small churn changes are big. Churn falls with tenure, so manage milestones rather than the average: a plan to get every member through month one (activation, expectations set in the sale, a fast first win pinned up front, ideally inside the first day), an outcome or behaviour change by about day 90, and a connection with other members by about month six; a programme younger than these milestones will show inflated churn, so don't overhaul it on early numbers alone. Ask every canceller why in a short message, tag the reason (price, overwhelm, not using, missing feature, not what was expected), and every 20 or so cancels fix the top one or two. Answer price complaints with a lower tier only when ideal members raise them; when non-ideal members do, the price is filtering as intended. Find what matters by asking members which one thing they would fight to keep and which one they wouldn't miss. Give busy members a short weekly path to keep up and keen members optional depth. The owner or a named person shows up daily: reads complaints, answers messages, thanks good behaviour and removes members who break the published rules. Introduce each new member to a few long-standing regulars with something in common, and recognise about ten model members publicly and invite them to contribute. Judge churn against who the business serves: very small businesses and part-time buyers come and go whatever the quality, so expect higher churn there and lower with established businesses, and compare with the business's own earlier cohorts rather than one average. The target that matters most is a milestone (often month 12) after which retained clients almost never leave; track the share who reach it and their churn afterwards, and treat the period before it as part of selling (M23, the cost of a permanent client). Keep each continuity feature only if it alone would justify the price several times over; if not, delete it, even if that leaves one or two features. If clients leave because they don't see the value, show it for them: get access to the numbers that prove the result and walk them through a scorecard at a set meeting, rather than relying on clients to fill in reports themselves. If clients leave because they don't use what they have bought (it piles up unused), content that helps them use it (recipes, how-to sessions, plans) is a retention tool; test making much of it public too, since it also reaches new buyers and few members log in to gated content anyway. Restate each client's goal at onboarding and at every check-in, update it when it is met, and frame renewal as the next step to that goal. Find where renewals and upgrades have happened most in the past (often live events or reviews) and run those occasions on a schedule (for example quarterly) rather than by chance. Where upgrades need a sales conversation, have client success hand clients who reach an ascension point (M31) to a closer with a proper introduction, paying client success for each accepted handover and the closer on the sale; without a sales step, avoid paying commission on recurring revenue nobody actively earns. Never let the sales team handle unhappy clients: route them to a separate team or person whose job is to keep them, paid a share (about 10 to 15%) of the revenue each saved client keeps paying, and script and rehearse the few conversations that make up most of their calls; a client who asks to cancel is still let go as easily as they joined, with no delay or pressure (X rules on subscriptions and unfair practices), so the pay rewards fixing problems, never blocking exits. Contact an unhappy client more as days pass without a reply (once on day one, twice on day two, more after, across channels), the reverse of lead follow-up, since the problem grows while they wait. Match routine contact to the client: low-ticket buyers usually welcome frequent contact, high-paying ones often just want it to work. When a client uses only a small part of what they pay for, offer the cheaper level that covers what they use before they cancel; it often keeps them far longer and earns referrals. When a client wants to bring a service in-house, offer a consulting stage instead of losing them: help train their team at a lower fee (often a quarter to a third of the previous one) until their team beats yours, which keeps the relationship and much of the value. When churn is the constraint, find the activation point from cohort data and put the effort there: pause growth pushes and new features, take in only enough new clients to test cohorts, and show each client only the one next thing to do until it is done; if churn doesn't fall, the activation point is wrong. When results must be proved to a client's senior people, make the data needed a written condition of working (lawfully, X5, X8) and give the client the steps that make sharing it easy.Falling use is the earliest warning of a cancellation: track each client's use against their own normal (sessions attended, logins, actions done) and contact them at the first drop, escalating rather than tapering when they don't reply (once the first day, then more often), the reverse of lead follow-up, so they know someone noticed. Give each team member a fixed group of clients to check in with personally every two to three weeks about how they are, not to sell; systematised, never automated, since a message that reads as automated shows no one cares. Add unlockable bonuses by tenure, set just before the month most members cancel and spaced further apart as tenure grows, and by actions the business wants (activation, an upgrade, a referral within L66), never by a review or testimonial (X12, X13). At member events, invite each member to bring a friend, with any offer to guests stated the same for all. Start with what is cheapest for the return: onboarding, then unlockables, then exit interviews, then use tracking, then personal check-ins, then events. Name the activation point (the early event after which clients usually stay, such as a first result or a first action the business can see) and measure days from sale to reaching it; aim every early contact at it. Check-ins open with what most clients face at that stage ("most people at this point are stuck on A or B; which is it for you?") and, where possible, do the step with the client on the call, rather than asking an open "how's it going?". Lagging numbers such as churn are fixed through leading ones (logins, attendance, actions taken), tracked per client. Satisfaction scores: segment them by staff member, and allow for their biases (replies skew to the very happy and the very unhappy, and a survey sent right after onboarding reads high); judge retention and activation first. Stickiness levers to check: clients actually using what they pay for; switching costs made of benefits they would lose (never of friction or held data, X3, X8); having no real alternative (a unique method, protected know-how); handling the client's payment flow where that serves them; a cause they identify with; a community; a fixed term agreed upfront (X3); and regular contact with something coming up to look forward to, since cancellations tend to fall before an event and rise after it, so keep the next one always announced.
- M10 [the core offer's demand depends on something that will fade] When demand for the core offer rests on a deadline, wave or trend that will fade, plan the next promotion and ascension offer before it fades (a window opened by a new technology that is still hard to use well counts: the difficulty is the advantage, so move while it is hard and plan for when it becomes easy for everyone), and start building that offer and its proof during the current stage, not when demand drops. List the client needs that will still hold in 10 years and put D15 product hours and the next offer only into those; score the next offer and any idea-list candidate (F22) on whether clients would love it, it can grow large, it returns the hours and money (M19), it lasts, the need is underserved and the business can make it clearly different (O2): size alone is never enough. When results jump, check whether a wave in the market is carrying them before crediting the method (rising demand across the category, rivals growing too, a cost shift pushing buyers towards this kind of offer): if so, push hard while it lasts (more outreach, more spend within M20, more capacity) and plan the next offer for when it fades; never read a market-driven jump as proof the owner's method will work elsewhere, and never read a market-driven drop as proof it failed.
- M11 [a client has just bought] Offer zero-delivery-cost add-ons that recover acquisition cost. After a one-off high-price result the client can see, offer a yearly care plan of real upkeep (checks, maintenance, priority fixes) priced as a small share of the purchase, presented while the result is in front of them; every claim about how much longer the result lasts must be true, and any promise to pay for repairs or replacements is checked against the insurance perimeter first (X11).
- M12 [planning the year for continuity members] Run a short, defined extra programme for existing members about once per billing cycle, around their point of greatest need. After the first target: make one of them free with "bring another business", genuinely free (see X3), the guest introduced three-way with consent (L66), and the add-on offered at a second meeting. Later, after the first target, test events: a periodic members' meetup as a bonus; a small breakfast where consenting paying clients and invited prospects sit together, prospects told plainly it is a sales event, with no scripted testimonials; opt-in introductions between members who don't compete for the same customers; a paid in-person workshop day before a peak, capped at the real room size (O36), filmed only with every attendee's consent. Hospitality stays modest, the same for everyone and never conditional (see X16). Where members may compete, never share prices, customer lists or customer details between them, and share anyone's details only with consent (see X20, X8). Always announce the next event at the current one, and consider making events a reward for members who stay 6 to 12 months. Upsells at events go to qualified people in the room; judge conversion on them, not the whole room. At events only the owner sells, a warm room needs a short pitch, and it goes just before a break; test it against just after a modest meal. In any members' space, pin one line on what members are asked to share and thank each who does personally; test a challenge on members' own results, with no league table of prices or customer data and any prize run under X3. At any event, webinar or members' space: no planted attendees, no pre-arranged questions or testimonials presented as spontaneous, no friends posing as buyers, and a recording never pretends to be live; a live-only bonus is fine if true and stated in advance (C2) (see X9).
- M13 [a client asks for something outside the core offer] Stay productised: the same packaged steps for every client, no custom work. Turn repeated requests into package features only if it helps most clients and removes more effort than it adds (O21); otherwise keep it out or make it a separate template. Repeated out-of-scope requests from one client are a fit flag (F12). When declining a client's idea, say what's genuinely good in it, explain why it doesn't fit (most clients, the guarantee conditions), and log it on the D9 list. Stay narrow because most buyers don't yet know what they need; wide choice is only for buyers who already know (members picking from a bonus library). Never answer a client with "that's not how it works" alone: if a fixed step would hurt its result or its customers this week, do what's right, log it, and decide at the next D15 review whether the checklist changes for everyone; [Fixed] rules are never bent this way.
- M14 [opening a new delivery batch or offer] Pre-sell: fill the batch before delivery starts, and never take money for something the business won't deliver.
- M15 [a full delivery batch is running] One full batch must make enough before adding offers or batches. If it doesn't, raise the price. Before opening a second location or unit, write down the cost to open, the time until it makes a profit, when it will have paid for itself, the launch plan and how it will be staffed and trained, and fix the first unit's margins first, since the fastest route to two locations is not the fastest to many; soft open and run smoothly before any grand opening. When several units each make little profit, consider merging a weak one into a stronger one (members moved onto one fixed overhead, timed to lease ends) and get one unit to strong margins, running without the owner, before any further unit, licence or franchise: buyers and franchisees judge the unit's profit and return on capital, and most small franchise systems stall because their units earn too little. In a small-town market, expect lower costs and less competition but leads that fade after the first wave, so retention and word of mouth matter more; once the model works there, expand to similar small towns before a competitive city.
- M16 [designing an upsell] [stage: S3+] Price an upsell at about 5x the core price, not before the first target (F3). Rollover upsells must be at least 4x the credit (M35); anchors sit at 5 to 10x (M33).
- M17 [leads feel too expensive or slow] The usual fix is higher lifetime value (continuity, price rises), not cheaper leads.
- M18 [considering affiliates or partners] Partners who sell the offer (affiliates): not before the first target, then peel off one part of the offer for them to sell at or above a minimum price. Partner bonuses (O41) are different: allowed from the first paid batch, if each takes under 2 hours to set up. Every referral commission partners pay the business is extra profit on clients already paid for. Prefer offers the business owns and can add a back end to over a better-looking split on someone else's product: any joint venture or white-label must leave the business free to sell its own next offer to the client.
