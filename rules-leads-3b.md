## Leads and marketing (L), continued

### Content
- L58 [making any content, including sales tools and the lead magnet] A content unit hooks, retains and rewards. Hook with the topic (best from the owner's own experience: far past, recent past, present, trending, or manufactured), the headline (use at least two of: recency, relevance, celebrity, proximity, conflict, unusual, ongoing) and the platform's best-performing format. Retain with curiosity: lists, steps and stories, nested; state the number of list items up front. Keep a bank of retention devices and use one wherever attention could drop: show the result first, then each step that got there; name two options the avatar already weighs and reveal the real answer is a third; state a belief the avatar holds, then show why it is wrong; rehook mid-piece with a better, then the best, way; and flag the one condition without which the method fails, then explain it. Each device must be true: the options and belief are ones the avatar really holds, the better way is really better and the condition is real (X9), and devices are judged by where viewers drop off, never assumed to work (L60, L63). Every video carries accurate on-screen captions and must still make sense with the sound off, since much short video is watched muted; check each piece once on mute before posting. Reward by fully delivering what the hook promised: nothing is too long, only too boring. Make every piece for strangers, as if they've never heard of the owner: in every piece say who the owner is and why to listen (one true proof line, F21), title it so a stranger can tell the speaker's role without knowing the name, explain every reference to other pieces, and leave out inside jokes. Say "how I" and "my favourite ways", not "how to" and "the best way": no one can dispute the owner's experience. Mine calls and meetings for stories and keep notes and recordings; capture ideas the moment they come and make more of whatever does well. Documenting is cheaper and more sustainable than manufacturing; never teach what the owner hasn't done: until pilots prove it, document ("this is what I'm doing now") rather than educate. Use the strongest true proof available: clients like the viewer the owner has helped (consented, L32, X13), then the owner doing the thing the viewer wants, then the owner doing work the viewer won't (state the true time, money or attempts spent, so the piece offers a saving of time), then a factual breakdown of a named public figure's published, checkable results, never implying their endorsement (O25, X9). Long content is short units linked together, so start short. The audience decides: if it isn't bringing opt-ins, booked calls and sales from the avatar, the content isn't good enough yet. Wider list titles work when every item is a step of the core offer's method (O19). Humour is optional: entertainment widens reach, education changes behaviour, and humour is the first thing to cut. Viewers of a style or topic mostly want more of the same, so never assume entertainment or off-topic viewers move on to the business's teaching or buy; check it with the L60 per-post numbers before making that kind of piece a habit. Titles and thumbnails say plainly what the piece delivers, so the right viewer knows it is for them; curiosity goes into how, never into what the piece is about (headlines for messages and emails follow L73). Production serves understanding: words on screen, and charts or diagrams that show scale or change over time, stay; effects, changing backgrounds and decoration that help no one learn are cut. Periodically repost or re-record the owner's own best pieces from the past year where the platform's rules allow (some treat re-uploads as duplicate or reused content), since newer followers haven't seen them, and judge each repost like any post. A repost or re-record must earn its place by being better or meaningfully changed (a new hook, edit or angle); never make cosmetic changes (speed, music, caption, watermark) only to slip past a platform's duplicate or originality detection, and never post the same piece many times a day hoping one copy escapes it. A short, recognisable sign-on after the hook, with small variations, is an option to test (L63), never a warm-up before the hook (L73). To make a framework: collect many real examples (best pieces, best clients, best calls), list everything each one did, keep only what all of them share, and give the few shared items a short, memorable name.
- L59 [using content alongside cold outreach] Keep a master list of the best content and tools, labelled by the problem each solves and the benefit it gives, and send the right piece before or after calls to handle common concerns. When sending a case study or testimonial, point to the exact part that answers their concern (a timestamp, or a screenshot with that line highlighted), only from consented, true proof (M45, L33; see X13). People need reminding more than teaching: repeat the message in new formats (written, video, a one-page sheet), new contexts (different client types) and new stories from what the owner actually did that week. Log every question from replies and calls: each is the next piece of content, an FAQ line or a line in the pre-call video. Keep the claims file's blocks (O47) as reusable modules and build every message, page, video segment and letter from them, so the wording stays consistent and true everywhere (D15). After about 100 logged questions, group them and name sessions and playbook chapters from the top 6 to 10, in the avatar's words. After every 10 calls or replies, write one page on the challenges that came up repeatedly (their words), how they reacted to the positioning line and which prospects leaned in, feeding O5, F12 and L3. Once a month, scan sent replies for answers written 3 or more times and turn each into a pre-call video, FAQ, C14 or playbook line checked against O47. Keep a rough grid of avatar type against stage (problem aware, weighing options, choosing), with one asset and one next step per cell.
- L60 [if content becomes a channel after the first target] [stage: S3+] Give, give, give until they ask: keep the give-to-ask ratio well above 3:1, give in public and ask in private. Once paid ads run, count them as asks in the ratio across everything a viewer sees and lean organic content further towards gives; a pointer to something free counts as a give. In short pieces, keep the ask out of the video or in its last seconds, and put it in the description, the first comment and the profile; the profile tells a stranger in one line who the owner is and why to listen (F21, L58), with one link or keyword. Asks offer what more content can't: the personalised version (a review or plan for the viewer's own situation), since more of the same is free everywhere. A comment or message keyword may trigger an automated reply through the platform's own permitted tools, sending only what was asked for, with at most one follow-up; contacts join email or text marketing only with consent (X2, X8). Test ideas in the cheapest format first (short text posts), then promote winners up the formats: a short video read from it, clips, then a long piece built from several winners (L59, L63). Decide on purpose whether the owner is the face: public recognition is hard to undo, and an owner who doesn't want it can keep content brand-led or rely on the Core Four methods that already work (L40). The audience, not the content, is the compounding asset. Asks are integrated (a short plug inside content, after a valuable moment or at the end, added one at a time while checking growth doesn't slow; best for long form) or intermittent (an ask after every 10 give posts; best for short form); point to the lead magnet if unsure, and set an ask cadence alongside a fixed posting cadence. The first post may include an ask; if it gets nothing, give for a while, then ask again. Content is harder to personalise, faces more competition and gets copied, so keep innovating. Batch record, and post manually rather than scheduling. Scale depth then width (master one platform, then add the next), or width then depth if the business is bigger. Start narrow (one small audience and topic) before wider topics; short pieces may cover adjacent topics, long pieces stay on the core problem. Before making a piece, name its job: reach (new strangers, the awareness value posts below) or conversion (opt-ins, calls, sales), and estimate how many people could find the idea relevant at all. To raise a reach piece's ceiling, widen one of three things in the hook: the outcome (a broader stated result instead of a narrow metric, still true and evidenced, O15; see X9), the avatar frame (one level up the label, so the avatar is still inside it, L73), or the words (terms more people already know, L11). Each widening trades conversion rate for audience size, so conversion pieces stay narrow (O3, O12), and a reach piece never widens past the market that holds the avatar (L2); judge both kinds by the avatar results below, not views. Judge content on opt-ins, booked calls and sales from the avatar, never likes, views or followers; use a platform's ranking signals (watch time, sends, likes per reach, or whatever the platform's own published statements name, dated in the claims file, O47; never a creator's ranking or benchmarks), the share of viewers who leave in the first 3 seconds (the hook check, L73) and followers gained only to diagnose why a post didn't reach the avatar; where a platform pays per view, revenue per 1,000 views (on YouTube, RPM) is a fast check on audience quality, also a diagnostic only. Weigh comments by who writes them: praise from outside the avatar doesn't count (F14), so ask avatar members directly whether recent pieces still help them. For each post, track attributable opt-ins and sales per 1,000 views, using a post-specific comment keyword or link so each lead traces to its post (L14); that is the conversion number content is judged on. Hashtags are labels for search, never a reach tactic: use a few that describe the piece exactly, within the platform's current limit, and spend no testing time on them. Once content is a channel: leave the most useful comment on posts the avatar reads, giving value first (F15) and within each platform's self-promotion rules; pair short videos with long captions that carry the teaching, and use short clips to feed long pieces, more often across platforms than within one, checked with the per-post numbers by format before relying on it, since short and long viewers are often different audiences; re-score platforms for the avatar; run content on a fixed weekly slot with a named producer and a checklist, never willpower; before blaming the platform, the niche or the content, count last week's posts on each platform the business uses and those of the leading accounts in its space, since slow growth is most often too few posts; then raise volume on the main platform by one post a day for 30 days, only at a pace that can be kept up for months, with every extra post still meeting the L58 standard, and judge the change on the avatar numbers below, never reach; if good ideas run out before time does, the constraint is ideas, so feed the idea list from the O5 phrase bank, the O10 objection list and the L59 log rather than lowering the bar; the cheapest way to keep that cadence is a default format: one everyday part of the work the avatar wants to see (making, packing, fulfilling, a day in the job), filmed fresh each time to one fixed script structure, opened with a hook type already proven in the niche (borrowed as a structure, never wording, L73), and run as a series; the footage is new every time (never the same clip reposted, see L58), only one element changes between runs (L63), and the series stays only while its avatar numbers hold; any outside writer works only from the claims file (O47, T2); pages target the avatar's own narrow phrases from the O5 phrase bank and the L59 log; earn links and shares honestly (a guest article after months of reading, never implying endorsement, O25; never bought links or keyword stuffing; anything given in return labelled as an ad, see X14); borrow hook structures, never wording, from rivals' outlier posts (at least 4 times their usual views) (W1); keep a mix of four post types (awareness value, conversion value, social proof, connection); film 3 hooks for each video, post all 3 for 24 hours and keep the best; where a platform offers a test feature that shows a piece to non-followers before followers, use it for hooks, new topics and formats (the cheapest test, L63), compare each test with the account's own earlier tests rather than with ordinary posts, judge it on the avatar numbers below, and promote a winner with the platform's own share option rather than uploading a second copy; run a wording audit of the account's own pieces, with a story-audit skill or tool that reads their transcripts and performance data, or by an outsider or a fresh AI chat working from transcripts (the owner is too close to their own words): every working day, check whether at least 3 pieces have settled (14 or more days old) since the last audit, and if so rerun it on one topic and platform; hold out and name every piece under 14 days old; split winners and losers by each piece's result against the account's own average, never raw views, top and bottom third, with at least 3 in each or a plain statement that the sample is too thin; compare the hook, the first 45 words, any escalation beat, where the payoff line sits and the call to action in seven passes (frame verb, such as turning something the viewer has into something better against giving them a new task; the noun the subject is turned into; how early a concrete, nameable object appears; whether the escalation adds a new capability or only a bigger adjective; whether the payoff line comes after the demonstration or before it; word count of the opening sentences; exact figures against vague quantifiers), each stated as hit counts per bucket; look first for controlled pairs (two pieces alike on most passes but far apart in result), and treat near-identical pieces far apart in result as distribution, not wording; try to kill every finding (one outlier carrying a bucket, topic or timing differences between buckets) and report only a pattern holding in at least 80% of both buckets, saying plainly when nothing survives; save every run to the business's findings log beside the claims file (O47): date, pieces, holdouts, the finding with its counts, what showed no signal, confidence, the next test and its status (hypothesis, testing, confirmed or killed); each finding stays a hypothesis until a one-variable test (L63) with readable samples (L43) confirms it on avatar opt-ins, not views, and only confirmed findings change hooks or scripts; end every value post with the lead magnet, which itself ends with the core offer's call to action (L44, L47); and pick formats from a proven bank, skipping any that don't fit the avatar. Posts meant to win new prospects aim for sends (something they'd forward because it surprises them, always true and backed by the claims file, O47, never fear or shock for its own sake, O11); posts for existing followers aim for likes. A movement (an attractive character, a future-based cause, a new opportunity), interviews with partner prospects, and recorded F25 interviews cut into one long and several short pieces are options, judged like any content. The audience needs reminding more than teaching: the owner tires of a message long before the avatar has noticed it, so keep repeating the few core messages that resonated, in new forms, rather than chasing novelty; someone not buying now will think of the business first when they are ready. Choosing and re-ranking platforms: score each platform for the avatar on five things, with the evidence written down: who is there (the share of the avatar, from published survey data dated in the claims file), how many of them, how easily a new account is discovered, depth (time spent per piece), and how easily a viewer can act (messages, stories, links, keyword replies); then add the account's own results per platform: share of site visits or leads against share of followers, time on the site from each platform, and leads and sales per 1,000 followers. Put time where avatar results per hour are highest and re-rank each quarter from the data, cutting platforms that earn least per hour even if they have the most followers. Make content for each platform's own format, since what works on one rarely transfers unchanged. Give in public and sell in private: the public post gives, and the offer happens in messages or on a call. Never buy followers, likes or views, or build an audience outside the avatar to look established: platforms show new posts first to existing followers, so a bought or irrelevant audience suppresses reach to the real avatar (F14). An owned list (email, L44, X2) is where content viewers convert best: move followers onto it and send to it regularly with value. A marketplace where buyers already trust the platform (a book or product listing) can be a discovery channel: a genuinely good product there, with a lawful pointer inside it to the owner's free resource, brings in buyers who would never have found the owner (X14 where commissions apply). Pick topics from the buyers who pay most: list the top 20% of clients by money spent, their common traits and the problems and words they came with, and make pieces that solve those problems; expect advanced pieces for them to get fewer views and more revenue, and rank every piece each quarter by the revenue its tracked link or keyword produced, never by views, since platforms show what most people like, not what the most valuable people like. Broad reach pieces must still give real value to the top buyers as well as beginners, so the wide net still holds the avatar. Longer formats and live sessions give far more time with each prospect than short clips, so they build more trust per viewer; use short clips mainly to get the owner's face recognised and to feed retargeting and long pieces. Set view expectations from the size of the avatar's market, not from entertainers' numbers: the topic does the targeting, so a narrow topic with small views can still be the best performer if the right people watch. Before choosing where asks point, find where sales actually come from now (often messages, not the website) and point every ask there: in long pieces add a short spoken mention about a third of the way in as well as the end, put it above the fold in the description, in a pinned comment and on the profile, and update the descriptions and pinned comments of the biggest past pieces, since each old piece keeps sending viewers. Where the platform allows and X2 is met, send each new follower one short question (are they here for free content, or do they want help?) and treat each yes to help as a lead. Let people buy where they already are: if they watch a video or post on a platform, use the platform's own shopping, link or sign-up feature so they never leave mid-flow, since each extra step loses buyers; when a platform launches a new native feature it wants used, test it early, while the platform is still pushing it. Organic content also warms paid traffic: people who see an ad then look up the owner's content show up and buy more, so start it alongside ads and treat it as nurture before it becomes acquisition. Keep calls to action out of the content itself, with the next step in the description or profile link, and let paid ads carry the direct ask to the same audience. Use volume to learn what works, then put more time into fewer pieces: a better piece can earn far more than proportionally more reach.

### Paid ads
- L61 [considering or running paid ads] [stage: S3+] Paid ads rent strangers' attention: reach is guaranteed, return isn't, so they're a game of efficiency. They're the fastest way to scale but not the most efficient: they speed the business towards wherever it's heading, including somewhere bad, so add them only once the offer, the message and the opt-in already convert, not before the first target (L4, F3, F32). Four steps: pick a platform with the avatar on it; target it (lookalikes built from the best lists, then filters); make the ad repel everyone else (callout: labels, yes questions, if-then statements, unusual results; contrast, likeness and scene in the visuals; then value using What-Who-When, O5, and a clear call to action); get permission to contact them on a simple landing page that matches the ad. The first 5 seconds and the headline matter most: record about 10 ad bodies and 30 opening lines a week and combine them, and check hooks and awareness level before campaign structure or bidding (L73, L76). Build ads as three interchangeable parts: hooks (the first 5 seconds, each a different angle), bodies (a deeper explanation, a teaching point, a belief-breaking piece in C14's formula, or a demonstration, which must be real, see X9) and calls to action; record each part once and cut every hook with every body and every call to action, so a batch of hooks, bodies and calls to action multiplies into many ads. Make only as many combinations as the testing budget can give readable samples (L43); if it can't, test hooks first on one body and one call to action, then bodies, then calls to action (L63, front to back). Read results by part, not by ad: pool each hook, body and call to action across the ads that used it to find the best few of each. Next round, keep the winning body and call to action, re-record the best hooks in a new wrapper (setting, clothing, background), and write new hooks to beat that control (L63). Study competitors' ads in the platform's ad library and break down about 50 long-running ones; an ad running a month or more is probably profitable. Read the library by hand or through a tool the platform permits, never by automated collection its terms forbid; borrow formats, structures and angles from what rivals run, never their wording, images or footage (W1; see X9). An AI tool may draft ad variants and copy from the claims file (O47) and propose budget moves, but the owner approves every ad before it runs and every budget change against the three phases below and M19; give any tool connected to the ad account the least access it needs (D17). Three phases: track money (set up tracking before spending), lose money (testing: allow each new ad up to 2x the cash a customer pays in the first 30 days, and kill it at 1x with no leads), print money (when returns beat cost, spend as much as capacity allows). Set the budget backwards from customers wanted x cost per customer, plus 20%. Learn with a small spend on one ad, from a monthly learning budget the owner can afford to lose. Choose a platform the owner uses, can target on, knows the formats of and can afford the minimum spend for, starting with the placements competitors use. Build lookalikes from clients first, then the warm list, then cold leads, keeping all lists in one place (including ad leads who didn't buy); if lookalikes aren't possible, target by interests. Start in a puddle and grow to a pond, lake and ocean: the ratio falls but total profit rises; with local targeting add few filters, because narrow audiences burn out faster. The person shown should look like the avatar. Run the best organic pieces and testimonials as ads with a simple banner call to action rather than re-recording them, chosen by saves and follows, not views: the flow is organic to paid. Native-looking is fine, disguised is not; never target sensitive categories, and where group targeting isn't allowed, let setting, props and dress attract the matching audience; run a client's or partner's post as an ad only with written consent, labelled, with any payment or gift disclosed (L37; see X14, X8, X13). For colder audiences, test a short article-style bridge page between ad and offer page. Ad platforms are auctions, so whoever converts best can outbid the rest. Search ads are harder to crack but steadier once working, and a good-enough agency can run them under T2; social ads need fresh creative about every 2 to 3 weeks for a small audience, so plan that creative load before starting. Later test: ads aimed at the followers of a Dream 100 name who won't promote the owner, never using sensitive targeting. Paid clipping (third parties cut the owner's long pieces into clips and post them on their own accounts, paid per 1,000 approved views) is paid reach and follows this rule: only once there are long pieces with proven clips (L60) and a funnel that converts; set a total budget, a rate and a per-clip maximum, so spend is capped whatever the views; write a brief from the claims file (O47) and approve only clips that follow it, carry the ad label (X14) and make no claim beyond it; trace each clip's opt-ins with its own link or keyword (L14), and judge it like any ad on avatar opt-ins and sales per pound spent, never views, since clippers' audiences may be far from the avatar (F14). For a launch or deadline campaign: spend a small share (about a tenth) in the first week only to find winning creative, raise spend steadily, and expect to put most of the budget into the last few days; set the most the business will pay per buyer from cash collected in 30 days and lifetime gross profit (M20, M23), never an arbitrary cap. Think in hundreds of pieces of creative, from sources already there: screenshots of buyers' posts and results, buyers' own videos (encourage them with a bonus unlocked by filming their first result in the first week, with consent, X13), short clips from live sessions, and the owner's best past ads re-recorded in several settings. To keep results as spend grows, add problem-aware hooks for colder viewers and callouts for many narrow sub-groups of the avatar (many small pools rather than one ocean). Optimise campaigns for purchases and track earnings per click. Before any image ad runs, look at it at the size it appears on a phone (send it to a phone): if the offer and any text can't be read, cut text until they can; lead with the offer and a true local or callout headline, and test many variations of colour and image, including real work and real clients with consent. A proven organic piece can become an ad by adding a few seconds with the offer and call to action at the end. Once ads work, add retargeting across every platform the business advertises on, and bid on the business's own name and branded terms in search so competitors can't take people already looking for it. Increase spend while return holds, after attribution is checked; fix the page and the ads before raising spend, since that alone may create a supply limit (D11). If reported returns contradict the accounts (a ratio that would mean losing money while the business is profitable), the tracking is wrong: fix attribution before moving any budget, using platform conversion tracking, a post-purchase "where did you hear about us?" question and, where safe, pausing one campaign or area for a set period to see what sales actually drop; then move money from break-even campaigns to the winners. An offer sold warm (to an audience or list) straight to checkout will convert far worse on cold ad traffic: plan the cold sales route (often a booked call, C2) and launch it together with the ads, judging both together, since good ads with a weak sales route fail and the reverse. When spend stops scaling, the usual limit is creative, not budget: once a piece wins, make many variations of the same piece (colour treatments, styles, a short animated version, re-filmed versions) rather than new concepts, and keep winning copy running for months while creative changes often. Good video usually beats still images, but still images beat poor video. Show a range of real people within the avatar in the creative so the platform can find more groups who see themselves in it, never by targeting sensitive categories. Someone native to short-form platforms can spot weak hooks fast. AI-generated people may appear only as clearly fictional presenters: never presented as real customers or describing results that didn't happen (X9, X12). When testing new ads, let each campaign spend up to about twice the allowable acquisition cost (or twice the gross profit collected from a client in the first 30 days) while it still produces leads before cutting it, since early campaigns are inefficient; then make variations of whatever works. Research others' ads in the platform's public ad library: ads that have run for months are probably working, retargeting ads stay the same longer than cold ones, and the comments under ads list prospects' objections for the next batch. Make ads look like the organic formats trending on the platform, since the best ads don't look like ads. A challenger can frame "us against them"; a market leader frames itself against the alternative (doing nothing) instead; never fill an ad with the negative image, since viewers associate whatever is shown with the brand. Restate the price in everyday terms the avatar already spends on, and test several such comparisons.
