## Case patterns (K), continued

Same format and use as rules-cases.md.

- K26 [a small retailer selling other companies' branded products, where shoppers try in store and buy online elsewhere] Situation: shoppers came in to be sized or try products, then bought the same item online for less. The move: make the in-store item one of one rather than one of many (the exact piece that fits them, since sizes vary between batches), make the visit an experience worth having, carry the sizes shoppers ask for, and consider a paid fitting or styling service credited against purchase. The underlying problem is selling a commodity; the long-term fix is own-brand or exclusive products and services that can't be bought elsewhere (O2). Fits when: the retailer's margin depends on in-store conversion. Linked rules: O2, O6, M2, C12.

- K27 [a profitable online membership for a hobby, sold through paid live challenges a few times a year, capped by how much the owner dares spend on ads] Situation: buyers paid a small fee to join a live challenge and about a fifth joined the membership, delivery could take twice the customers, yet the owner capped ad spend and made only a handful of ads per launch. The move: pitch only the annual plan with a physical starter kit as the premium (switching to annual-first had already doubled order value), open monthly after the cart closes, make hundreds of ads from members' posts, a first-week task where members film their first result, clips of the owner's live sessions and re-shoots of the best past ads, add problem-aware hooks and narrow callouts, spend most of the budget in the final days, set the spend limit from lifetime gross profit, and test the challenge as a paid trial of the membership. Why it works: the business was purely demand-limited and its numbers already worked, so more spend and higher order value scale it. Fits when: churn is low and the buyer-value maths is known. Linked rules: L61, M40, M20, M23, O26, X13.

- K28 [a profitable local home service with a very high close rate and show rate, one big referral partner, and ads already returning well on an unoptimised page] Situation: demand-limited, delivery could take double the leads, one complementary trade partner sent steady work in exchange for the high-ticket jobs the business deliberately didn't do, and the ads sent traffic to the home page. The move: a modest price rise with a written break-even close rate; a dedicated ad landing page with the free named inspection as the offer and every site button pointing to it; image ads checked at phone size with the offer first and many variations, the best organic pieces turned into ads with a closing call to action; more spend only once the page and ads were fixed; cross-platform retargeting and bidding on its own name; monthly reactivation sequences to past customers on true angles (savings measured from consenting customers' bills, a seasonal allergy theme, case studies); a partner offer where complementary trades sell the low-cost entry check to their own customers and keep the money; and outbound to community-group organisers for event slots, chosen as one of the two partner routes rather than both. Why it works: the numbers already worked, so fixing conversion and then volume scales it, and complementary partners never compete with it. Fits when: the business is demand-limited, the entry service is cheap to deliver, and trades that serve the same homes don't do its work. Does not fit when: delivery is already full (D11) or partners would see it as a competitor. Linked rules: D11, M5, L44, L61, L69, L76, L79, M35, L32.

- K29 [a door-to-door sales team moved onto bought leads whose cost per client then exceeded what each client was worth] Situation: a field-sales service had grown on door knocking; bought leads made the team stop knocking, and acquisition cost passed the gross profit per client. The move: send the team back to outbound, give inbound leads only to the best closer as a promotion with a separate commission plan, iterate the offer, lead source and process with that one seller, and hire an experienced marketer in house. Why it works: outbound keeps volume and cost in check while paid leads are tested with the one variable that matters. Fits when: a working outbound team exists and paid leads are new. Does not fit when: there is no proven closer. Linked rules: T1, M23, L61, L62.

- K30 [an online store selling standard and custom products, most sales from one ad platform, reported returns that make no sense, few repeat buyers] Situation: profitable, but ad tracking said every sale lost money, and custom orders were tucked away on a few pages. The move: fix attribution before changing spend, then move money from break-even campaigns to winners; focus on the least price-sensitive buyer group it already had most of rather than trade buyers who shop on price; send custom enquiries through a one-step form and calendar, a short pre-call video with price ranges, text questions on budget, decision makers and timing, and a call that closes with a choice of card or financing; put the custom route on every product page; email the list twice a week. Why it works: custom work is the differentiator rivals can't match and carries far better margin than standard items. Fits when: some buyers want custom work and the business can deliver it. Linked rules: L61, L2, C2, M32, L57, L36.

- K31 [an event-based training business running many one-day events and a few multi-day camps, with a copycat chasing the one-day format] Situation: the one-day events created heavy operational drag and the bottom fifth lost money, while each multi-day camp made far more. The move: cut the weak one-day dates, scale the camps, raise prices on both, and let the competitor copy the low-margin format rather than reacting to it. Why it works: profit sits in the format that delivers the deepest result, and reacting to a cheaper copycat by cutting price or adding value for the same price destroys profit without reducing churn (M2). Fits when: one format is clearly more profitable per event and per staff day. Linked rules: M24, F31, M2, F30.

- K32 [an information business selling a cheap book funnel into coaching, almost all customers from one ad platform, spend stalled and payback slow] Situation: demand-limited, a very visual result (trips customers had taken), one winning image ad, and few sellers working a growing lead flow. The move: build a customer content loop (customers post short clips of their results in exchange for an extra resource and give consent for ad use), make many variations of the winning creative, hire someone native to short-form platforms, turn organic winners into ads, score leads at opt-in on the two facts that predict buying and call the highest first with a larger sales team, and open calls with the trade-offs. Why it works: creative and conversion were the limits, not the channel, so fixing them lowers acquisition cost and lets spend reach bigger audiences. Fits when: the result is visual and customers are happy to share it. Linked rules: L61, L62, L72, O11, X13.

- K33 [a small coaching business for a niche group of owners, maxed out on the founders' time with unlimited messaging access, a low-price programme with the same acquisition cost, and costly multi-day challenges] Situation: supply-limited, slow payback, and the low-touch product had broken the economics. The move: one offer at the high price, delivered as a one-to-one deep dive then quarterly small-group sessions of about four plus two in-person events a year; unlimited messaging replaced by the community and a few emergency lifelines; the cheaper programme closed to new buyers; a quarter paid up front with layaway as the downsell; one long themed live session instead of a five-day challenge; consented client videos (with a stated rebate) and organic winners as ads; renewals sold in person. Why it works: the same founders serve several times as many clients at the same price, and cash comes in sooner. Fits when: the founders are the draw and clients value the in-person events most. Linked rules: D11, M37, M28, L79, M9, L61, X13, X14.

- K34 [a local, capital-heavy subscription service losing money, with two buyer types (individual households and associations that award contracts by tender) and several half-run channels] Situation: door-to-door already converted about one in twelve doors, cash ran short because equipment and delivery were paid before the next bill, and association contracts were won on lowest bid. The move: serve individual households only (no auctions), make door-to-door the one channel with commission-paid sellers trained by shadowing and drilled scripts, and rebuild the first transaction so blended first cash beats every cost to the next bill: a core option of equipment fee plus first quarter, and a yearly prepay with the equipment free, as an either-or. Why it works: each sale funds itself, so growth no longer needs debt. Fits when: delivery has spare capacity and the conversion is proven. Does not fit when: the market is mostly tendered contracts. Linked rules: M20, M32, M37, L4, T1, X3, X18, X22.

- K35 [a high-end personal service sold to wealthy individuals, with a premium onboarding package, optional continuity, a weak opt-in page on paid traffic, and a few referral partners quietly bringing a large share of revenue] Situation: paid clicks went to a bare "get more info" form, so very few clicks became clients; continuity uptake was low because many clients sat on old lower rates, prices appeared only after the call and there was no anchor; a handful of partners found by a few dozen cold emails produced about a third of revenue. The move: fix the page first (a qualified free taster of the service as the lead magnet, a short pre-call video, budget, authority, need and timing plus an income question at opt-in, and a headline naming the free thing and removing the main worry); then send ten times the partner emails to every adjacent business that holds the avatar at the right moment, offering events or extras for the partner's clients and farming each partner as an account; then add a premium continuity anchor, present continuity as the normal next step after onboarding, and keep raising price while gross profit rises. Why it works: the service already converted, so the leak, not the offer, capped growth, and partner outreach had the best return per hour. Fits when: close rate on the few qualified calls is already high and partners share the avatar without competing. Does not fit when: the service itself is not yet producing results or referrals. Linked rules: L42, L44, C2, L73, L69, M7, M8, M5, O2.

- K36 [a multi-location appointment service with several co-owning partners, little ad spend, weak rebooking and staff who leave with clients] Situation: demand-limited with spare chair time, the busiest days priced the same as quiet ones, clients returning only when they remembered, front-end promotions sold well but the core upsell depended on who asked, staff had no path, and partners no longer shared goals. The move: settle the partnership first (equity swap across co-owned locations, a buyout tied to replacing the departing partner), then a caller who rings new leads within a minute, peak-day prices with members exempt, rebooking and the membership offer before the service, prepay at checkout, an arrival checklist for attribution, and a two-year title-and-pay career path with a profit-share route to run a new location. Why it works: the partnership drain freed the owner to execute, and rebooking plus membership raise visits per client without new acquisition cost. Fits when: the service repeats on a natural cycle and close rates on promotions are already high. Does not fit when: clients buy once. Linked rules: D11, M8, M37, L14, L72, T1, T3, X3.
- K37 [a busy owner-run restaurant or other high-volume, low-margin walk-in business preparing to open a second location] Situation: full at peak times with queues, quiet off-peak, strong reviews and word of mouth from a good product, almost no sales of the highest-margin items such as drinks, round prices, no menu or add-on system and no item-level margin figures. The move: fix the first unit before opening the second: a price ending just under the round number, published peak-day prices, the menu ranked by price and margin with a first-visit pick and "goes well with" lines, one rotating special on a table card, first-time visitors marked and given a surprise ending and a personal comeback card, a lawful review ask, staff drilled daily on four short if-then scripts with a small leaderboard prize, a pre-opening promoter night for the new unit. Why it works: on volume, small changes per order add up to large profit gains without new customers, and the product already brings people back. Fits when: demand is already strong and the product is good. Does not fit when: the product is weak, since marketing only brings more people to leave. Linked rules: M5, M32, M8, M15, D11, L65, L69, C13, D5, X3, X12.
- K38 [a niche professional service with a content audience losing money across several product lines] Situation: an expert firm serving one creative industry ran a profitable done-for-you service, an education line that broke even and a software line that lost money, with content traffic sent to a website while most sales came through messages, a low hourly consultation fee charged before the call and lifetime access sold cheaply. The move: shut the losing line (or sell it to an interested large customer for a lump sum), keep the education as long-term nurture, point every call to action at the messaging route that already sold, message every new follower with one question, book free sales calls qualified on budget, authority, need and timing with a pre-call video, sell a fixed outcome package priced for at least 80% service margin with an optional share of the client's deal or a higher flat fee, downsell education only to unqualified callers, and expect supply (more practitioners) to be the next constraint. Why it works: the audience and demand existed; they were spread across lines that lost money and sold at the wrong price. Fits when: one line already makes money and buyers ask for it to be done for them. Does not fit when: no line makes money. Linked rules: F22, M24, M1, M2, M7, C2, L60, D11, X4.
- K39 [a business-to-business consultancy selling a large project to strangers from cold ads] Situation: a specialist consultancy sold a large up-front project from cold ads straight to calls, needed several calls per sale, had a cost per client close to the client's value, few clients moving to its retainer and retainer clients leaving after two periods because they never saw their numbers. The move: call out the larger client segment in the ads with pain-based hooks; a lead magnet that reveals the problem or gives a free tool; a two-step page with the booking on the second page, the pre-call video above the booking and no repeated questions; a small, fast first offer (a pricing review) sold on one call; the main project offered at the first result with the first offer's price credited; the set-up fee waived for a year's commitment or paid with quarter-to-quarter continuity; the owner doing the first deliveries and the sales calls; the consultancy pulling the client's numbers itself for the retainer review; ad tests allowed about twice the allowable acquisition cost per campaign. Why it works: cold buyers take a small step easily, and a delivered result makes the larger sale an easy one to a customer rather than a stranger. Fits when: the main offer is large and the traffic is cold. Does not fit when: buyers arrive warm and already informed. Linked rules: L2, L73, L44, C2, M20, M35, M40, M9, L61, C13.
- K40 [a subscription for a consumable physical product, bootstrapped from pre-sales] Situation: a founder who had lost a venture-funded business started a subscription for a hard-to-find food product, meant as a small hobby business. The move: pre-sell through a crowdfunding page whose ranking rules were studied to win free reach; ship the first orders fast and phone every buyer to convert them to the subscription with a paid caller; turn an accidental free item in every order into a "for as long as you stay" bonus, later sold to existing members as add-ons; recruit promoters from a competitor's public partner list by offering to feature their recipes, then a recurring commission per paying member; track gross profit per order fully loaded and negotiate every line; outsource every operation to long-established partners under specifications and audits except the one input whose loss would stop shipping; hire experts out of retirement; later, cut a swollen middle-management layer, use a token-price friend trial for referrals, and buy a content business to help members use what they bought. Why it works: customers funded growth, each order was profitable from the first, and retention bonuses made leaving costly. Fits when: the product is consumed and reordered and hard to find elsewhere. Does not fit when: margins per order are too thin to give a recurring bonus or commission. Linked rules: M20, M41, M1, L69, L66, T2, T1, T3, M9, F22.
- K41 [a practice paid mostly by third-party payers (insurers or schemes) whose payments are falling] Situation: a multi-site practice billed insurers outside their networks, payments per visit fell while costs rose, profit reached zero, it offered many services to many avatars and had always relied on referrals. The move: choose one of two different businesses: join the payers' networks and win on operational efficiency and cost (no pricing power), or move to private payment and win on marketing and selling; for private payment, cut the lowest-paying payer type to restore cash, advertise one to three entry offers locally with leads called within a minute, take a small paid assessment at booking, sell the full package at the end of the assessment, upsell and cross-sell the rest, and use the cash to hire senior talent. Why it works: the two paths need different skills, and a cash practice lives on acquisition efficiency and lifetime value. Fits when: buyers can and will pay privately. Does not fit when: the service is one buyers only accept if covered. Linked rules: F28, F30, F12, M31, C3, L72, T1, X4.
- K42 [a long-established, low-margin made-to-order products business selling both to repeat organisations and to impulse gift buyers] Situation: a family business with thin net margin, prices set by adding a margin to cost, a loss-making second shop, two different avatars (price-sensitive repeat organisations and impulse gift buyers), a strong search channel run by an agency, one viral short video that sold out a month's stock, no email follow-up to its list and nothing in the package to bring buyers back. The move: close the loss-making location and move its best staff; price-test the impulse items and, more cautiously, the price-sensitive ones; max out the search channel that was returning many times its cost and bid on the brand's own name; choose the impulse-gift path and post short-form content several times a day with a young creator who knows the trends, recruit affiliates on the selling platform and put spend behind organic winners; an in-package offer and email flows to make one-off buyers repeat. Why it works: thin margins make small price and cost changes large in profit, and impulse gifts are shareable. Fits when: production can absorb more volume. Does not fit when: the business needs the price-sensitive repeat buyers for a sale. Linked rules: M5, M31, L57, L60, L61, L69, M15, M19, F22.
