## Offers (O), continued

### Value equation

- O21 [designing, reviewing or pricing the offer] Value equation: value = (dream outcome x perceived likelihood of achievement) / (time delay x effort and sacrifice). Raise the top two, cut the bottom two. Every offer must answer the buyer's four questions: what will I get (dream outcome), how will I know it will happen (likelihood), how long will it take (time delay), what is expected of me (effort). Score each driver 0 to 1 for every offer version and fix the lowest first. Time delay has two parts to cut separately: the time it takes the buyer each day or week (micro) and how long until the full result (macro). Buyer-side five-step pass (F34 run on the buyer's journey, from first contact to the full result): list every step, task, decision, wait and worry the buyer must go through, then (1) question each one (who says the buyer must do it), (2) delete it or take it on (the owner does the prep, the setup, the switch-over), (3) simplify what stays (one recommended option, ready-made templates, fewer decisions), (4) cut the time to the first visible result and to the full result, aiming for about half the usual time, (5) automate the buyer's remaining steps only once 1 to 4 are done. Each change lowers effort or time delay; then raise likelihood (proof like theirs, L32, O25) and reverse the biggest remaining risk with a real, stated guarantee (O8, X11). Keep any effort that produces the result itself (practice, the client's own decisions) and remove only friction around it. Ask buyers which single change would most change their decision (F25, O6's 5 to 10 call test) and build that one first; one material change at a time (F30). Price only after the pass: the honest comparison against the total cost of the buyer's next best option (money, hours, mistakes and risk, C14) should make the price look small next to the value (M2). Every speed, ease or risk claim must be true and deliverable every time (X9, O15); "all risk removed" is said only of what the guarantee actually covers.
- O22 [improving the offer] Focus on the bottom of the equation. Bigger claims are easy and everyone makes them; making results faster and easier is hard to copy and is where the best businesses win. Push time delay and effort towards zero.
- O23 [improving the offer or the client experience] Perception is reality: an improvement only adds value once the client perceives it. Show progress and remaining effort visibly (for example a progress tracker). Look for psychological solutions as well as logical ones: logical fixes have usually been tried already. Anyone can sell at a discount; it takes good marketing to sell the same thing at a premium. A psychological fix, for example: offer fewer places at a higher price, which makes more people want them, only if the limit is real (O16).
- O24 [writing copy, messages or the offer] Dream outcome: channel desires the avatar already has, never create them; list them in their own words. Status drives value most: frame benefits by how others will see them (partners, rivals, their own clients). When two offers promise the same dream outcome, it cancels out: win on likelihood, speed and effort instead. Find the built-in, lasting pain of the avatar's kind of business and show how the offer avoids it. From calls, list their unspoken desires and use them only to frame what they already want (M27). Frame status by how the buyer sees themselves too: for cautious buyers, looking prudent is status, so the offer never looks flashy or risky.
- O25 [writing the offer, messages or calls] Likelihood of achievement: people pay for certainty. Raise it with: proof the owner has done it; proof that people just like them got the result; the number of clients helped or volume of reviews; certifications and accreditations; statistics and research; experts vouching for the owner; a new, unique mechanism they haven't failed with before; describing their situation so well they think "they get me"; a live demonstration or recording of the outcome; what the offer includes and leaves out; and the guarantee (O8). The same work is worth more when they're more certain it will work for them. Cite only independent recognition: paid "best of" lists presented as awards mislead; genuine small awards or listings help and can open a call, with the awarding body and year named. Years of experience count as authority but must not imply the business has traded that long. Borrowed credibility comes only from accurate quotes of published sources (C14), never implied endorsement, and "even businesses like X use it" or popularity claims only with dated, documented evidence in the claims file (O47) and consent, never before that data exists: popularity stands in for proof, so it must be true (see X9). Make the owner easy to check: the X2 trading details on the email signature, offer doc and lead magnet page, and an open invitation to check the business on the public company register, through references (C9) and its real insurance (O42): a con relies on nobody checking. Assume a replying prospect will search the owner's name and the business name: once a month, search both and fix anything stale or inconsistent in the first results; any public profile stays plain, complete and a check point only, with no messaging from it before the first target (L4). Between a well-known client and a similar small local one as proof, lead with the similar one. Put any genuine, relevant qualification where buyers will see it (signature, booking page, video caption, video-call background), stated exactly, never implying a qualification or accreditation not held (see X9). Make proof stronger by making it recent (dated, the newest first), visual (a screenshot, photo or video of the result before and after, with consent, X13), plentiful (gather every genuine review the business already has across platforms and show them together, where each platform's terms allow, X12) and told from the pain the client started in, so the prospect sees themselves before the result. Ask for a testimonial at the moment of greatest satisfaction, straight after a win, and make the sale at the moment of greatest need (M31).
- O26 [designing the offer or onboarding] Time delay has two parts: the long-term outcome they buy, and the short-term wins that keep them in long enough to get it. Build a big emotional win as close to purchase as possible, with visible milestones along the way. In copy, say how fast clients start seeing results, how often, and how long the best results take. Activation is the client's first measured outcome by their own count (D8); the first deliverable live is the leading indicator. Track days from signing to each, and test which predicts take-up of the continuity offer once there are 10 clients.
- O27 [competing with free options, or writing about speed] Fast beats free: the only thing that beats free is fast. Against free guidance, free vendor webinars and doing it themselves, lead with speed (sorted before the next moment that matters to them).
- O28 [designing the offer or delivery] Effort and sacrifice: list every hidden cost for the client (staff hours, learning new tools, data security worries, buy-in from other decision makers, the standing of their most experienced person (give that person a reviewer or owner role, D7), fear of changing tools, risk of complaints) and cut or reverse each one. Done-for-you is valued higher because it removes effort, so in a done-with-you offer cut perceived effort hard: do the prep, give ready-made templates, keep sessions short. In copy, show the good things they can keep doing and the bad things they can stop doing, and list both sides of the sacrifice other solutions demand: what they would normally have to start doing that they dislike, and what they would normally have to give up that they enjoy; the offer removes each one it truly can, taken from the avatar's own answers (F25), never assumed. Done-with-you is the harder sale and the harder retention, because the client does the work: expect to need more proof and follow-up than a done-for-you seller. Once measured on the first clients, state the total time the client needs as a true figure in copy and onboarding, never an estimate presented as fact (see X9). For every AI or automated step, write what it does that the client's current way can't do as well; if nothing, that step stays out, because learning new tools is itself a cost.
- O29 [deciding what to build or include] Find what clients value most, triple down on it, and ruthlessly cut everything else. Sell what they value; use it to give them what they need. Before adding anything to the core offer, the continuity offer or a lead magnet, list what could be removed or replaced instead, prefer the version with fewer parts that still gets the result, and answer in writing: which client, which problem, the single most important benefit (O3), how the owner knows (a logged quote or count, never a guess) and what the client's experience of it looks like step by step (O7). Promote as much as you like outside the offer, but when tempted to add a session or recording inside it, put that energy into outreach instead unless clients asked for it. Two product strategies, pick one: rank and build (clients vote on requests and the top one is built each cycle, which suits buyers who want many options but drifts towards a cluttered product), or elegant subtraction (design the best end experience and leave out whatever gets in its way, which suits buyers who want simplicity); mixing them serves neither. Ask clients two questions: if only one part of the offer were left, which would you keep, and which part would you not miss at all? Remove the part nobody would miss for new clients first and watch who notices; existing clients keep what they were sold until their term ends (X3), and complaints from poor-fit clients are not a reason to put it back. Test a new product or line on the most engaged clients before building it fully: a limited, scheduled release (a small set, a sample box, a pilot batch) sold to the list, then judge by sell-through and repeat orders which items to build out.
