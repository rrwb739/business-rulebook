## Delivery and operations (D)

- D1: moved to X4.
- D2: moved to X5.
- D3 [planning or running client sessions that change a client's workflows] [shape: service] Think in workflows, not roles: list each client staff member's tasks in the area the offer covers, then automate one workflow end to end before the next. Before automating, ask each staff member who will run it where it breaks now and what they would change, and bring them into questioning and deleting steps, so the version they run is partly theirs. Then go in this order (F34, the core method): question each step (why, for whom, what output, who owns the requirement), delete what does not serve the output, simplify, speed up, then automate; never automate a step that should have been deleted. Roll each new workflow onto a small batch first (for example 10 cases), check quality (errors, complaints, outputs received), then widen. Messages the client sends its own customers give a choice (two routes, two dates) with a one-line reason rather than a bare demand, are accurate on any rule they cite (X4), and are tested against demand wording on that batch, measured on the response the workflow needs. Lead each such message with what the recipient gets, in five beats: show the client understands how the recipient works, the real consequence plainly, one specific action, an easier route, and thanks; any penalty or rule fact comes from official guidance and the client checks it (X4, O47). Never say how many recipients are late: where the client's records show it, say most have already responded, keep disapproval on the late act, never the person, and give the how-to and the recipient's benefit. Before automating, walk each workflow backwards once (from the final output to the first step, asking at each step what had to be true for it to happen on time; the owner's own funnel likewise, from paid invoice back to first message), and write down the one thing the current way does best so the new version keeps it. Every automated workflow has a named exceptions pile (a reply, upload or record it does not expect) handled by hand by a named person and counted weekly, with the commonest cause fixed in the checklist (D5), and a written manual fallback the client can run in its busiest week if a tool, integration or the owner's access fails, rehearsed once before the first live deadline. If a workflow error reaches any client's customers, pause that workflow in every client running it until the L75 review and checklist fix are done, telling each client the same day. All of this stays within X4 and X5.
- D4 [building prompts or AI drafting for a client] [shape: service] Have the AI ask its clarifying questions first, then train it like a new employee: written rules plus the client's best examples. Judge it against the human process given equal training time. Every AI-drafted message to the client's customers gets a named human check against a short checklist before it goes. In session one, tell staff that questioning or stopping any step that looks wrong, the owner's included, is part of the job, and praise it when they do (D13).
- D5 [designing or reviewing sessions, or training anyone] Success means the team can run it without the owner. Document, demonstrate, duplicate: record the task and write the checklist, then do the next work block from the checklist alone (if the result is still top quality, the draft holds); demonstrate it while they watch and fix the checklist wherever they stop the owner; then they do it in front of the owner. Before trainees run a workflow, show 2 or 3 recorded examples done right (X5) and ask them to talk through what they would do, plus one or two anonymised error cases on a made-up test case, each followed by "what would you do instead?" and the right step. Once clients consent, examples show people in a similar role at a similar-sized client, not only the owner (D13). Give each trainee one part of the method to learn first and teach back to the others, with the named lead checking all of it. Let the named lead choose within the fixed method (tone, timing, batch order, M13), and replace bare bans with the replacement step and its reason. The bar: could a stranger get the owner's results from the checklist alone? If they get it wrong, the checklist was confusing; a step that needs explaining should be split. Competence is not performance: plan reps (slow, smooth, fast). Judge whether they follow the directions, not the result: if they follow them and the result is wrong, the checklist is wrong. Praise each correct step, do not take over when they slip (unless a client's customer would be affected right now; then step in and debrief one to one, D16), give one piece of feedback at a time, and never punish mistakes in training. When performance dips, retrain, find the dropped step and reward following it. A step is learned only when the person does it in the real situation that should trigger it, so rehearse in that situation and praise straight after the right step; as the step becomes habit, praise it less often, and correct by ignoring a minor slip once rather than punishing it, since punishment works only while the punisher is present. Before giving feedback, ask them to check the run against the checklist themselves, with the fix slot booked (D14); for any repeat task, set one timed target with a stop point, time each run, keep a list of missed steps to repeat, and point out every improvement. Any role suggested for client staff comes with a written scope, carrying one line on how it moves the client's own count, and the client appoints them. Done-with-you scales; done-for-you hits the owner's hours. Front-load training early, plan reps for the rest, and never promise full independence by a date unless pilots show it (O15). Never sell someone else's delivery at the owner's price until their recorded client results match the owner's; until then, ration the owner's access (group sessions, recordings). After any handoff, compare the numbers: if they fall, the owner abdicated rather than delegated, so work side by side until they hold. On every handoff (setter to closer, closer to delivery), introduce the next person by name and role, book their first contact, pass the notes, edify them, and stay the owner of it until they confirm contact; step back from calls or support gradually. Before the last week, hand reinforcement to the client's named lead: the short list of steps to praise when done and correct when skipped. Add every fix a client finds (X5) to the shared checklist so the next client gets it. Size the detail to the person: junior staff get step-by-step checklists with exact wording, senior people get the outcome and the check, and every checklist is written for the least experienced person who will run it. Ship every workflow as fill-in templates in the client's own branding and wording, with as few decisions as possible and one task per short video, and check the spread of pilot results, not just the average: a wide spread means a variable is still uncontrolled. Put each workflow's first step as a cue where staff do the task. Staff copy what the owner does more than what the owner says: before each intake, check the habits they will see. Before the first pilot, run each session aloud, timed, with an outsider, count teaching reps under F24, and after each real session note where staff stalled and whether the explanation caused it. The same checklist can run the owner's own repeated work through AI: only for a workflow already done by hand at least weekly, first put through D3's order (question, delete, simplify, speed up), map each stage into its workflows and each workflow into its steps, turn one workflow at a time into an AI skill, and run it on real tasks, correcting it until its output matches or beats the owner's own against a fixed checklist; only then chain passed skills into a longer run, with a human review at every handoff and before anything is published or reaches a client, least access for every connected tool (D17), and tools only as tools.md allows; a skill that slips below the checklist goes back to being done by hand until fixed. To take the owner out: from a week-long time study, list every task, then mark each green (someone already on the team can take it once shown), yellow (needs a one-off process the owner can build) or red (needs a person or skill the business doesn't have), and hand off in that order. Write the recurring decisions as if-then rules, with a spending limit each person can approve alone, checked in the monthly figures. Ask each person how their role makes the business money, turn the mistakes that would cost money into their scorecard, and hand over only once they hit it; 60 to 80% of the owner's standard is enough to start if they are improving. Hand over in three steps: they watch the owner, the owner watches them, then the owner is available but not involved. Set the checklist's detail by the learner's level: fewer, bigger steps for someone experienced, more and smaller steps for a beginner, and add a step wherever the owner did anything off the checklist while demonstrating.
- D6 [delivery work that repeats across clients] Template it so each new client costs less to deliver.
- D7 [onboarding a new client] [shape: service] In the first 48 hours, make and keep small promises and say exactly what happens next. Paying only gives them permission to start: say so at sign-up, and get their first task done inside those 48 hours (D12). Within 24 hours of signing, send a short personal welcome video that thanks them for their trust and says the owner's job now is to justify it. Re-sell the destination at onboarding and at the first-week check-in. Test: offer nearby clients an in-person kickoff, within F1 hours, and compare referral and follow-on take-up with remote kickoffs. At kickoff, ask what the client will stop or pause for the programme and record it as an action with an owner (D14); ask the named lead to write down what result would make them the best case, predict out loud the tasks the team will complete and sessions they will attend, and share that goal with their colleagues; record it in their words and read it back at the first check-in. All are questions, never conditions or promises (M27, O15). Offer the named lead the role as the demanding job it is, with true stretch challenges; public dares stay out (M27). Tell staff where and how to ask between sessions, that small questions are welcome, and the owner's real reply time (O8), shown on every session slide and checklist footer; check each quarter that nothing sits between staff and that reply time (M9). Offer two support routes with the same reply time and let them pick; ask how the team handles a mistake today (fixed quietly, reported or blamed), logged in their words (C4), to set the D4 and D13 tone. Write down what the team expects from the owner, then what the owner can expect from them, beside the guarantee conditions; this adds to the written terms and never changes them (O8, X3). For group intakes, run the kickoff as one group session (still with the welcome video and first task in 48 hours), opening with a 2-minute round per client (location, team size, main problem this period; nothing on fees, customer lists or customer details, M12), each followed by one true, specific line from the owner's notes; compare first-task completion with one-to-one kickoffs. Give the most experienced staff member a reviewer or owner role (O28). At 48 hours, ask the decision maker "how do you feel about having signed?" (1 to 10), log it against the sales call recording and review any low score for pressure (M27, L75). Before kickoff, get the names and roles of everyone attending and use them. The named lead enters the session dates in their own calendar. Ask each staff member who will run a workflow what they want from it and tie their tasks to that answer (D12). If two people claim the same area, give each one named part to own (D14). Give each client a one-page map: the parts of the method in build order, one line on why each comes before the next, the named lead and weekly tasks, and what each side can expect, in the client's wording, labelled as the owner's own method (O25; see X9), pinned where the team works. Release the rest in weekly portions: at kickoff only the map, the guarantee conditions and week 1's checklist; the full terms still go before commitment (O8, X3). Spend 5 minutes before every client call or session rereading the notes, the kickoff goal, last actions and the previous outcome. Where the offer is meant to grow the client's revenue, make the first onboarding step a fast-cash campaign from the client's own unused assets (past customers, old enquiries, an existing list or following), contacted lawfully (X2, X8 in the client's country), so the service pays for itself within about 30 days; test the campaign on a few early clients before building it into onboarding, and say plainly what it depends on, never promising its result (X9). Tie the client's own actions to it: bonuses unlocked by completed actions, a guarantee conditional on named actions stated before purchase (O8, X11), and the activation point measured and enforced openly. Run onboarding one-to-one where the price allows, piloted first on a small share of new clients against the current process (L63). On the call: restate why they bought and the goal in their words, tie the next three actions to that goal, get one quick win live (access working, first step done), and fix any technical problem on the spot by bringing in support. Tell them at the start that notes will be sent, then send a summary with links only to the guides for the parts they said they care about, and introduce their named contact live on the call, booking the next meeting before anyone leaves. Every hand-over (sales to onboarding to account manager) is introduced in person, so the client always knows who their one contact is. For low-ticket offers, run one group onboarding call a week that every new member is invited to.Send short, frequent updates that need nothing from the client (what was done for them today, what moves next), so they can see the work; a client who never hears what was done assumes little was. Once a contract is signed, deliver as if the client could leave next week: contract defensively, deliver offensively.
- D8 [measuring usage or client results] [shape: service] Ask specific count questions, not yes or no questions, at set points (day 1, 30, 60, 90 and the end; L32). At each check-in, note in the named lead's exact words whether they call the workflows "we" or "it"; a shift from "we" to "it" is an early warning to act on before renewal (M9), marked as an inference (C4). Track by workflow and the named lead's own tracker entries, never by monitoring individual staff (screen, keystroke or per-person activity logs); per-staff data is the client's decision as employer (see X8). Build triggers into the tracker for misses on the owner's side (a reply past the stated time, a cancelled session, a template error) and for a guarantee milestone missed while the client met the conditions; when one fires, tell the client the same day and apply the stated remedy before they claim it (O8, O42, L75). When a client's own account disagrees with its tracker, treat the measure as suspect first: check how it is counted against the source, re-measure at the next set point, log both, and never overrule the client with the number.
- D9 [deciding what to improve] Ask clients open, non-leading questions about what need it fills, and invest there. At each pilot review, ask what they value (let more of them get it, sooner), what they do not (cut it), and what they do not understand (reword it). Invite the 1 or 2 most engaged clients to be design partners, framed as the favour it really is, with any perk disclosed and never tied to a positive review (X3, L37). Collect feedback on a private form before any group discussion. With written consent, credit a design partner in a template's version notes, never used publicly to imply endorsement (O25). Tell each person who suggested a change what was decided and why, including when it was not used. At the first follow-on check-in after a real cycle of use, ask the named lead to rank the parts from most to least useful and name one thing missing. At each review and exit interview, ask what suppliers in the field usually do that annoys them and fix one where X3 and F1 allow. The owner reads every client and prospect message, giving any single surprising remark a dated "why is this happening?" check within a week, while product changes still wait for F27's three clients unless the check finds a fault. Tag every complaint as a result issue (they did not get what they wanted) or an experience issue (not the way they wanted it); a good experience never excuses a missed result (M9). At each review, ask "if you could keep only one thing, what would it be?". Ask clients who have stayed longest why they stay, and keep asking until one shared reason appears; that reason, not what the owner assumed, becomes the value line in O3, and making it truer becomes delivery's main goal (O29). Rank problems to solve by reach (how many suffer), impact (how much), confidence the fix works and cost, and set a fixed monthly time or money budget for the top-ranked client problem. The brand's promise stays fixed while the way it is kept keeps improving, since what buyers experience outweighs anything the business says (L65). In a community or client group, spend most of the time listening for the problem many people share right now, check it with a few active members, fix the most pressing one, then tell everyone in one short message: here's the problem you had, here's what I made, here's how to use it. If the problem persists, the fix didn't work: make it simpler and try again.Pair it with the reverse: "if we removed one thing and it changed nothing for you, which would it be?"; across many clients the two answers rank the parts of the offer, so invest in the top and cut or shrink the bottom.Each quarter the owner goes through the product as a buyer would (buys, uses, attends, eats) and asks whether a buyer would bring it up unprompted to the next person they speak to; if not, that is the improvement to work on before adding promotion (F30). To improve a result clients struggle with (churn, margin, leads, close rates), survey clients for who performs best on that number, interview about 20 of the best together, and keep only the few practices they all share, not everything any of them does; roll those out only when the gain is clearly worth the cost of change.
- D10 [the owner's own admin] If the business sells a method, use it on the owner's own admin first: the owner should be the first proof. Use the same D3 order: question, delete, simplify, speed up, then automate. Time how long logging one touch takes and cut it towards 30 seconds (one lead-sheet entry sets status, note and next touch due, L8); tools may queue and draft, never send unapproved (L7) or play recorded messages (X2). From the first paid sale, keep the business's books in proper accounting software and reconcile monthly.
- D11 [client acquisition starts working well] Delivery capacity becomes the bottleneck: check it before raising outreach volume. Count onboarding and offboarding hours per client: if they grow while tenure falls, check fit (F12 red flags) before adding outreach. Build extra delivery capacity a quarter before a planned push, not during it. When the business can't take more clients because the owner is delivering everything, the constraint is delivery, not demand: don't change the selling model, change delivery or add a deliverer. First compare what was promised with what is actually given: extra calls, instant replies and open access given out of worry train clients to ask for more, so deliver what was promised and route other requests to a resource, the support route or the next set call slot (D7). Early on, access to the owner can be a real differentiator; as the business grows, move it into the published tiers (M7) rather than giving it to everyone. To change delivery for existing clients, tell them plainly and in good time what changes, why (the owner can't keep the promise to everyone at the current load), what they gain and what they lose, keep their current terms until the contract allows a change or they agree, and let anyone who prefers leave on the existing exit terms (X3). Never present less as more or use the change as pressure; accept that some will leave and that new clients join on the new terms. Where every role is one person deep, one absence pulls the owner back into delivery: plan for at least one trained backup per delivery role, funded by price (M5) and a premium guarantee the business can meet (O8), before adding demand. When delivery is the limit, there are four fixes, each building a different business: raise the price (a brand business, M5), change the ratio from one-to-one to small groups of about four, then larger (the owner keeps improving and stays the draw), productise (a marketing and sales business, O34), or hire deliverers (a recruiting, training and culture business, T1). Pick by what the owner wants to run; when the value is the owner's own skill, hired coaches trained in days usually dilute it, so prefer a better ratio first. Moving to groups, present it as better (members hear others' questions and solutions) and never apologise for it; keep existing promises until their terms end (X3). Replace unlimited messaging access with set calls, questions answered in the shared community so everyone benefits, and a few stated emergency lifelines, framed as the client learning to decide rather than outsourcing every decision. First triage every growth question as supply or demand with one test: if outreach or ad spend doubled tomorrow, would sales double or would delivery break? Breaks means supply: raise price substantially first, then change the delivery ratio (one to many or small groups), then add trained deliverers. Doubles means demand: fix acquisition and conversion, and check for a leak (opt-ins that don't book, bookings that don't show, calls that don't close) before adding spend or sellers. When thin margins block both hiring and marketing, the fix is usually more per customer (price, or a short scripted add-on offer at the end of every job). When demand peaks at certain times and runs empty at others, move it with published time-based prices (a lower off-peak price is easier to accept than a peak surcharge) or longer hours. A physical-product business that sells out can sell its base something with no supply limit (a warranty within X11, a partner's product, a service) and use the cash to secure supply, rather than buying more ads. The constraint moves between supply and demand as each is fixed, so re-run the test each quarter and move attention with it. When a part of delivery clients love is full, raise the price or add people before changing its format: shortening or grouping the loved part to fit more people can destroy what works, and something clients love is far harder to find than the right price.When demand is steady but the right people can't be hired, the business is in the recruiting business: treat candidates as the avatar, use the same Core Four to reach them, offer a clear career path, and size recruiting spend and any staff referral bonus against the yearly gross profit one more deliverer adds rather than against habit (paid through payroll on stated terms once the hire stays a set period, X18); solve that before raising price again or changing the model.
