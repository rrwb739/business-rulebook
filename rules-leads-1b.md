- L30 [tracking sales, or judging any new channel or method] Track the whole funnel: sent, replied, booked, showed, offered, closed, cash collected. A 20% lift anywhere counts the same, and gains at separate steps multiply. Use leading indicators, not only lagging ones: before the first sale, judge each step on its own number (list accuracy and bounce rate, opens or pick-ups, replies, how long a call lasts before a hang-up, second-call show rate). Revenue, churn and sales are results the owner can't act on directly; the steps before them are. A new method can look like failure from outside for months, so judge whether each step is moving the right way, not only whether a sale happened. Track show rate (usually the cheapest lift), calls per close, cash collected, close rate of shows and of offers, offer rate (a low one is a targeting problem), the share of each sale paid upfront (it shows how deep discovery went), follow-up attempts per lead, and, from recordings, talk-to-listen ratio and questions asked. Replace any published benchmark with the business's own numbers as soon as they exist. Log follow-up call pick-ups by day and time slot and move call blocks to the best slots, within sensible business hours and the calling rules (X2, L50); also log show and close rate by call slot, and test morning slots against straight-after-lunch slots for price calls. Each day, read 3 or 4 headline numbers (engaged leads, calls held, cash collected, months of runway, M26), the one the owner least wants to see first, and the rest of the funnel at the weekly review. Each month, count qualified calls held (every C2 qualifying box ticked) and its change on last month, read beside the inputs (L41) and cash, never instead of them. Track numbers in pairs, volume next to quality (emails sent and reply rate, calls booked and show rate). Log the median time to reply to a lead each week. Give each stage a one-line definition someone else could check ("replied": a human reply that isn't an opt-out, bounce or auto-reply; "showed": joined within 10 minutes of the start). After a first call, count a prospect in the pipeline only for an action it took (confirmed the C5 recap, returned the pre-call form, got every decision maker to the next call, replied about the offer doc), never document-open tracking. After every sales call, log a 0 to 100 chance it closes by a stated date with one reason, and score it at month end (add for closes, subtract for misses); if forecasts run high, check which qualifying boxes and CLOSER steps were skipped (L16). Tag every offer not closed in 14 days with one lost reason (C14's five types, no urgency, no decision maker) against the CLOSER step that should have caught it, and review monthly. Tag every engaged lead by type (reply, lead magnet use, free guide request, call booked) and business type (L8), and track each type's rate to call held and to sale and its days to signature, so an easy count never stands in for the one that pays (L14). Each day, before new outreach, clear a breaches-only list from the lead sheet: replies past the L13 window, bookings without the C3 confirmation or reminder, lead magnet takers not contacted within a working day, follow-ups past their due date. When a number and what buyers say disagree, check both against the source before acting (D8). Diary use: aim for about 70 to 75% of open call slots filled, never above about 85% (no slack for fast bookings); below about 60% means more outreach. Also track booking cancellation rate, opt-in to close and scheduled to close.
- L31 [a client has measurable results] Ask for referrals, and mention who sent the owner when contacting the person referred. Buyers referred by their peers in the avatar are usually the best leads.
- L32 [marketing, messages, calls or planning delivery] [Fixed] Proof beats promise, and proof is the top priority: a small promise with lots of proof beats a big promise with none. Capture proof at set milestones and show it wherever the business sells. The strongest proof is specific, recent, on video and from a similar buyer. Proof comes in three degrees, weakest to strongest: what the owner has done themselves, what the business did for buyers like them, and what they get themselves from the free tools and content (strongest, but slowest). Measure every client at set points (day 1, 30, 60, 90 and the end) and keep improving delivery until the numbers are compelling. From 3 clients, publish average results in four parts: the share of clients (average or median) who got X result, in Y time, under Z conditions; fewer conditions is more convincing, and say what the bottom 20% got too. Label pilot results as pilots and never present hand-picked or unpaid pilot averages as what paying clients get until paid clients confirm them; label any demo on made-up data as a demo, never a client result (O15; see X9). Pick the testimonial closest to the prospect's situation and let them connect the dots; never claim "I can definitely help you". Do the impressive work first, then state the facts about it. Trust and belief are levels, not yes or no: for each step of the process, list what raises and lowers them, and remove the lowerers. Proof is the one thing competitors can't copy. In the body of a message, pain or outcome comes first and proof second (the hook itself still leads with proof, L73). At the earliest stage, brand is reputation for results: every client outcome, and no unhappy clients (L75), is the brand work. Once there are several results, also show the hardest case solved, so easier buyers infer it will work for them. Where possible, have a result confirmed by someone other than the owner (the client saying it on video, or a report from the client's own system), with consent and no client confidential data showing (L37, M45, X5). In case studies, make the client's named person the hero (with written consent), not the owner: open on them in a real, relatable spot with one true flaw, split their desire into the external goal and the internal struggle, name the real obstacle as it was, never inflated, show the counted result and the change in the team in their words, and tell a pilot that missed its target only as one that missed it (O11); test five turning points against a plain before-and-after (L63). Once there are pilots, build letters, case studies and pre-call videos mainly from short consented case histories, each framed as a buyer like them that took the risk first, labelled as pilots (L33). When choosing a testimonial for a hook, prefer one that speaks to a hidden feeling logged under O5. Once 3 or more pilots consent, test a short reel of several clients against the single closest testimonial, judged on bookings (L63). Lead with true peer proof at moments of most uncertainty (a market change, a first key date, a reply saying they're unsure how others handle it), using sourced figures or the business's own counted, dated data, worded as an invitation in the buyer's terms with the sourced number, never vague ("countless") and never implying the reader is following a herd. Test raw, plainly filmed proof against a polished version (L63). Show small, everyday results as well as big ones, because some prospects disbelieve big ones. The higher the stakes of the advice or purchase, the more proof buyers need, and the less a faceless or AI-made voice can supply it: pair third-party proof (results, numbers, recognised credentials) with live demonstration, the owner visibly using their expertise on real cases. Build proof into delivery so it is captured, not created: record client sessions and audits with consent (X8, and call-recording consent rules in the buyer's state or country), pull real moments and stories from them, and where there are few clients, do documented free audits or demonstrations for prospects and publish them with permission (X13); in product businesses, the product in use is the demonstration. So that acquisition doesn't depend on the owner's face, build proof capture into normal operations: a weekly collection of what clients say in the community, recorded sales, onboarding and delivery calls joined into before-and-after stories, and key result moments documented, all with written consent (X13, X8); turn genuine public reviews into marketing only where the platform's terms allow; any reward for giving a testimonial is offered whatever its content, and disclosed (X12, X14).
- L33 [creating content or posts] Document the journey only with each client's written consent, and never show any client's confidential data (X5). With no results yet, show effort instead of claiming proof: post the volume of work done and what was learned, true and countable (L32); people who follow the effort are an audience before there is a product, and a waitlist is the next step (M14).
- L34 [someone turns down a free pilot or free offer] If a free offer can't be given away, one of these is true: they don't want the outcome, they don't believe it, the hidden costs are too high, or they don't want to feel they owe the owner, so say plainly what the exchange is (the M5 written commitments) and that nothing else is owed. Ask "What would I have to do to make it worth it for you to continue?" The answer shows hidden costs (staff time, setup effort, risk). Remove them, then charge more than rivals who still carry them. Magic question, on calls and pilot reviews, with price and time fixed: "For [the price] over [the length], what would this need to look like for you to be keen to go ahead?" Fixing the price makes them solve inside it (M2 holds; log answers under L59).
- L35 [contacts who haven't bought yet] Keep them warm: check in like a human and share useful results regularly, so they come asking. Before each date that matters to the avatar, send every past pilot, client and leaver the business can lawfully contact a short personal note in the owner's voice: one true line about their own result or the coming date, no ask, with the opt-out (X2). For a replied prospect who stalls, send one template marked as a draft and ask what they'd change so their team would use it, then rebuild it to their notes (within the business's lane, X4; no client confidential data, X5; never the full paid sequence, O14). After 3 conversations with no decision, change the approach rather than repeat the pitch (F24).
- L36 [choosing or relying on a lead source] No third party should control the lead flow. Build the business's own email list of prospects who replied or opted in, and keep it in a system the business owns. No outside party (an agency or outsourced closers) should own a large share of sales: it lowers what the business is worth. List single points of failure (sending domain, messaging account, lead source, booking tool, payment provider) and keep a warmed backup for any that would stop outreach or payment for more than a week. Log every payment indemnity claim, card chargeback and refund request monthly; any one is an early warning to investigate within a week. On a first late or failed payment from a client with a clean record, assume something went wrong (often on the business's side or the bank's) and ask before chasing; a pattern goes to the F12 red flags; check the late payment rules before using or waiving statutory interest (see X20).
- L37 [after delivering value, or a client finishes] [Fixed] Ask for an honest review, and for them to pass something useful to another buyer like them: people link the value of what they're introduced to with the person who introduced it. Give value first with no expectation. Send a short handwritten thank-you to any client that gives a reference call, review or testimonial, and to pilots at the end, with nothing of value attached (M27). Reviews must be genuine: never make a free pilot or a bonus conditional on a positive review, and say clearly where a reviewer got the service free (see X12).
