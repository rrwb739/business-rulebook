## Leads and marketing (L), continued

### More, better, new
- L62 [growing lead flow from what already works] Three levers, in order: more (turn up the volume of what works to maximum capacity), better (once volume breaks, find the constraint, the step with the biggest drop-off, and test there), new (only when more and better return less: a new placement on the same platform, then a new platform, then a new Core Four method, usually in that order). Volume is the order-of-magnitude lever: a ratio is capped at 100% and volume isn't, so don't polish ratios while volume is low; volume negates luck. Within more, lowest risk first: more message variants (L63), then more daily volume, then more methods (L4). "Breaks" means the cost per customer gets too high to sustain: keep the volume up while fixing the constraint, switch from more to better when improving a step beats one more unit of volume, then go back to more. Ask: "What stops me doing 10 times what I do now?" After any new placement or platform, measure it, scale it with more and better, then repeat. A second working acquisition channel makes the business safer and worth more to a buyer (not before the first target, F3). Before any new channel, method, big spend, supplier (T2) or partnership (T3): write the need-to-believes (the avatar is there, the owner can reach them lawfully, at a cost that fits M23); write the money and months committed before judging it, treating early spend as an investment judged on step-by-step progress (L30); list each way it could lose money with a likelihood, triple each likelihood to correct optimism, and go ahead only if the plan still fits M26's runway; name the non-cash return (what will be learned, which proof or reusable asset it leaves); and define a win as clients signed who reach their first result on time (O26) and keep doing the work for the first month, not signatures alone. Iterate step by step while the need-to-believes hold (replies, then opt-ins, then whether they're the right prospects, then the message or targeting; L42, L63), and pivot only when data proves one false. Before going new, make what works consistent and partly automated (templates, batching) so the new method doesn't starve the old. Run the rule of 100 again for each new medium: skill on one doesn't carry over. Easy (numbers rising) means more volume; hard (a step stalled after an L43-sized sample) means change the method at that step, never the offer, market or business (F22, L3). Judge "more" on absolute profit, not the ratio: spending twice as much for a lower return is still worth it while total gross profit rises and the ratio stays above M23's floor; for paid ads, more creative first, then more spend, then more platforms. Before turning up more, list each lead source's share of new clients against the share of the owner's hours it takes: a source bringing a large share for little time gets the next hours, then ask whether it is exhausted or can take far more volume, which it usually can. When estimating what several levers could add, weight each by its channel's share of revenue and add the results; multiply only for levers acting in sequence on the same funnel (a better page and more spend on the same ads), since growth in one channel does not multiply another. Single-channel risk: when more than about half of leads or clients come from one channel, one platform change, account ban or domain block can cut sales overnight. Before the first target, accept it (F3) and keep the L36 backups. After it, in this order: build long-term nurture of the owned list (useful contact on a schedule with occasional offers, L57) and the referral system (L66), both low-risk cash; then open a second channel with only one thing new: hand the first channel to someone trained first and check its numbers hold (D5, T1), then put the owner or a proven person on the new channel, never a new person on a new channel, since two unknowns can't be told apart; expect a delay before it works and set the stopping rule in advance (L43).
- L63 [testing anything in outreach or marketing] Test one thing at a time per channel per week, and each test must still reach L43's sample size. Every Monday: pick last week's winner, log it in a test log so nothing starts from scratch, and set up the next test to beat the current best. Fix the constraint first: improving the worst step pays most; of all possible tests, pick the one likely to move the result most given the time and money, and when two look equal, the one that costs fewer hours. Test front to back in the order the prospect meets things (subject line and opening, then the value, offer and call to action, then the booking page). If the current version can't be beaten in four tries or a month, move to the next constraint. A change at one step can hurt a later step, so check the steps after it. Don't waste a test on trivia. Keep the lines that land, move them to the front, and replace only the weak parts each round. Write many message versions: exceptional ones are found by making more. Split effort 70/20/10: about 70% copying exactly what works, 20% close variations, 10% something genuinely new with no expected return; the same split applies to call scripts (L16), the product (O29) and reinvestment (M19). Every change has a certain cost and an uncertain benefit, and the owner will tire of a winner long before prospects do: keep a winner's hook and message and vary the wrapper (subject line, opener, line order, example, P.S., format). Expect most tests to lose and keep the log going; run tests side by side only once traffic gives readable samples (L43). On a list or send large enough, keep a test running at all times: a random 10 to 20% gets the challenger and the rest gets the control, each part still meeting L43's sample before it is read; on smaller sends, alternate whole batches between control and challenger instead. Once a control has had its L16 sample and is tiring, make the challenger a wholly different angle, not a variant (O13), and log each control's age and send count so a win against a tired control isn't over-read. One test a week, every week, compounds.

### Lead getters
- L64 [planning how to scale lead flow] Leverage is what the owner gets for the time spent. Lead getters are people who do the Core Four for the business: customers (referrals), employees, agencies and affiliates. They're not a Core Four method: use the Core Four to recruit them, then they use it for the business. Start with referrals because they're available now; add the others as they become affordable. Every lead is a potential lead getter, and the best setup is a lead getter who recruits other lead getters. Leverage isn't working faster: it's getting more from each step, and someone far ahead is doing different things, not the same things faster.
- L65 [any client relationship; designing referrals] Referrals grow exponentially, everything else linearly: if monthly referrals beat monthly churn, the business grows with no other advertising. Few referrals means the product isn't remarkable enough, or the owner isn't asking. Build goodwill (value minus price) six ways: (1) sell to better-fit clients (find what the best clients share and target only that); (2) set expectations lower than delivery (lower the promises step by step until close rates start to fall, then stop); (3) get more clients better results (track client activities such as time to first result and attendance, compare average clients with the best, interview the best, make new clients do what they did, measure the lift, and make those actions guarantee conditions); (4) make wins faster and more frequent (many small deliveries, regular updates even without progress, cram wins into the first 48 hours, always book the next contact, add 50% to timelines and deliver early); (5) keep cutting effort (each month, find the most common problem from support data, surveys and reviews, get fix ideas from clients who succeeded despite it, trial the fix on a small group of struggling clients, roll it out or go back a step, then move to the next problem; when a fix ships, tell every client who hit the problem, in one line, what changed); (6) tell them what to buy next (M8). Ask: if every future client had to come from one existing client, how would that client be treated? Write the growth loop down (outreach, then pilots, then measured results, then proof in outreach plus referrals and introductions, then higher reply rates, then more clients), and each quarter find the weakest link and fix it; measured client results (D8) are its centre, so when the loop stalls, check results first (F18). Keep a calendar of each client's business milestones (founding anniversary from public records, its first key deadline after delivery, the anniversary of working together) and send a short personal note on each; no birthdays or personal dates unless the person offered them (C4). Three levels of influence, weakest first: what the owner says (ads, content, outreach), what other people say about the owner, and what buyers experience themselves; every promise is judged at the third level, so keep every promise fully (what was said, the way it was said, by when) before raising volume at the first. Free content is a run of small promises (this will save you time, fix this, raise that): keep each one inside the piece, since kept small promises are what make buyers believe the big one. The cycle to build on purpose: promise, keep it, buyers tell others, others try it, keep it again; it takes time, so the owner measures it as the share of new clients who came from a client or a client's word (L14 tagging) each quarter. Influence (how likely people are to do what the owner asks) rises with four things, strongest together: status (controlling something the audience wants), power (people followed the owner's past directions and got a good result, so every piece of content should give advice that works when followed), credibility (true proof verified by others, L32) and likeness (the owner being themselves, so people like them see themselves). Bigger asks need more of all four. Branded items worn or used in public spread word of mouth: for recurring clients who visit often, give a well-made branded item free and unconditionally (a slogan the avatar likes on the front and the logo smaller on the sleeve or back while the brand is weak), never in exchange for a review (X12). To take the owner out of improving the product: each month have clients rank their biggest problems, pool a budget across all clients to solve the top one in a way no single client could afford, test the fix on a sample of clients chosen to represent the whole base (strong and weak, large and small, different areas), never only favourites, and hand the winners to every client with instructions.
- L66 [asking for referrals] [Fixed] Referring puts the client's own reputation at risk, so they refer only when their benefit outweighs that risk: raise the benefit with incentives and lower the risk by keeping every promise. Treat the ask as an offer. Ask at the moment of purchase, and test a second ask at the first measured result (L63); ask after a delivered result, linked to what they and their team actually did, with any incentive as a thank-you rather than the reason. Ask "who" not "if" ("Who else do you know who's facing [the problem]?"), and get a real three-way introduction, not just a name. Incentive options: a one-sided reward worth up to the cost per customer; a two-sided split (credit for the referrer, a discount or bonus for the new client); unlockable bonuses; referral events (1 to 4 weeks, named, sold with real success figures); an ongoing programme (a standing line in emails and on calls); a named, expiring voucher worth about a third of the price for a friend, sent with an introduction (true value, real expiry, O37); or a referral as a bargaining chip (a lower price only in exchange for introductions, never a plain discount: "They got that for three introductions; I'm happy to give you the same."). Default: two-sided, same total as a one-sided reward, because it protects the referrer from looking as if they cashed in on a friend. Match the incentive to what the business sells (extra support or credit rather than cash); the more outrageous the referral offer, the more people refer. Compliment framing works ("who do you know who's as good at this as you?"); if no one comes to mind, "Fair enough. If anyone mentions [the problem], would you pass my name on?", never a guilt line. Offer the introducer one true line they can use if they want, never wording they must use (M45). After a measured result, the owner may bring a short list of non-competing businesses built from public directories (L9), never from the client's own contacts, ask which they'd be comfortable introducing, and drop it at the first hesitation (C11). Tell an introduced prospect plainly there's no obligation and what, if anything, the referrer gets; never use the referrer's name as pressure or say the referrer loses a reward if they don't buy (M27). Disclose any incentive wherever a referral is public, and get consent before contacting a referred person who wasn't introduced directly (see X15). After the first target, tests: a hosted walkthrough where a client invites 3 to 5 non-competing peers, any host reward disclosed to guests, guests' details shared only with consent and no fees or client details shared (M12; see X18, X8); and a stated donation to the referrer's chosen charity for each introduced client who buys, disclosed wherever public and never tied to a review (X3; see X15). Low priority, only with clients who've had a measured result and never before any paid ask: a genuine question they can answer no to ("would you say you like helping others in your field?") before the ask. Measure referral rate and churn monthly, and log whether referrals stop when an event or voucher ends. Make referring good for the referrer's standing: the friend gets something they value (a real service or session, not a small credit), and clients get guest places to bring someone with them to appointments, asked as "who do you want to bring next time?" at each booking rather than "do you know anyone?"; ask at the moment results are felt (O25), and add a one-line P.S. with the current referral offer to every newsletter. In regulated fields, check rules on inducements before rewarding referrals (X4). Word of mouth needs two conditions: no reason for the client to keep the business quiet (business clients often won't tell competitors what gives them an edge) and frequent contact between clients and people who could buy. Where either is missing, referrals will stay small whatever is done; measure revenue retention instead and put effort into keeping clients (M9) rather than into referral schemes. For subscriptions, test letting members give a friend a first order at a token price (often just the delivery cost) with no reward to the member: some members prefer giving a gift to earning one, and referred clients tend to stay longer; judge on how many trial takers keep paying. Referrals are uneven: a few clients bring most of them and nobody can tell in advance which, so over-deliver for every client; when one client brings many, study what happened and make it the standard for all (L65). Fix the product before the incentive: an incentive speeds up people who already want to refer and rarely starts someone who doesn't. Once a referral or affiliate route works, keep investing in it (fewer steps, better training, easier sharing) rather than leaving it to run unattended.

- L69 [considering affiliates or partners, after the first target (M18)] [Fixed] [stage: S3+] An affiliate is a business that tells its audience about the owner for pay or product; new affiliates compound where new clients alone don't. Check every partner's track record and finances (public filings, references), never personally guarantee their obligations or carry all the risk, and take on only affiliates, partners or sellers who have used the core offer and genuinely rate it. Six steps: (1) find them by asking "who already has my leads?" (the software, products, services, groups and events the avatar uses; a business in several categories probably has many), kept as a sheet built from online searches, asking clients if stuck; when selling to consumers, add the employers of the avatar, offering a free benefit for every new starter through their onboarding pack and an occasional on-site session, which costs the employer nothing and adds value for their staff; where the price is too low for direct outreach to pay back within L54's limit, use the Core Four to recruit businesses that sell to the avatar in volume instead of reaching buyers one by one; (2) make them an offer (more money from clients they already have, no delivery work, start tomorrow), with callouts to affiliate owners, their clients, their products, or the owner's own clients ("do you know anyone who runs...?"); (3) qualify them so they're invested and get a fast first win, because if they pay, they pay attention (buying to keep status, a minimum order phrased "anything extra, or just the minimum?", or paid certification at about 10 to 20% of first-year earnings with product purchases counting as credit); the first purchase is an activation threshold, priced just high enough that they care, never as a profit line, and certification exists so they never make promises the business can't keep; if too few sign up, lower the commitment; if too few follow through, raise it; (4) pay for the outcome wanted (new and repeat clients, later reliable earlier steps such as booked calls), up to the maximum allowable acquisition cost: one third of the client's lifetime gross profit (M23's 3:1, since the payout is that client's acquisition cost), counting any other acquisition cost for the same client inside that third; pay in three tiers (about 25% of the maximum on sign-up, 50% on activation, 100% on sustained results), which lowers the average payout and funds contests and recruiting; set the activation point from what the most successful affiliates actually did first (for example bought, certified and used the product for a month), so it is an activity fully in the affiliate's control, and set the top tier at the sales level the business counts as a material producer, earned after one to three months in a row and kept only while sustained; pay in product where it's worth more to them than it costs the business, with low tiers paid in product alone and cash as volume grows, and attach a true status the affiliate can use in their own marketing (a named partner listing) to keeping a set sales level, labelled under X14; (5) launch with Whisper, Tease, Shout (O37), giving them everything ready to use; (6) keep them going by integration (they give away or sell the lead magnet, or sell the core offer; or placements such as a P.S. line in their emails, a thank-you page slot or a joint product, each labelled as advertising with any payment disclosed); let them white-label existing lead magnets and keep all the money from selling one, pay for as long as the client stays, and never cap payouts. Measure affiliate return as gross profit left after delivery and payouts, divided by the cost of recruiting the affiliate: 3:1 minimum, aim for 5:1 to 10:1 and up; if weak, lower recruiting cost, add a launch process so more activate, or improve integration. Start with 10 to 20 affiliates, use their feedback to fix the offer, terms, launches and integration, turn their results into affiliate case studies, then scale. Affiliates must label promotions as advertising, the owner shares responsibility, and no structure may pay mainly for recruiting other affiliates (see X14, X17). Keep a names-only Dream 100 list from early on, sized to the market (about 20 to 30 for a small one): the people who already hold the avatar's attention (newsletters, podcasts, writers, group organisers) and the businesses above; before the first target, no contact, only subscribing and logging what each is working on. After it, give first, ask "what project matters most to you right now?" (F15), and keep in touch about monthly with a short useful update, honouring every opt-out, with any gift low value, the same for everyone and never conditional (see X2, X6, X16). Each partner keeps 3 or 4 written, measurable standards (reply time, claims only from the O47 file, complaints per quarter, ad labelling), reviewed quarterly, with the tools to meet them and an exit under written terms; the invitation states the hard parts and who it isn't for; partners pass on every prospect question and objection for L59 and C14, or introduce and let the owner take the call. Swapping referrals with partners does not scale, because the business has only so many referrals to give back: lead with an offer that pays the partner without needing the owner's referrals in return, such as selling the business's low-cost entry service (the lead magnet) to their own clients and keeping all that money, priced so the business's cost per new client (cost to deliver the entry service divided by the share who buy the core offer) passes M23. Keep the business's own scope narrow enough that partners never see it as a competitor, and send them the work it doesn't do. One person owns the partner programme as a defined job before scaling it (T1). When partnering with a creator or public figure: affiliate pay comes after sales, a sponsorship pays first for posts or for ads run from their account (with their written consent, labelled, X14), and an equity partnership gives a share for promotion; with equity, give part up front, part on measured results and part vesting over the agreed period, and write down exactly what they will publish and how often (posts, links, ad recordings) so both sides know what was bought. The creator's association with the business is permanent once public, so check fit both ways (O2). For a launch or campaign promoted by many affiliates, customers or fans at once: choose rewards that are also a give to the promoter's audience (a session where the audience's questions get answered, a recording they can watch), so promoting spends none of the promoter's goodwill; run two tiers, an unscalable top prize for the few biggest promoters and a long-tail reward with lesser versions of it (a recording, a free extra, a chance at a seat) for anyone who passes a threshold, any draw for seats run as a free prize draw within the law (X3); set the threshold by how much effort each action takes (fewer people for an in-person visit, more for a simple opt-in); cash rewards bring paperwork and tax reporting for every payee, so consider product or experiences where they are worth more to the promoter than they cost. Give it an affiliate sales page (the rewards, exactly what to do, the help given); on sign-up, a personal tracking link and a leaderboard updated often enough to prove attribution and give feedback; pre-written, pre-approved collateral for each kind of promoter (posts, emails, ads, short messages for group chats), written from the claims file (O47) with plain compliance guidance (no exaggeration, labelled as advertising, X14), then refreshed with the pieces that are converting best; regular updates on what is working behind the scenes; and personal help from the team for those moving up the board. Recruit through the same Core Four: one or two emails to the whole list, separate emails to past promoters, a link on the purchase thank-you page and content explaining the programme. Before a new location or launch, a pre-opening night for local promoters (creators, community organisers, owners of businesses the avatar visits) with a traceable offer for their audience; reuse their footage as ads only with written permission, credit and the ad label (X14, X13). To recruit promoters, offer what they value most, which is often reach or recognition rather than money (featuring their work, with credit and a link, in what clients receive), then open the commercial conversation once they reply; build the list from the public partner or affiliate pages of businesses that already use promoters; a recurring commission for as long as each referred client keeps paying suits subscriptions, and a dedicated email from the promoter with an offer and a real deadline usually outperforms social posts. A partner offer must be strong enough to change what the partner does: the usual percentage for a referral changes nobody's behaviour. Give each group of partners an account manager who visits or calls regularly, closes the loop after every referral (without sharing any client's personal or health details unless the client has agreed, X8), and asks which of the partner's current clients would fit rather than whether any would; a partner's first referral is a trial, so make that client's experience exceptional. The promoters chosen shape the brand as much as the brand shapes who wants to promote it: choose them for the range of the avatar they represent and for associations the avatar likes (O2), and give them ready-made materials so their promotion stays on brand and is labelled (X14). Affiliates stop sending work unless kept active: treat each as an account with its own contact rhythm, and run a joint event or useful session for partners and their clients about once a quarter, ideally bringing several non-competing partners into one room and pitching it as collaboration that serves all their clients; log what each partner sent after each event. When recruiting, build separate lists by partner type (each kind of business that holds the avatar at a buying moment), send a fixed daily number of personalised emails, and send once more to non-responders (L52). A larger company that is weak in a channel the owner is strong in may be a partner rather than a rival: offer to run or share that channel for them (co-branded campaigns, joint offers), in writing (T2), keeping the owner's own clients and data separate (X8).
