## Leads and marketing (L), continued

### More, better, new
- L62 [growing lead flow from what already works] Three levers, in order: more (turn up the volume of what works to maximum capacity), better (once volume breaks, find the constraint, the step with the biggest drop-off, and test there), new (only when more and better return less: a new placement on the same platform, then a new platform, then a new Core Four method, usually in that order). Volume is the order-of-magnitude lever: a ratio is capped at 100% and volume isn't, so don't polish ratios while volume is low; volume negates luck. Within more, lowest risk first: more message variants (L63), then more daily volume, then more methods (L4). "Breaks" means the cost per customer gets too high to sustain: keep the volume up while fixing the constraint, switch from more to better when improving a step beats one more unit of volume, then go back to more. Ask: "What stops me doing 10 times what I do now?" After any new placement or platform, measure it, scale it with more and better, then repeat. A second working acquisition channel makes the business safer and worth more to a buyer (not before the first target, F3). Before any new channel, method, big spend, supplier (T2) or partnership (T3): write the need-to-believes (the avatar is there, the owner can reach them lawfully, at a cost that fits M23); write the money and months committed before judging it, treating early spend as an investment judged on step-by-step progress (L30); list each way it could lose money with a likelihood, triple each likelihood to correct optimism, and go ahead only if the plan still fits M26's runway; name the non-cash return (what will be learned, which proof or reusable asset it leaves); and define a win as clients signed who reach their first result on time (O26) and keep doing the work for the first month, not signatures alone. Iterate step by step while the need-to-believes hold (replies, then opt-ins, then whether they're the right prospects, then the message or targeting; L42, L63), and pivot only when data proves one false. Before going new, make what works consistent and partly automated (templates, batching) so the new method doesn't starve the old. Run the rule of 100 again for each new medium: skill on one doesn't carry over. Easy (numbers rising) means more volume; hard (a step stalled after an L43-sized sample) means change the method at that step, never the offer, market or business (F22, L3).
- L63 [testing anything in outreach or marketing] Test one thing at a time per channel per week, and each test must still reach L43's sample size. Every Monday: pick last week's winner, log it in a test log so nothing starts from scratch, and set up the next test to beat the current best. Fix the constraint first: improving the worst step pays most; of all possible tests, pick the one likely to move the result most given the time and money, and when two look equal, the one that costs fewer hours. Test front to back in the order the prospect meets things (subject line and opening, then the value, offer and call to action, then the booking page). If the current version can't be beaten in four tries or a month, move to the next constraint. A change at one step can hurt a later step, so check the steps after it. Don't waste a test on trivia. Keep the lines that land, move them to the front, and replace only the weak parts each round. Write many message versions: exceptional ones are found by making more. Split effort 70/20/10: about 70% copying exactly what works, 20% close variations, 10% something genuinely new with no expected return; the same split applies to call scripts (L16), the product (O29) and reinvestment (M19). Every change has a certain cost and an uncertain benefit, and the owner will tire of a winner long before prospects do: keep a winner's hook and message and vary the wrapper (subject line, opener, line order, example, P.S., format). Expect most tests to lose and keep the log going; run tests side by side only once traffic gives readable samples (L43). On a list or send large enough, keep a test running at all times: a random 10 to 20% gets the challenger and the rest gets the control, each part still meeting L43's sample before it is read; on smaller sends, alternate whole batches between control and challenger instead. Once a control has had its L16 sample and is tiring, make the challenger a wholly different angle, not a variant (O13), and log each control's age and send count so a win against a tired control isn't over-read. One test a week, every week, compounds.

### Lead getters
- L64 [planning how to scale lead flow] Leverage is what the owner gets for the time spent. Lead getters are people who do the Core Four for the business: customers (referrals), employees, agencies and affiliates. They're not a Core Four method: use the Core Four to recruit them, then they use it for the business. Start with referrals because they're available now; add the others as they become affordable. Every lead is a potential lead getter, and the best setup is a lead getter who recruits other lead getters. Leverage isn't working faster: it's getting more from each step, and someone far ahead is doing different things, not the same things faster.
- L65 [any client relationship; designing referrals] Referrals grow exponentially, everything else linearly: if monthly referrals beat monthly churn, the business grows with no other advertising. Few referrals means the product isn't remarkable enough, or the owner isn't asking. Build goodwill (value minus price) six ways: (1) sell to better-fit clients (find what the best clients share and target only that); (2) set expectations lower than delivery (lower the promises step by step until close rates start to fall, then stop); (3) get more clients better results (track client activities such as time to first result and attendance, compare average clients with the best, interview the best, make new clients do what they did, measure the lift, and make those actions guarantee conditions); (4) make wins faster and more frequent (many small deliveries, regular updates even without progress, cram wins into the first 48 hours, always book the next contact, add 50% to timelines and deliver early); (5) keep cutting effort (each month, find the most common problem from support data, surveys and reviews, get fix ideas from clients who succeeded despite it, trial the fix on a small group of struggling clients, roll it out or go back a step, then move to the next problem; when a fix ships, tell every client who hit the problem, in one line, what changed); (6) tell them what to buy next (M8). Ask: if every future client had to come from one existing client, how would that client be treated? Write the growth loop down (outreach, then pilots, then measured results, then proof in outreach plus referrals and introductions, then higher reply rates, then more clients), and each quarter find the weakest link and fix it; measured client results (D8) are its centre, so when the loop stalls, check results first (F18). Keep a calendar of each client's business milestones (founding anniversary from public records, its first key deadline after delivery, the anniversary of working together) and send a short personal note on each; no birthdays or personal dates unless the person offered them (C4).
- L66 [asking for referrals] [Fixed] Referring puts the client's own reputation at risk, so they refer only when their benefit outweighs that risk: raise the benefit with incentives and lower the risk by keeping every promise. Treat the ask as an offer. Ask at the moment of purchase, and test a second ask at the first measured result (L63); ask after a delivered result, linked to what they and their team actually did, with any incentive as a thank-you rather than the reason. Ask "who" not "if" ("Who else do you know who's facing [the problem]?"), and get a real three-way introduction, not just a name. Incentive options: a one-sided reward worth up to the cost per customer; a two-sided split (credit for the referrer, a discount or bonus for the new client); unlockable bonuses; referral events (1 to 4 weeks, named, sold with real success figures); an ongoing programme (a standing line in emails and on calls); a named, expiring voucher worth about a third of the price for a friend, sent with an introduction (true value, real expiry, O37); or a referral as a bargaining chip (a lower price only in exchange for introductions, never a plain discount: "They got that for three introductions; I'm happy to give you the same."). Default: two-sided, same total as a one-sided reward, because it protects the referrer from looking as if they cashed in on a friend. Match the incentive to what the business sells (extra support or credit rather than cash); the more outrageous the referral offer, the more people refer. Compliment framing works ("who do you know who's as good at this as you?"); if no one comes to mind, "Fair enough. If anyone mentions [the problem], would you pass my name on?", never a guilt line. Offer the introducer one true line they can use if they want, never wording they must use (M45). After a measured result, the owner may bring a short list of non-competing businesses built from public directories (L9), never from the client's own contacts, ask which they'd be comfortable introducing, and drop it at the first hesitation (C11). Tell an introduced prospect plainly there's no obligation and what, if anything, the referrer gets; never use the referrer's name as pressure or say the referrer loses a reward if they don't buy (M27). Disclose any incentive wherever a referral is public, and get consent before contacting a referred person who wasn't introduced directly (see X15). After the first target, tests: a hosted walkthrough where a client invites 3 to 5 non-competing peers, any host reward disclosed to guests, guests' details shared only with consent and no fees or client details shared (M12; see X18, X8); and a stated donation to the referrer's chosen charity for each introduced client who buys, disclosed wherever public and never tied to a review (X3; see X15). Low priority, only with clients who've had a measured result and never before any paid ask: a genuine question they can answer no to ("would you say you like helping others in your field?") before the ask. Measure referral rate and churn monthly, and log whether referrals stop when an event or voucher ends.

- L69 [considering affiliates or partners, after the first target (M18)] [Fixed] [stage: S3+] An affiliate is a business that tells its audience about the owner for pay or product; new affiliates compound where new clients alone don't. Check every partner's track record and finances (public filings, references), never personally guarantee their obligations or carry all the risk, and take on only affiliates, partners or sellers who have used the core offer and genuinely rate it. Six steps: (1) find them by asking "who already has my leads?" (the software, products, services, groups and events the avatar uses; a business in several categories probably has many), kept as a sheet built from online searches, asking clients if stuck; when selling to consumers, add the employers of the avatar, offering a free benefit for every new starter through their onboarding pack and an occasional on-site session, which costs the employer nothing and adds value for their staff; where the price is too low for direct outreach to pay back within L54's limit, use the Core Four to recruit businesses that sell to the avatar in volume instead of reaching buyers one by one; (2) make them an offer (more money from clients they already have, no delivery work, start tomorrow), with callouts to affiliate owners, their clients, their products, or the owner's own clients ("do you know anyone who runs...?"); (3) qualify them so they're invested and get a fast first win, because if they pay, they pay attention (buying to keep status, a minimum order phrased "anything extra, or just the minimum?", or paid certification at about 10 to 20% of first-year earnings with product purchases counting as credit); the first purchase is an activation threshold, priced just high enough that they care, never as a profit line, and certification exists so they never make promises the business can't keep; if too few sign up, lower the commitment; if too few follow through, raise it; (4) pay for the outcome wanted (new and repeat clients, later reliable earlier steps such as booked calls), up to the maximum allowable acquisition cost: one third of the client's lifetime gross profit (M23's 3:1, since the payout is that client's acquisition cost), counting any other acquisition cost for the same client inside that third; pay in three tiers (about 25% of the maximum on sign-up, 50% on activation, 100% on sustained results), which lowers the average payout and funds contests and recruiting; set the activation point from what the most successful affiliates actually did first (for example bought, certified and used the product for a month), so it is an activity fully in the affiliate's control, and set the top tier at the sales level the business counts as a material producer, earned after one to three months in a row and kept only while sustained; pay in product where it's worth more to them than it costs the business, with low tiers paid in product alone and cash as volume grows, and attach a true status the affiliate can use in their own marketing (a named partner listing) to keeping a set sales level, labelled under X14; (5) launch with Whisper, Tease, Shout (O37), giving them everything ready to use; (6) keep them going by integration (they give away or sell the lead magnet, or sell the core offer; or placements such as a P.S. line in their emails, a thank-you page slot or a joint product, each labelled as advertising with any payment disclosed); let them white-label existing lead magnets and keep all the money from selling one, pay for as long as the client stays, and never cap payouts. Measure affiliate return as gross profit left after delivery and payouts, divided by the cost of recruiting the affiliate: 3:1 minimum, aim for 5:1 to 10:1 and up; if weak, lower recruiting cost, add a launch process so more activate, or improve integration. Start with 10 to 20 affiliates, use their feedback to fix the offer, terms, launches and integration, turn their results into affiliate case studies, then scale. Affiliates must label promotions as advertising, the owner shares responsibility, and no structure may pay mainly for recruiting other affiliates (see X14, X17). Keep a names-only Dream 100 list from early on, sized to the market (about 20 to 30 for a small one): the people who already hold the avatar's attention (newsletters, podcasts, writers, group organisers) and the businesses above; before the first target, no contact, only subscribing and logging what each is working on. After it, give first, ask "what project matters most to you right now?" (F15), and keep in touch about monthly with a short useful update, honouring every opt-out, with any gift low value, the same for everyone and never conditional (see X2, X6, X16). Each partner keeps 3 or 4 written, measurable standards (reply time, claims only from the O47 file, complaints per quarter, ad labelling), reviewed quarterly, with the tools to meet them and an exit under written terms; the invitation states the hard parts and who it isn't for; partners pass on every prospect question and objection for L59 and C14, or introduce and let the owner take the call.

### Execution
- L70 [planning the owner's day or week for leads] One-page advertising plan: (1) pick the type of engaged lead (clients now; later employees or affiliates); (2) pick rule of 100 or open to goal; (3) fill in the daily checklist (who, what, where, to whom, when, why, how, how much, how many follow-ups, how long); (4) do it daily until someone else can be paid to; (5) then recruit help and repeat. The current plan lives in the instructions box. Guard one long uninterrupted outreach block every working day, before anything else, at the same time and place each week, protected even in busy call weeks; use quiet call weeks to work the pipeline (within L12). Don't change the routine (tools, location, schedule) in the middle of an outreach run. In the outreach block the only allowed activities are outreach or doing nothing: no lead-sheet tidying, tool testing, rulebook work or admin; research batches and reminders get their own blocks (L50); if stuck, sit with the list open until something is sent. Work leads that acted since the last block first (replied, used the asset, booked, rang back), then due follow-ups, then new touches. Once there is a team (stage S3+), plan advertising in four directions, not one: prospects into clients, clients into repeat buyers, candidates into employees (T1), and employees into engaged, better-trained staff; growth on the client side needs the same growth on the staff side.
- L71 [the owner is discouraged, comparing themselves with others, or wants to quit] The many-sided die: every roll teaches something, and results compound. The owner can't see what roll others are on; successful people look lucky because they kept rolling, and the owner can't lose without quitting. Once something works, stick with it for years; don't swap methods after a few losses. Anyone earning more is better at some part of the game: learn from them instead of putting it down to luck, shortcuts or cheating. Growth comes in steps (flat stretches, then a jump once something is figured out), so a flat month isn't proof of failure: check inputs first (L17). If a comparison makes the owner act worse (rushing, skipping steps, switching), drop it or compare with someone at the same stage. Growing pains beat the stress of standing still. Asking why it isn't working yet is the cue for the next input (10 more touches or one more call), not to re-plan or switch. After a failed offer, template or pilot, write down what it now takes, then start the retry within a week. Keep a one-page list of why the owner is sticking with the core offer (the L3 need-to-believes still holding, the market's timing, their own reasons) by the desk, and reread it before quitting or switching anything and before any F22 idea review. When stalling, write what the next 4 quarters look like if inputs stay as they are. Nothing hard lasts forever: the owner quits, it gets easier, or the owner gets better. Patience is working out what to do in the meantime. Keep a tally of hard things already got through, and reread it when the next one hits.
- L72 [a lead replies, books or opts in] Speed to lead: contact an inbound lead as fast as possible, ideally within 60 seconds; a slow reply lets them shop around and compare prices. If the owner's set hours make that impossible, keep reply notifications on and a booking link ready to send at once, and target minutes rather than the hour in L13. Once there's budget, someone paid just to call new leads fast is one of the first hires.
- L73 [making any video, writing a subject line or first line, or opening a call or session] [Fixed] The hook decides whether anything after it gets seen, so spend the most effort on the shortest part: the subject line, the first line, the first 5 seconds; lifting the front end multiplies everything behind it. Every email, message and call has a headline, so write it on purpose: curiosity first, then being different; a headline's only job is to get the next line read, so it doesn't sell or name the offer. In a batch, write many hooks (about 50 a session, about 80% reworked from proven winners and 20% new), a few bodies and one or two calls to action. Call out the reader as specifically as still reaches enough of them, in their words; if they wouldn't call themselves the label, move it up or down a level. Lead with proof, promise and plan (for videos, also picture and pain in the first 30 seconds), in any order, leading with whichever the audience most needs (proof first where its first question is why it should listen); in a teaching piece the intro proves the owner can deliver what the title promised rather than repeating the title, and cut every warm-up before the hook. Framing changes how everything after it is judged, so the pre-call video and each session's introduction shape the client's whole view. On video the hook is visual as well as verbal. Once a hook works, keep reusing it (L63). Source hooks from the avatar's own words in replies and calls (O5) and from proven winners, never from an AI brainstorm or gut feel alone (AI may vary a proven hook, not invent one); keep a swipe file of subject lines and openers the owner actually opened, study the first 3 seconds or first line of what already works, borrow structures from unrelated industries, and daily pull apart any message that made the owner click or reply. Hook types, true statements only and one type per campaign when testing (L63): quotes, questions, yes questions, if-then lines, "what [X] doesn't tell you" aimed at the prospect's own hidden cost, a myth reversal with the true answer in the asset, "can't be done" or "only" lines when literally true and evidenced, the reader's own frustration with the usual route, well known but little understood, little known with a big difference, "this changes everything" only for a real change, crystal ball only with a sourced figure (L76, O15), back to basics, and confirming a suspicion only when true and in the claims file; and the outcome-and-character hook: name a specific outcome the avatar wants (in their words, O5) and tie it to a real person who got it (the owner, a consented client like the viewer, L32 and X13, or a sourced public example, never implying endorsement, O25), with the true numbers and time frame, and the result labelled as one result unless it is typical (see X9). Never run down rivals, authorities, the prospect's staff or clients (O2, F27, M27), never use a secrecy primer, and never invent proof: until pilots, use the owner's own true proof (F21, O11, O15). Test a pain-first opener (line 1 names a recognisable pain and its one cause, line 2 the true proof line) against the proof-first control, and an outcome-first hook against an information or tip hook on the same body, judged on avatar opt-ins per 1,000 views (L60), not views. "Free" and "new" are the strongest words: use them only when literally true (see X9, X3). For long assets, draft the body first from the claims file (O47), then write the hook from its strongest claim, which must be unusual (not what rivals already say, O2), powerful (tied to the biggest pain or outcome in the avatar's words, O5; relevant, never overwhelming) and marked proven. To make new candidates, pair one real avatar phrase from the claims file with one image from their world, or a proven claim with a hidden feeling logged under O5, true and never shaming (M27). Long copy works for a motivated buyer with a real pain (O5 sets where it's allowed). Once a quarter, take one proven long-form piece, write out its strategy (target, big idea, claim order, proof, ask), then copy it out by hand as practice (F24), never as a template to send. Findings from the owner's own logged calls or a newly published update, dated in the claims file (O47), may be framed as recent and first-hand; never claim insider contacts.
- L74 [when warm outreach becomes possible] For a beginner, the short-term order is outreach to people the owner knows with a compelling offer, then content for the long term. If the owner's personal network can't be used (L4), cold outreach is slower to start: lean harder on volume and patience (L41, L43).
- L75 [reply or booking rates fall while volume and targeting stay the same; launching anything new; any unhappy client] Bad word of mouth travels further and faster than good. A sign: cost per engaged lead rises while the effort to reach each prospect stays the same, so fewer of those reached respond; this matters more in a small market where the avatar talks. Keep early mistakes small and private: run every new offer free with a few clients first, ask "tell me how I can make this better", fix what they say, and only then sell to strangers (M5). Don't take money from a client the owner expects to be unhappy, and fix any problem fast (M27). With any upset client: invite the whole complaint ("tell me all of it; I'd rather hear everything"), never minimise it, call the concern reasonable, be more upset about the problem than they are, name the feeling with their own word, never a softer one, then ask what you can do together to fix it, opening with the goal they set at kickoff (D13). In a payment or complaint dispute (L36), hear them out fully before discussing money and thank them for raising it. On a disputed result or guarantee claim, go through their evidence with them by question first before citing terms, and never let questions stall a valid claim (O8). Tell a client about any problem with their work, data or deadline the same day, in plain words with the fix. When the owner slips, tell the client the same day, before they notice: what happened, that it's the owner's, and the fix or a redo in the same breath; never blame a supplier, a tool, an authority or their staff, and admit only what's true (O11); if an outside service fails, say so plainly as a fact, then say what the owner controls. Where a slip cost the client's team time, fix the cause in the checklist, say what changed and offer a proportionate make-good (a redo, an extra session), the same for every client in the same case and logged, never cash or gifts to staff (M27). Within M27's order (rollover first, then refund without a fight), offer up to three concrete fixes and let them choose, or name something else. Within a week of any error that reaches a client's own customers, or any data incident, write a one-page review (facts only, every contributing cause, which step was assumed safe, the checklist change, who was told and when), with no personal data in it, read by someone not involved (F15). Before admitting anything that could become a claim, tell the insurer and take advice first (O42); a personal data breach also goes to the client (see X8, X5).
- L76 [writing any message, choosing what to send, or deciding how much to give before asking] Buyers sit on a line from needing little information to needing a lot; those who need little are few and everyone fights over them. Match each message to the reader's awareness: unaware (broad curiosity; any loss figure needs evidence first, O15), problem aware (name the pain), solution aware (why this kind of solution beats the alternatives), product aware (the offer's terms and proof), most aware (clients and replied prospects: just make the offer). Find where most of the avatar sits and lead there; where most are problem aware, cold messages lead with the pain, not the offer. Frame uptake figures as a cost to the prospect and a reason to act now, never as "most are waiting", because publicising inaction can spread it; test a version without the peer figure (L63). To reach the larger group who need more information, give more than you ask: at least 3.5 gives to 1 ask, where the gives for a service are outcomes the owner has achieved, outcomes for clients like them, and free tools that get them a result themselves (L32). Education pays slowly, so it runs alongside direct asks, never instead of them; each lead should get several useful touches before the ask. The bigger the plane, the longer the runway: the higher the price and the colder the reader, the more selling it takes, so a stall often means a warm-style message went to a cold reader. Each step out in awareness reaches a bigger audience with fewer buyers per hundred: the ratio falls, the total rises. If deals routinely need 2 to 4 calls, there wasn't enough education before the first call: fix the pre-call material, not the number of calls. On each call, check which buyer beliefs (O10) the pre-call material has already settled, and spend the call only on the rest. If replies come only from prospects already shopping and dry up as the list grows, widen the hooks to the problem aware and unaware; when results plateau, the usual fix is a hook good enough to reach the next, less aware group, not a new market (L3). At each L1 re-score, also check market sophistication: if rivals all make the same claim (check by sampling about 20 rival pages, M4), lead with the mechanism (O4), never a bigger number (O15, O22). Direct response and brand are one job: every message both asks for a response now and keeps the same promise. For lead magnet takers the owner may lawfully email, test a plain-text sequence of about 4 emails over a week: the true origin story (F21), then one C2 belief per email, each ending with what the next answers, the last asking for the call, each with the opt-out (see X2). Map the avatar's buying journey stage by stage, with the questions asked at each from L59, and put each asset at the stage it answers.
