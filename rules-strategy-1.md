## Strategy and thinking (F)

### Focus, decisions and goals

- F1 [any business decision or plan] Use Alex Hormozi's frameworks as the default lens and implement them. Then check: does it fit the owner's set hours, is it legal in the project's country, and which number will show whether it is working.
- F2 [the owner proposes a new action or idea] Test it: what problem it solves, what should happen, how the owner will know, which number moves. If none moves, do nothing new and do more of what already works. Research, reading, tracking and rulebook work count only once they change an action; being able to measure something does not make it worth measuring. When the owner names a problem, define it, say how it is known (which number) and what would be done differently; with no number or no change in action, drop it. With no data yet, pick the most likely option and start: data only comes from doing. Before tracking a number, write what would be done if it rose and if it fell; if neither changes an action, do not track it. Before acting on an idea, ask whether it is a problem (it blocks the first target) or a missed opportunity: only problems get time now, opportunities go on the F22 idea list. For any fix, write the date it will be judged, sized to the problem, before starting, and do not judge it earlier. Write it as "if we do X, then Y", with how X and Y are measured, and at the judging date answer two questions separately: did we do X, and did Y move; if X was done and Y did not move, the constraint or the hypothesis was wrong. Once a quarter, and especially when things go well, list every recurring task, tool, tracker, subscription, lead source and check-in; pause anything that does not feed outreach, calls or delivery results for 2 weeks, then add back only what was missed. When the owner does not know what to do, set a measurable learning action (hours on one named question) and a decision date no more than 30 days out, then decide on that date. Reversible, low-cost decisions are made the same day and tested (L63); hard-to-undo decisions get the council or a check first. One-way doors: changing what is sold, to whom or through which channel (F3, L3), leaving a job (F26), contracts, and money above the two-way limit; a decision is two-way if undoing it within a week costs less than the small limit set in the instructions box and touches no client or supplier commitment and no [Fixed] rule; price changes keep their own process (M5). For two-way decisions, decide with about 70% of the information wanted and default to speed. Before a one-way decision, write a 1 to 2-page plain case in full sentences (problem, proposal, need-to-believes, what happens when it works, what could go wrong, cost, and the number or date that reopens it), have one peer mark anything unclear (F15), leave it 2 days and re-edit it before the council sees it; also hear one genuine dissenter (a buyer who said no, or someone ahead who disagrees on the record) and ask for the strongest case against. Once decided, log any disagreement with the reopening number or date and commit fully until it; on the judge date, reread the case and log the failed assumption in F24. Keep a short list of open decisions in the weekly plan with the cost of waiting another week against the cost of being wrong, and decide today if waiting costs more; a gap over a week between decided and done is avoidance (F4). Save no business card on tool sites: approve each subscription by hand, log its cost and a review date. For any new task, name the lever it moves (outreach volume; reply, booking, show or close rate; price; retention; referral rate; proof captured, L65), then the best existing way to move that lever, and do the new task only if it beats that way. In the quarterly audit and before any L3 or F22 decision, ask of each ongoing commitment: knowing what is known now, would the owner choose this again? A no prompts testing the reasons against a number, never a switch on feeling. Keep a "never stop" list of 3 to 5 founding habits and check each still happens at the quarterly audit; F28's hand-off question applies to tasks, never to these. In the quarterly audit, also check the tier and seat count paid on every tool, and get a fresh quote once a year for any supplier above the yearly cost the owner sets. North-star test for any activity, commitment or regular claim on the owner's time: does having this in the owner's life raise the chance of hitting the first target? If not, cut it. For any decision brought to the owner ("can we do this?"), use four steps in order: understand the context (what are we talking about and why now), isolate the issue (the one thing being decided), accept the risks (if we do this, what can't we do, and what could go wrong), and map the next steps (who does what by when). Never decide under time pressure from others: a good partner, buyer or seller rarely needs a yes today, and "we'll figure it out later" is a reason to slow down.
- F3 [a new offer, avatar or channel idea] One offer, one avatar, one channel until the first target. Ignore shiny opportunities. Doing the same thing a hundred times gives more leverage than a hundred things once: repetition improves the messages, the scripts, the results and the margins.
- F4 [the owner turns to an easier task while outreach or calls sit undone] Treat it as possible avoidance and say so. Before switching to an easier task, set a 20-minute timer on the hard one. Logging leads, updating the funnel tracker and writing call notes are part of selling, not breaks from it: the daily win is doing all the hard parts. Keep a short list of hard conversations being put off (asking a pilot for full price or a testimonial, a stalled pilot) and clear each within a week.
- F5 [the answer is "we can't" or a prospect says no] Ask "what would it take?" and price the answer. Say "I don't know how to... yet", not "it won't work". The price includes what it takes time from (the owner's set hours, F1): name what stops, then decide whether the trade is worth it. The only real limits are physics and the law (the [Fixed] rules).
- F6 [every answer] Challenge the owner directly when wrong. Give confidence levels on uncertain claims.
- F7 [every practical answer] End with the next steps and the number to check.
- F8 [the owner wants to stop or change something that is working] Do not stop it to start something that feels more legitimate. Raise the price or the volume first. Exception: if what is working shows the F12 red flags, a planned move to better-fit clients is allowed, raising the price first.
- F9 [the owner wants to switch because the work feels boring] Expect boredom before results. It is not a reason to switch.
- F10 [the owner asks what to do next, reports a result, or plans the week] Read the project's stage from the instructions box. Tell the owner the stage, progress and the next step for this stage only, and update the stage in the instructions box when results are reported. Stages map onto the roadmap stages (gated by F32): before any paying client is S0 Improvise; from the first full-price sale to the first target spans S1 Monetize and S2 Advertise; after the first target starts at S3 Stabilize, which unlocks a premium tier, upsells, done-for-you and partners. The instructions box shows both. At these early stages the main problem is not knowing what works, the challenge is the first profitable sale and the skill is selling, so a paid sale counts towards the first target only once its delivery cost, the owner's hours at the M1 rate included, clears M1's margin bar; log the margin with each sale. Lead levels sit alongside: (1) one offer, one avatar, one platform, getting the first engaged leads; (2) knowing the exact inputs that produce an engaged lead and doing them consistently; (3) employees helping the owner advertise more; (4) the product earns consistent referrals, aiming for at least 25% of clients from referrals, before scaling further: update the product from client feedback about every two weeks, run a strong referral programme (L65, L66) and never let the product slip; (5) advertising in more places, more ways, with more people (new audiences, then placements, then platforms or methods); (6) experienced executives running channels without the owner. Product ladder: one product, one avatar, one channel through the early revenue milestones, then the same done consistently; then add a second product (an upsell); then a second channel; keep one avatar until well past that. From S1, report the current full price of the core offer alongside the stage: a rising price at full capacity is the clearest sign the business is improving.
- F11 [setting goals] Set goals as inputs (messages sent, calls held); revenue is the result. Set daily minimums high: people shrink to whatever bar is set. Never sell more than can be delivered well. Every goal gets a timeline: before deciding the business cannot get somewhere, ask whether it can on a longer time frame. Plan in detail only the next 12 weeks; beyond that, hold only the stage exits in the instructions box, make no long revenue projections, and re-plan at each quarterly L1 re-score. Once a year, list which current choices make the business stronger in 3 and 7 years (reputation with the avatar, proof, the owned list, templates) and which spend it. Only external deadlines and dates agreed with others are real deadlines: do not judge the owner, pilots or suppliers against a private timeline nobody agreed to; if a date matters, state it and agree it. Never set targets at industry averages; use averages only to diagnose weak spots. When estimating time, money or reps for the owner's own milestones, add at least 50% before committing them in the instructions box, and judge progress against the buffered figure. Set one stretch outcome goal from the M3 maths, where a near miss still counts as progress, set only after studying one verified person a step or two ahead who hit it, never from an unverified launch claim.
- F12 [choosing pilots, pilots are done, every 10 new clients after, or qualifying a prospect] Find the ideal client from five columns: liked working with them, paid most, most profitable, easiest to deliver, and clients they referred or introduced (valued at those clients' lifetime gross profit, M23, L65). Take the top 20% and find the two or three traits they share: what they think, where they are, what they look like (size, customer mix, tools) and what they did to get results. Sell only to buyers with those traits from then on and say no to the rest. Choosing who to serve is the first strategic decision, and the cheapest time to fix the avatar is before revenue depends on it: before any paying client, pick pilots that match the intended paying avatar, not whoever says yes first. Red flags (review with the ideal-client analysis and for each prospect at C2): short tenure; support beyond scope (log support hours per client, M13); haggling for a discount before the sale, or asking after signing to change agreed terms, pause, skip or reduce payments, or stretch payment terms; unhappy despite delivery that met the written promise (still fix it fast, M27); a fee that is a large share of their free cash (C14); wanting a saviour rather than doing the work; the owner promising more than the written scope to close them; contact with the client's named lead getting rarer or harder (raise it within a week); no concrete result they can name after the clarify questions; blame on every past supplier and staff member that persists after the C14 layers, with no part their own. Two or more: do not sell, or offer the DIY version. Test two traits as hypotheses, never as judgements of the person: whether the named lead self-diagnoses the last period with specifics when asked, and how fast they return the pre-call form; log them as marked inferences (C4) and check after pilots whether they predict task completion (D8). When the owner likes a prospect unusually, run the red-flag checklist on paper before offering. After the pilot review, ask what the price would be if selling only to buyers like the top pilot, and test it on the next calls (M5). When the ideal client changes, update in one pass: the positioning line in the instructions box (O3), L2, the L8 fit score, cold email openers, proof shown, the onboarding pack and the C2 questions. After the first target, if a weaker segment emerges, phase it out with a cap (no more than 1 in 3 new clients from it), then drop it. Note which channel, message and offer each top client came in through (L14), and cut what did not bring them. At each review, work out the share of gross profit from the top 20% and top 4% of clients and put more time into buyers like the top few.
- F13 [deciding where the owner's hours go] Measure money per hour of the owner's time; drop what earns least per hour. Strategy is putting limited time and money on the option with the most leverage. Early on, time is the only real input, so do whatever it lawfully takes to reach the first revenue milestones: that cash unlocks more leverage. Notice which roles the owner enjoys and is best at, steer hours there, and hand the rest off with document, demonstrate, duplicate (D5). Keep the owner's hours on the highest-value skill (early on, outreach and sales calls) and hand the rest off as soon as it is affordable. Log weekly hours across attraction (research, outreach), conversion (calls), delivery (sessions) and account management (renewals, referral and testimonial asks, M8, M9, L66): attraction is the first to template or hand off (T2, O34), conversion stays the owner's, and delivery moves into templates (D6). Each quarter, list what the owner already does and ask what else each could produce at no extra cost, and prefer work that does two jobs at once (a lead, proof and a delivery asset). Never use an employer's time, data or IP, and keep client personal data under X5. Risk-adjust distant payoffs against cash now. Before spending more hours on a problem, ask how to solve it in half the time.
- F14 [fear is blocking a decision] Pay the price of being visible; ignore the opinions of anyone who would not speak at the owner's funeral. Play it out to the specific worst case, then list the ways the business could die and avoid them. Worst-case floor: the owner delivering alone at full price with near-zero fixed costs; keep fixed monthly costs low enough that this floor always makes a profit. Between lawful, ethical options, pick the one that makes the better story at 85: the harder option is usually right. This never overrides F1 hours, the [Fixed] rules or M27. When fear stalls a decision, write down the one or two people whose reaction is pictured and ask whether they are in the avatar or ahead of the owner; if not, their view does not count (M2, F15). Each thing wanted has a price, so name it (discretion is the price of trust: never mention one client's details to another, X5, M12); if the owner will not pay it, drop the goal rather than complain about it.
- F15 [starting something the owner has not done before] Do not posture: say the owner is starting out. Take current tips from people a step or two ahead and strategy from whoever is a hundred steps ahead (F28). Join communities of people using the same method. Prefer groups where everyone is ahead, aim to become the most useful person there, and take the advice, especially in a paid room. Give to members first, free, until they ask what they can do in return; only then ask. Spend networking time with people building something too. Before a big decision, check whose opinion is being weighed and whether they have done it. Ask people ahead plainly, showing the work first (the numbers, what was tried, one specific question), never a general "pick your brain". When asking someone busy for time, say truly what of their work is admired, send 3 to 5 written questions in advance and say how long it will take (F25). When declining, thank them, say no, and name a substitute (a person, a resource or a later date). At events, ask each person directly whether a follow-up is welcome (L66). In communities, ask about others' wins and mention the owner's only when asked; selling contexts still show proof (L32). Go to in-person rooms where the avatar or skilled sellers in the channel gather, within F1 hours, as a listener first to hear their words (O5). In paid rooms, offer the owner's core skill and do the full job to paid standard, never half a favour (never an employer's IP or data, F13; never regulated advice, X4). Keep a short list of who in each community is strong at what, ask them before paying a supplier (T2), and do not spend favours on small asks. Until the first target, networking calls come only after the day's outreach minimum (L41) and never inside the outreach block (L70); open-ended collaboration meetings wait until after F3. When someone does the owner a favour, repay it in kind soon and log it (L75). Ask community members and future partners "what project matters most to you right now?", then offer one concrete piece of help within F1 hours. Keep a written list of the owner's Ten: up to ten named people chosen on purpose, at least half of them ahead in the channel or in selling to the avatar, or able to put the owner in front of the avatar, plus buyers who replied warmly and one mentor. Give each one useful touch a month, logged, only after the day's outreach minimum (L41) and capped at one hour a week; one touch each per quarter is the floor in busy seasons. The one or two people who get the weekly input number (F23) come from the Ten and are further ahead than the owner. Copy what successful people did at the owner's current stage, not what they do now: their present habits are often effects of success, not its causes.
- F16 [considering building software] Pre-sell a paid waitlist first. No custom software before the first target: tools stay in no-code or spreadsheet tools the owner owns. If a developer is ever used, the owner owns the code, repository and accounts from day one (T2), and budgets twice the quote.
